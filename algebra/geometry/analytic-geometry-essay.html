ANALYTIC GEOMETRY

Analytic Geometry

how a set of points becomes a shape, and why

A shape as a question, asked at every point

Classical geometry hands you a circle already drawn: here is the compass, here is the pin, here is the curve it traces. Analytic geometry throws away the compass and keeps only the question the compass was answering. Pick any candidate point \(P\) in the plane and ask: is the distance from \(P\) to a fixed center exactly equal to a fixed radius? Every point in the plane can be asked this question, and the circle is nothing more than the collection of points that answer yes. Nobody drew it. It was assembled, one verified point at a time, out of the entire plane.

Definition — locus

A locus is the set of every point satisfying a stated condition:

A "shape," in analytic geometry, is just a locus with a name. The circle, the ellipse, the parabola, the line — each is the answer set to one particular yes/no test, nothing else.

Insight

A shape is not a picture. A picture is only what you get when you shade in every point that passed the test. The real object — the thing analytic geometry studies — is the infinite yes/no test itself. The drawing is a courtesy, added afterward for eyes that can't scan infinitely many points one at a time.

Euclid already had the idea — he just had no arithmetic

This is not a modern invention grafted onto old geometry. Euclid's own definition of a circle, stated in words with no coordinates in sight, is already a locus condition: the set of points in a plane that lie at a given distance from a given point. That sentence has exactly the shape of the set-builder notation above. What Euclid's geometry lacked wasn't the concept of a locus — it was a way to test "lies at a given distance" without physically holding a compass to the page. Analytic geometry's real contribution is narrower and more useful than it sounds: it supplies every geometric condition with an arithmetic test that a symbol-pusher, with no picture in front of them at all, can still carry out correctly.

Note — every condition needs a number

Turning "equidistant" from a comparison you can only check by eye into an equality you can check by arithmetic requires assigning coordinates to points and a formula to "distance." That handoff — geometry's vocabulary translated into algebra's — is exactly what Panel II builds, and it is the hinge the rest of this essay turns on.

The Coordinate Dictionary

Descartes' trick: give every point two numbers, and every distance a formula

Turning "point" into "pair of numbers"

Fix two perpendicular reference lines in the plane and a unit of length. Every point \(P\) now has a unique address: its signed distance from each line, written as an ordered pair \((x,y)\). This is the entire content of Descartes' and Fermat's innovation in the 1630s — deceptively small on paper, and enough to fuse two subjects, algebra and geometry, that had been studied separately for two thousand years. Once a point is a pair of numbers, any relationship between points becomes a relationship between numbers, and numbers can be added, squared, and solved for in a way that pictures cannot.

Distance, by way of Pythagoras

Two points \(P_1=(x_1,y_1)\) and \(P_2=(x_2,y_2)\) sit at the corners of a right triangle whose legs run parallel to the two reference lines, with lengths \(|x_2-x_1|\) and \(|y_2-y_1|\). The Pythagorean theorem hands back the hypotenuse — the actual straight-line distance — for free:

Insight — why squaring matters

Distance is defined through a square root, but every locus condition built from distances gets that root squared away during the algebra needed to test it — and what survives squaring is a polynomial in \(x\) and \(y\). This one mechanical fact is the reason every shape in Panel III turns out to be a polynomial equation: a locus built purely out of distances collapses into a polynomial expression because the Pythagorean theorem underneath it is itself just a sum of two squares.

One entry is worth naming now: the set of points equidistant from two fixed points \(A\) and \(B\) is itself a locus — a straight line, the perpendicular bisector of segment \(AB\). It returns as the cleanest example in the interactive lab, because it's the one case here where the shape is flat rather than curved.

The Conics as Loci

Circle — equidistant from one point

In words: every point whose distance to a fixed center \(C=(h,k)\) equals a fixed radius \(r\).

One square root, one squaring, done. Every other conic below is this same move, repeated on a condition with one more distance term in it.

Hyperbola — the ellipse's algebra, with one sign flipped

Keep the same two foci and the same two radicals, but change "sum" to "difference": \(\big|\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2} - \sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}\big| = 2a\). Running the identical isolate-and-square sequence used for the ellipse, with a sign change at the one place it matters, lands on:

Insight — one inequality decides oval vs. open

The ellipse's derivation needed \(a>c\) to make \(b^2=a^2-c^2\) positive — two foci close together relative to the total distance \(2a\), so the locus closes into an oval. The hyperbola's derivation needs the opposite, \(c>a\), to make \(b^2=c^2-a^2\) positive — foci far apart relative to the fixed difference, so the locus never closes and opens into two branches instead. Nothing else about the algebra changed. One inequality, flipped, is the entire reason one shape is bounded and the other isn't.

Every entry lands on degree 2, and for the same structural reason each time: a condition built from distances always resolves after at most two squarings, and squaring a first-degree expression under a root produces, at worst, a second-degree polynomial once the roots are gone. This is why "conic section" and "second-degree curve" turn out to name the same family — one definition comes from slicing a cone with a plane, the other from distance conditions in the plane, and Panel VI returns to how completely those two independent definitions agree.

Why One Equation Draws a Curve

counting degrees of freedom: what a constraint costs, and what an inequality never pays

Two free numbers, one constraint

A point in the plane carries two independent real numbers, \(x\) and \(y\) — two degrees of freedom, matching the plane's own two-dimensionality. A single equation \(F(x,y)=0\) is one scalar constraint tying those two numbers together. Near almost every point that satisfies it, the equation can be locally solved for one variable in terms of the other — \(y\) as a function of \(x\), or \(x\) as a function of \(y\) — which leaves exactly one variable still free to move. One free direction to travel in is precisely what "a curve" means. Stated as arithmetic: two dimensions, minus one equation, generically leaves a one-dimensional solution set.

Definition — codimension

The codimension of a locus is the number of independent equations imposed, subtracted from the ambient dimension. One equation in the plane: codimension \(1\), generic solution set \(2-1=1\)-dimensional — a curve. Two independent equations in the plane: codimension \(2\), generic solution set \(2-2=0\)-dimensional — isolated points. This is the arithmetic behind the familiar fact that two curves typically meet in a finite set of points, not along a whole stretch of curve.

Why "generically" is doing real work

The count above predicts the typical outcome, not a guaranteed one. Four honest exceptions are worth having in view, because every one of them is a real equation that fails to behave like a genuine constraint somewhere:

Warning — four ways the count can fail

Empty: \(x^2+y^2=-1\) has no real solution at all — the left side can never be negative, so the equation was never satisfiable to begin with, and "dimension" was never entitled to apply. A single point: \(x^2+y^2=0\) forces both squared terms to vanish together, collapsing what the count predicted as a curve down to the lone point \((0,0)\) — dimension \(0\), not \(1\). A union of lines: \(x^2-y^2=0\) factors as \((x-y)(x+y)=0\), two full lines crossing at the origin — one second-degree equation, but not one smooth curve. Everything: the trivial equation \(0=0\) constrains nothing, leaving the full two-dimensional plane.

What distinguishes a "genuine" constraint from a degenerate one at a given point is whether the equation's gradient is nonzero there — informally, whether \(F\) is actually changing in some direction near that point, rather than sitting at a flat spot. Where the gradient vanishes is exactly where the count above can misfire, which is why it predicts the generic case rather than promising it. The companion Jacobian essay in this collection works out that gradient condition — the implicit function theorem's real content — in full.

Level sets, and why inequalities don't shrink

\(F(x,y)=0\) is called the zero level set of \(F\); every other constant \(k\) gives another level curve \(F(x,y)=k\), and together they tile the plane the way contour lines tile a topographic map. An inequality \(F(x,y)\le 0\) is a different kind of statement entirely: it doesn't pin \(F\) to one exact value, so it never costs a dimension. It only tells each point which side of the boundary it falls on. The solution set stays two-dimensional — a filled region, not a curve — because an inequality is boundary and bulk, while an equation is boundary alone.

Insight — what the next panel makes literal

Take the exact same \(F\) used to define a circle, and widen the test from "\(F=0\) exactly" to "\(|F|<\varepsilon\), for some small tolerance." That widened test carves out a thin ring — a genuinely two-dimensional strip with a small but positive area. Shrink \(\varepsilon\) toward zero, and that strip thins with it, its area falling toward zero right along with \(\varepsilon\). What survives in the limit — the set with \(\varepsilon=0\) exactly — is the circle itself: a one-dimensional curve, with zero area, sitting at the center of every one of those shrinking strips. An equation is what a region becomes once its thickness is squeezed to nothing. The lab in Panel V lets you drag that tolerance down and watch it happen.

Reading the Algebra Back

from a bare second-degree equation to its shape, systems as intersections, and where this leads

Classifying a conic without ever mentioning a focus

Panel III started from a geometric locus condition and derived an equation. The trip is fully reversible: handed a bare general second-degree equation, with no foci or directrix mentioned anywhere,

its shape can be read off from a single number, the discriminant \(\Delta = B^2-4AC\), with no further work:

The discriminant test
Sign of B² − 4ACShape familyDegenerate members at the boundary
\(\Delta<0\)ellipse (a circle, if also \(A=C,\,B=0\))a single point, or no real solutions
\(\Delta=0\)parabolaone line, two parallel lines, or none

The degenerate column is not a footnote — it is Panel IV's exception list, arriving from a different direction. Take \(x^2-y^2=0\): here \(A=1,B=0,C=-1\), so \(\Delta = 0-4(1)(-1)=4>0\), and the test correctly flags it as belonging to the hyperbola family — even though this particular member of that family has degenerated into the two crossing lines \((x-y)(x+y)=0\) from Panel IV's warning. The discriminant tests the family the equation belongs to; whether one specific member of that family happens to sit at a degenerate boundary value is a second question, answered by the same gradient check Panel IV already described.

Where this leads

Three threads extend directly out of everything above, each picked up in full elsewhere in this collection:

A locus described by motion, not by equation

Every shape in this essay was pinned down implicitly, as the solution set of \(F(x,y)=0\). There is a second, equally valid way to specify the same set: parametrically, as the positions visited while a parameter \(t\) sweeps through its range — the unit circle as \((\cos t,\sin t)\) for \(t\in[0,2\pi)\), for instance, tracing exactly the locus \(x^2+y^2=1\) without ever writing that equation down. The companion Functions as Mappings essay treats this parametric view — a curve as the image of a mapping — as the primary object.

Systems of equations as intersecting loci

Panel IV's codimension count turns concrete the moment two loci are asked to hold simultaneously. The line \(y=x\) and the circle \(x^2+y^2=1\) are each one equation, each codimension \(1\); demanding both at once is codimension \(2\), and solving directly confirms it lands on isolated points, not a shared stretch of curve:

Exactly two points, exactly as \(2-2=0\)-dimensional predicted.

One more dimension: surfaces as level sets

Add a third coordinate and nothing about the counting changes: a single equation \(F(x,y,z)=0\) in three-dimensional space is still codimension \(1\), so its generic solution set is \(3-1=2\)-dimensional — a surface rather than a curve, a sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2\) being the direct three-variable cousin of Panel III's circle. The companion Geometry & Algebra essay picks this thread up from the coordinate-space side, and the Jacobian essay covers the gradient condition that decides, in any dimension, where such a level set is guaranteed to actually behave like a smooth surface rather than pinching down to a point or a line.

Insight — one line to carry away

Every shape in this essay came from the same two-step move: state a condition on a point, then let arithmetic decide which points satisfy it. The circle, the ellipse, the parabola, the hyperbola — none of them is more than a distance condition once the coordinates are supplied, and none of them is a curve for any deeper reason than that one equation, in a two-dimensional plane, generically has exactly one dimension of freedom left over. Change the condition, and the shape changes. Change the number of conditions, and the dimension changes right along with it.
