ALGEBRA FROM SCRATCH

What Algebra Actually Does

how a symbol for an unknown number turns arithmetic into a solving procedure

The one thing algebra is actually for

Arithmetic computes with numbers you already know: \(3+5\), \(12\div4\). Algebra begins the moment you don't know a number yet, and want to reason about it anyway. You give it a name — usually \(x\) — and you keep manipulating the sentence it sits inside using moves that are guaranteed never to change which number \(x\) actually is. That's the entire enterprise, stripped to its core: a name for an unknown, and a small set of moves proven to preserve truth. Everything else — quadratics, cubics, the formulas this essay builds — is that one idea, pushed further and further.

Definition

A variable is a symbol standing for a number not yet known. An equation is a claim that two expressions are equal for some (possibly unknown) value of that variable. A solution is a value of the variable that makes the claim true. "Solving" an equation means finding every such value — no more, no fewer.

Equality as balance

The single license underneath every algebraic manipulation is almost embarrassingly simple: if two things are equal, and you do the same thing to both of them, they're still equal. Add 5 to both sides, multiply both sides by 3, square both sides — none of these can turn a true equation false, because they don't touch the relationship between the two sides at all, only their shared shape. Picture an equation as a balance scale holding equal weight on each pan: any operation applied identically to both pans keeps it level.

BEFORE the same move on both pans keeps the scale level [ 3x + 5 ] ═══**═══ [ 17 ] │ subtract 5 from both pans │ ▼ ▼ [ 3x ] ═══**═══ [ 12 ] │ divide both pans by 3 │ ▼ ▼ [ x ] ═══**═══ [ 4 ]

That's not a special trick for this one equation — it's the entire method. "Solve for \(x\)" means: find a sequence of balance-preserving moves that strips away everything surrounding \(x\) until \(x\) stands alone on one side. Since \(3x+5\) was built by first multiplying by 3 and then adding 5, undoing it means running those two steps in reverse order: subtract 5 first, then divide by 3. Solving is quite literally running the construction backward.

Insight

Equality is a relation you're allowed to operate on both sides of at once. That license, applied over and over, in whatever order undoes how the equation was built, is the entire content of "solving an equation" — at least for the simplest kind, where the unknown appears exactly once. Keep this picture in mind; the moment it stops being enough is exactly where quadratics and cubics get interesting.

Note — where the word "algebra" comes from

The word itself is a fossil of this exact move. It comes from the Arabic al-jabr, meaning roughly "reunion of broken parts" or "restoration" — the title of a 9th-century treatise by the Persian scholar al-Khwārizmī, whose Latinized name also gives us "algorithm." Al-jabr specifically named the operation of moving a term across the equals sign to keep both sides balanced — precisely the "same move on both pans" idea above. The subject is named after its founding trick.

Why the unknown appearing twice breaks the method

Now try \(x^2 + x = 6\). The unknown \(x\) shows up in two different shapes at once — once multiplied by itself, once plain — and there is no longer a single nested chain of operations to run backward. You can't "subtract, then divide" your way to isolating \(x\), because \(x\) wasn't built by a chain of operations applied once to a single copy of itself; it was used twice, combined by addition, at two different powers. Any one balance-preserving move you make touches both copies simultaneously, but not in a way that cancels one of them out. This is a genuinely new kind of problem. Solving it requires manufacturing a wholly new move — not just reapplying "undo in reverse order" harder.

Scribes in Babylon were solving specific numeric versions of exactly this problem — using clay tablets, base-60 arithmetic, and geometric recipes rather than any symbolic notation — roughly four thousand years ago. The geometry came first, by a long way; the symbols came much later. Panel II reconstructs the essential trick they were already using, and dresses it in modern notation so it applies to every quadratic equation at once instead of one specific numeric case at a time.

Note — a preview

The move that rescues the quadratic case, in Panel II, is a kind of recentering: reshaping the equation so its structure becomes symmetric around a single point. That same idea — recenter to expose a hidden symmetry — reappears, upgraded, when this essay tackles the cubic in Panels III and IV. It is one of the few genuinely reusable ideas in elementary algebra, and this essay is largely the story of squeezing as much out of it as it will give.

The Quadratic Formula, Derived

from a square with a missing corner to a formula that solves every quadratic at once

Reducing to one clean case

Every quadratic equation can be written \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) with \(a\ne0\). The leading coefficient \(a\) is just a uniform scaling of every term, so dividing the whole equation by \(a\) — a legal balance-preserving move — costs nothing and simplifies everything:

So it's enough to solve the monic reduced form \(x^2+px+q=0\) once, in general, and then substitute \(p=b/a\), \(q=c/a\) back in at the end to recover the answer for any \(a,b,c\).

Completing the square: the geometric picture

Here is the move Babylonian and later Islamic-era mathematicians actually used, before any of this had symbolic notation. Picture \(x^2\) not as an abstract product but as the literal area of a square with side length \(x\). The term \(px\) is then the combined area of two thin rectangles, each \(x\) long and \(p/2\) wide, glued onto two adjacent sides of that square. Together, square plus two strips has area exactly \(x^2+px\) — but the resulting L-shaped figure is almost a bigger square. It's missing one small piece: a corner square of side \(p/2\), sitting in the notch where the two strips would otherwise meet.

THE SQUARE x² ADD TWO STRIPS OF WIDTH p/2 ┌───────────────┐ ┌───────────────┬───┐ │ │ │ │ (p/2)│ │ x² │ ---> │ x² │ ·x │ │ │ │ │ │ └───────────────┘ ├───────────────┼───┤ │ (p/2)·x │ ? │ ← corner missing └───────────────┴───┘ fill that corner with exactly (p/2)² and the L-shape becomes one honest, complete square of side (x + p/2): (x + p/2)² = x² + px + (p/2)²

That boxed identity is not an algebraic coincidence to be verified by expanding \((x+p/2)^2\) and checking the cross term matches \(px\) — though it certainly does — it is the geometric fact the picture already shows: a square of side \(x+p/2\) decomposes, by area, into exactly those four pieces. "Completing the square" is the algebraic shadow of physically filling in that missing corner.

Note — why the historical picture assumes p is positive

Al-Khwārizmī's own diagrams, like the one above, implicitly assume \(p>0\) — negative numbers weren't yet accepted as legitimate quantities to have an area of anything. The algebra derived below has no such restriction; the final formula handles negative \(p\), negative \(q\), and even complex results automatically, via the \(\pm\) that appears once a square root is taken. The picture is a launching point for the idea, not a limitation on where it ends up applying.

Insight

The quadratic formula is nothing more than "complete the square, then solve for \(x\)," carried out once symbolically instead of by hand for every new triple \((a,b,c)\). That is the entire reason it's worth memorizing: it is a pre-solved template of a single geometric move, not a separate idea layered on top of it.

The discriminant: what's under the root is doing real geometric work

The quantity \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\) is called the discriminant, and its sign alone answers a genuine geometric question: how many times does the parabola \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) cross the \(x\)-axis?

Definition — the discriminant

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\). \(\Delta>0\): two distinct real roots — the parabola crosses the axis twice. \(\Delta=0\): one repeated real root — the parabola is tangent to the axis exactly at its vertex. \(\Delta<0\): no real roots — the parabola never meets the axis, and the two roots are a complex-conjugate pair \(x=\dfrac{-b}{2a}\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{-\Delta}}{2a}\,i\).

This isn't a separate fact bolted onto the formula — it falls straight out of the completed-square picture. \(\left(\tfrac{p}{2}\right)^2-q\) is exactly \(\Delta/(4a^2)\) after the substitution above, so its sign is literally asking: does the completed-square equation \(\left(x+\tfrac{p}{2}\right)^2=\left(\tfrac{p}{2}\right)^2-q\) have a real number whose square equals the right-hand side? A negative right-hand side can't be a square of any real number — hence no real solution — while zero means the "missing corner" was exactly zero all along, i.e. \(x^2+px+q\) was already a perfect square before you did anything to it.

This fact looks like a minor curiosity attached to the quadratic case, but it is the exact mechanism Panel IV uses to crack the cubic: Cardano's substitution works by manufacturing two new unknowns whose sum and product are both known, then reading off Vieta's formulas in reverse to recover them.

Depressing the Cubic

the same recentering idea from completing the square, pushed one degree up

The general cubic, and why the old trick doesn't directly apply

A general cubic equation is \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0\) with \(a\ne0\). Exactly as with the quadratic, dividing through by \(a\) costs nothing and produces a monic reduced form:

The obvious next move would be to try repeating Panel II's trick verbatim: hunt for a single missing piece that turns the left side into a perfect cube. But cubing a binomial, \((x+k)^3=x^3+3kx^2+3k^2x+k^3\), produces two extra terms beyond \(x^3\) and \(k^3\) — not one — so there is no single "missing corner" that can absorb both the \(x^2\) term and the \(x\) term of a general cubic simultaneously. A genuinely different first move is needed.

The shift that kills the quadratic term

Instead of chasing a perfect cube immediately, aim for something more modest first: eliminate the \(x^2\) term entirely, and worry about the rest afterward. Substitute \(x=t-\dfrac{B}{3}\) — a shift of the unknown by a constant, chosen specifically so the \(t^2\) term cancels once everything is expanded. This is exactly the same maneuver, one degree up, that vertex form uses on a parabola: shifting the input variable to recenter the curve around a point where its lower-order asymmetry disappears.

Expanding \((t-B/3)^3+B(t-B/3)^2+C(t-B/3)+D\) term by term and collecting powers of \(t\):

The \(t^2\) terms are \(-Bt^2\) and \(+Bt^2\) — they cancel exactly, by design; that cancellation is the entire reason \(-B/3\) was chosen as the shift amount rather than any other constant. What remains, after collecting the \(t^1\) and constant terms, is the depressed cubic:

BEFORE THE SHIFT AFTER x = t − B/3 cubic curve, lopsided: same curve, recentered: inflection point sits inflection point now sits off to one side, an x² exactly at t = 0 — the term is present t² term has vanished x³ + Bx² + Cx + D = 0 ───▶ t³ + pt + q = 0
Insight

Compare this to Panel II. Completing the square shifted a parabola so it became symmetric about a vertical axis through its vertex — the resulting vertex form is a pure even function, \((x+p/2)^2\), plus a constant. Depressing a cubic shifts it so it becomes point-symmetric about its inflection point — the depressed cubic \(t^3+pt+q\) is a pure odd function, \(t^3+pt\), plus a constant \(q\). Same idea, "recenter to expose a hidden symmetry," one degree up, producing the odd-degree analogue of vertex form.

Note

This shift only removes the \(x^2\) term — it does not solve the equation. \(t^3+pt+q=0\) is still a genuine cubic, with the unknown \(t\) appearing at two different powers at once, exactly the obstruction from Panel I that made quadratics hard in the first place. A second, cleverer move is still needed. That move is Panel IV.

Cardano's Substitution

turning one unknown into two, so a cubic collapses into a quadratic in disguise

The trick: split t into two unknowns on purpose

Here is the move that cracks the depressed cubic \(t^3+pt+q=0\), due to Scipione del Ferro and Niccolò Tartaglia in the early 1500s and published, with credit, by Gerolamo Cardano in 1545. Rather than solving for \(t\) directly, deliberately write \(t=u+v\), introducing a second degree of freedom on purpose. This looks like it makes the problem strictly harder — one equation, now two unknowns — but that extra freedom is exactly what gets exploited: since \(u\) and \(v\) individually aren't pinned down by \(t=u+v\) alone, there is room to impose a second, convenient condition on them later.

Now compare this against the target equation \(t^3+pt+q=0\) term by term. They match exactly if the two extra conditions hold:

A quadratic in disguise

Cube the first condition: \((uv)^3=(-p/3)^3\), i.e. \(u^3v^3=-p^3/27\). Combined with the second condition, this hands over exactly two facts about the pair \((u^3,v^3)\): their sum is \(-q\), and their product is \(-p^3/27\). But two numbers with a known sum and a known product are precisely the two roots of a quadratic — this is Vieta's formulas from Panel II, run in reverse. Given sum \(S\) and product \(P\), the numbers are the roots of \(z^2-Sz+P=0\), so \(u^3\) and \(v^3\) satisfy:

This is the payoff of the whole essay's arc so far. A genuinely cubic problem has just been reduced, via one substitution and one symmetric-function trick, to solving a quadratic — and Panel II already solved every quadratic that will ever exist, once and for all. Nothing new needs to be invented here; the machinery already built gets reapplied directly.

Call the quantity under the root \(\Delta'\) — Cardano's radicand. Taking the "+" root as \(u^3\) and the "−" root as \(v^3\), then taking real cube roots of each, gives \(t=u+v\):

That boxed expression is Cardano's formula. Combined with Panel III's shift \(x=t-B/3\), it solves every cubic equation that exists, in closed form, built from nothing but the balance-scale logic of Panel I, the completing-the-square trick of Panel II, and one deliberately doubled unknown.

Casus irreducibilis: when the shortcut detours through complex numbers to reach a real answer

Here is the sting in the formula's tail. \(\Delta'\) can be negative even when the cubic has three perfectly good, distinct real roots — in fact \(\Delta'<0\) is exactly the condition for three distinct real roots (it relates to the cubic's own discriminant by \(\Delta'=-\Delta/108\), where \(\Delta=-4p^3-27q^2\)). When that happens, \(\sqrt{\Delta'}\) is imaginary, and Cardano's formula asks you to add together two complex-conjugate cube roots. Their imaginary parts are guaranteed, in the end, to cancel — the sum really is real, and really is a root — but there is no way to extract that real number using only real radicals along the way. The formula is forced through \(\mathbb{C}\) even though it lands back on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Warning — casus irreducibilis, worked

Take \(t^3-15t-4=0\), the example Rafael Bombelli confronted in the 1560s: here \(p=-15,\ q=-4\), so \(\Delta'=\tfrac{16}{4}+\tfrac{(-15)^3}{27}=4-125=-121\). Cardano's formula demands \(\sqrt{-121}=11i\), giving \(u^3=2+11i\) and \(v^3=2-11i\). Bombelli — treating these "impossible" numbers as legitimate objects to compute with anyway, purely to see what would happen — found \((2+i)^3=2+11i\) and \((2-i)^3=2-11i\) directly by expansion. So \(u=2+i\), \(v=2-i\), and \(t=u+v=4\), a perfectly ordinary real number. Check: \(4^3-15(4)-4=64-60-4=0\). ✓

Note — the real birth of complex numbers

This, more than "solve \(x^2=-1\)," is arguably where complex numbers first became something mathematicians took seriously rather than dismissed. A negative discriminant on a quadratic had been shrugged off as "no solution" for centuries — nothing forced anyone to care. Casus irreducibilis couldn't be shrugged off the same way, because the final answer, as Bombelli's example shows, was an undeniable, checkable real number that the formula could only reach by passing through square roots of negatives along the way.

Definition — the trigonometric alternative

For practical real-number computation in the three-real-roots case (\(\Delta'<0\), which forces \(p<0\)), the standard workaround sidesteps complex cube roots entirely using the triple-angle identity \(\cos3\varphi=4\cos^3\varphi-3\cos\varphi\). Substituting \(t=2\sqrt{-p/3}\,\cos\varphi\) into \(t^3+pt+q=0\) and simplifying (the \(\cos\varphi\) terms cancel by construction) leaves a single clean equation for the angle, \(\cos3\varphi=\dfrac{-q/2}{(-p/3)^{3/2}}\), and its three solutions give all three real roots directly:

Where This Leads

quartics, the wall at degree five, and the symmetry idea underneath everything

One more step: Ferrari's quartic

The pattern extends naturally to a fourth-degree equation \(ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e=0\). Dividing by \(a\) and shifting \(x=t-B/4\) (exactly Panel III's move, one degree up) kills the cubic term, leaving a depressed quartic \(t^4+pt^2+qt+r=0\). Lodovico Ferrari — a student of Cardano's — then found a substitution that manufactures an auxiliary cubic equation, the resolvent cubic, whose solution (via Panel IV, which by now already knows how to solve any cubic) supplies exactly the extra piece of information needed to factor the quartic into two solvable quadratics. The shape of the strategy never changes: reduce the degree of asymmetry by a shift, then solve an auxiliary equation one degree lower that a previous panel has already conquered.

The wall at degree five

The obvious next move is to keep going — find a substitution for the quintic, \(ax^5+\dots=0\), that reduces it to a solvable auxiliary equation the way the cubic reduced to a quadratic and the quartic reduced to a cubic. This does not work, and — this is the remarkable part — it was proven not to work, by Niels Henrik Abel and Paolo Ruffini in the early 1800s. Not "nobody has found the right substitution yet": there provably is no formula for the roots of a general quintic built from the coefficients using only \(+,-,\times,\div\) and taking \(n\)-th roots, no matter how many auxiliary unknowns or clever substitutions are tried.

Galois's answer to why

A conceptual explanation, without the machinery: Évariste Galois showed that whether an equation is solvable by radicals depends entirely on a symmetry question — specifically, which ways the roots can be permuted among each other while preserving every polynomial relationship among them that has rational-number coefficients. This collection of allowed permutations is the equation's Galois group. For degree \(\le4\), that group is always solvable in a precise technical sense — buildable, in stages, from simple abelian pieces — and it is exactly that structural fact which licenses the whole chain of reductions in Panels II through IV and their quartic cousin above. Starting at degree 5, the generic Galois group is the full symmetric group \(S_5\), which is not solvable — and that failure of a purely group-theoretic condition, not a lack of ingenuity, is the actual reason the chain of reductions stops.

Note — Vieta's formulas, in general

Panel II's Vieta relations for the quadratic, and Panel IV's use of them for \(u^3,v^3\), are the \(n=2\) case of a completely general fact: for any degree-\(n\) polynomial, each coefficient is (up to sign) the corresponding elementary symmetric polynomial in the roots — a sum of all products of the roots taken \(k\) at a time. This is the algebraic backbone Galois theory is built directly on top of: a polynomial's coefficients only ever "see" the roots through relationships that don't care which root is labeled which, which is precisely why permuting the roots is the right lens for asking what a formula built from the coefficients could possibly compute.

Insight

One line to carry away: the quadratic formula, the depressed cubic, and Cardano's substitution are not three unrelated tricks — they are the same move, "exploit the symmetric relationship between coefficients and roots to manufacture a solvable auxiliary equation," applied at successively higher degree, until at degree five the symmetry itself stops cooperating. Everything in this essay, from the balance scale in Panel I to the Galois group in this closing note, is one continuous idea, followed as far as it will go.
