ALGEBRA ↔ CALCULUS

Linear Algebra Meets Calculus

two subjects that grew up apart, and the one object — a matrix attached to a point — both were quietly reaching for

Why a slope can't survive more than one variable

Taught back to back, linear algebra and calculus can feel like two different kinds of mathematics in two different kinds of clothes. Linear algebra solves systems of equations, studies abstract vector spaces, multiplies grids of numbers. Calculus measures rates of change, draws tangent lines, computes areas under curves. Nothing about "eigenvector" seems to have much to do with "derivative," and it is entirely possible to pass both courses without ever being told that, past a certain point, they are the same subject.

They are the same subject because of one narrow, load-bearing fact: the derivative of a function of several variables cannot be a number. In one variable, \(f'(a)\) answers "how fast does \(f\) change as \(x\) moves?" with a single value, because there is only one direction to move in — forward or backward along the same line. The instant a function takes more than one input, that question stops being well-posed as asked. Moving away from a point \(a\) in direction \(\mathbf u\) can produce an entirely different rate of change than moving in direction \(\mathbf v\), and there is no single number that reports "the" rate of change in every direction at once.

The forced promotion

Calculus's response to this is not a convenient trick — it is close to the only option left. If a single number cannot describe how \(F\) responds to a nudge in every direction simultaneously, the object that replaces it has to itself accept a direction as input and hand back a rate as output: it has to be a function of the nudge \(\mathbf h\). Demanding that this replacement genuinely deserves to be called the derivative, rather than merely a reasonable local formula, pins down exactly what kind of function it is allowed to be — Panel III makes this precise, including why the pinning-down is forced rather than assumed. The answer, once it is pinned down, already has a name and a fully worked-out theory behind it in linear algebra: a linear map, and — the moment a basis is fixed to write it down in — a matrix.

Insight

A slope is a \(1\times1\) matrix that never had to learn it was one. For \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), \(f'(a)\) literally is the Jacobian of \(f\) at \(a\) — a \(1\times1\) matrix, whose lone entry is a partial derivative and whose lone column is the image of the number \(1\). Ordinary single-variable calculus is the special case of everything in this essay where the dimensions are too small for the difference between "a matrix" and "a number" to be visible at all.

What this essay is the seam between

This essay sits between two much longer ones already living elsewhere in this collection, and does not try to replace either. Functions as Mappings works out, from first principles, what a matrix actually is: not a grid of numbers, but a parts list — the images of a handful of basis vectors, stacked side by side, with matrix-vector multiplication defined to do nothing but recombine those parts. The Jacobian works out what a multivariable derivative actually is: the one linear map that best stands in for a nonlinear function in an infinitesimal neighborhood of a single point. Each essay proves its own half of the story completely on its own terms.

What this essay adds is the argument that those two halves are not merely compatible — they are, quite literally, one object described from two directions. Once that is seen clearly, several things that look like separate facts (why the chain rule multiplies, why a matrix means nothing without a chosen basis, why "linear" gets forced onto the derivative rather than chosen for convenience) turn out to be the same fact, said twice in two vocabularies.

The Matrix, Recalled

not a grid of numbers — a parts list, and one that means nothing without a chosen basis

What a matrix actually is, compressed to its essentials

Skip the row-times-column mechanics learned first in school — that is a computational shortcut, not the definition, and the companion essay linked in Panel I works through exactly why in detail. Start instead from a linear map \(T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\), meaning \(T(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=T(\mathbf u)+T(\mathbf v)\) and \(T(c\mathbf u)=cT(\mathbf u)\) for every pair of vectors and every scalar \(c\). Definition — the matrix, restated

Stack those \(n\) images as the columns of an \(m\times n\) array \(A\), and define matrix-vector multiplication to reproduce exactly the equation above:

A matrix is not a table of numbers that happens to combine correctly with vectors. It is the parts list itself, and \(A\mathbf x\) is nothing but your input's own coordinates telling that parts list how much of each part to use.

The clause that is easy to skip past: a matrix needs a basis to mean anything

"Column \(j\) is \(T(\mathbf e_j)\)" is not a basis-free sentence. It presupposes a basis for the domain (which vectors get to be called \(\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_n\)) and a basis for the codomain (the coordinates used to write each \(T(\mathbf e_j)\) down as a column of numbers). Change either basis and the array of numbers changes — even though the underlying map \(T\) has not moved a single point differently than before. If \(P\) is the matrix whose columns are a new basis \(\{\mathbf b_i\}\) written in the old coordinates, the two representations of the same map are related by \(A' = P^{-1}AP\).

Warning — the matrix is not the map

"The matrix of \(T\)" is always shorthand for "the matrix of \(T\) relative to a chosen pair of bases." The map \(T\) is the invariant object; the grid of numbers is a coordinate photograph of it, and a different choice of basis produces a legitimately different photograph of the exact same subject. This distinction is not pedantry saved for later — Panel III needs it immediately, because the Jacobian is, without exception, a matrix in precisely this basis-dependent sense, and Panel VI returns to it once the domain itself is allowed to curve.

This is exactly why the definition builds "\(L\) is linear" into its hypothesis, rather than deriving linearity afterward as a bonus. Requiring linearity is the single clause standing between "some function that happens to approximate \(F\) well near \(a\)" — of which there are infinitely many, none more canonical than the rest — and "the derivative," a single well-defined object. Once linearity is required, uniqueness follows immediately: a linear map's behavior along a ray \(\mathbf h=t\mathbf u\) scales exactly with \(t\), leaving no room for two different linear maps to be tied for closest fit. The full argument is Panel II of the companion Jacobian essay; this essay's job was only the step that essay takes for granted — showing the requirement earns its place rather than being handed down by convention.

Read literally, the Jacobian is Panel II's parts list, one derivative deep

Once \(L\) is required linear, Panel II's entire machinery becomes available with no modification whatsoever: \(L\) has a matrix, and relative to the standard bases, its column \(j\) is \(L(\mathbf e_j)\) — exactly the definition that made a matrix a parts list in the first place. Insight — what's exact, and what's approximate

Keep the two halves of this sentence separate, because they are easy to blur. The Jacobian, once assembled at a point \(a\), is an honest linear map — every fact of linear algebra (rank, determinant, eigenvalues, singular value decomposition) applies to it exactly, with no asterisk, the instant \(a\) is fixed. What is approximate is only the relationship between that linear map and the true, possibly curved, \(F\) at points away from \(a\) — the \(o(\|\mathbf h\|)\) gap the companion Jacobian essay spends an entire panel making rigorous, and Panel VII of this essay makes visible. Linear algebra is not "approximately" true of the derivative; calculus is what is approximately true of \(F\), using a fact from linear algebra that itself holds without approximation.

Why Composition Multiplies

the chain rule is not a separate law — it is one algebra fact and one calculus fact, shaking hands

Two facts, not one

"The chain rule multiplies Jacobians" is usually presented and memorized as a single rule. It is really the fusion of two independent facts — one that belongs entirely to linear algebra and needs no limit to prove, and one that belongs entirely to calculus and needs the \(o(\|\mathbf h\|)\) machinery of Panel III. Separating them is the fastest route to understanding why the rule is true, rather than only remembering that it is.

Fact A — algebra: composing linear maps multiplies their matrices

If \(S:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\) has matrix \(B\) and \(T:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^p\) has matrix \(A\), both built by Panel II's rule, then \(T\circ S\) is linear too, and its matrix is the ordinary product \(AB\). Column \(i\) of \(T\circ S\)'s matrix is \((T\circ S)(\mathbf e_i)=T\big(S(\mathbf e_i)\big)=T(\text{col}_i B) = A\cdot\text{col}_i(B)\) — apply \(A\) to column \(i\) of \(B\), for every \(i\) — and stacking those columns side by side is exactly the row-times-column rule for \(AB\). No limits anywhere in this proof; it is a corollary of Panel II alone.

The calculus half: derivatives of composites compose

Fact A says nothing yet about derivatives. The genuinely calculus-flavored claim is that if \(H\) is differentiable at \(a\) with derivative (linear map) \(DH(a)\), and \(F\) is differentiable at \(b:=H(a)\) with derivative \(DF(b)\), then \(F\circ H\) is differentiable at \(a\) with derivative the literal composition \(DF(b)\circ DH(a)\) — not yet a statement about matrices at all, just about linear maps.

Claim & proof

Claim. \(D(F\circ H)(a) = DF(b)\circ DH(a)\), where \(b=H(a)\).

Notice how little of this was calculus. The only place a limit was ever taken was in proving that a composite's derivative is the composite of the derivatives — a fact stated purely in terms of linear maps, with no coordinates or matrices in sight. The Sense of a Mapping

one word, three traditions, and the operation calculus performs on all of them: localization

Three traditions reaching for the same word

"Mapping" gets used loosely across mathematics, and it is worth pulling apart the three separate traditions that converge on it, because the convergence is not an accident of vocabulary — it is the actual content of this essay, restated historically instead of algebraically.

SET THEORY LINEAR ALGEBRA CALCULUS f: A → B assigns a map respecting + F is approximated, each element of A and scalar mult., at each point a, exactly one element classified completely by ONE instance of of B — no structure by a finite parts the linear-algebra required at all list (Panel II) object — glued smoothly point "relocate an "relocate a WHOLE to point element, arbitrarily" space, UNIFORMLY" "relocate a point, INFINITESIMALLY, differently everywhere"

The first tradition is set-theoretic: a function \(f:A\to B\) assigns to every element of \(A\) exactly one element of \(B\), with nothing in that definition requiring \(A\) or \(B\) to be numbers, or requiring a formula to exist at all — the companion Functions-as-Mappings essay opens with exactly this picture. This is "mapping" at its most permissive: relocate an element, arbitrarily, one at a time.

The second is geometric, and crystallized in Felix Klein's 1872 Erlangen program: define a geometry not by a list of axioms about points and lines, but by naming a space together with a group of transformations — rotations and translations for Euclidean geometry, a far larger group for projective geometry — and declaring "geometric" to mean "invariant under that group." Here "mapping" firmly means relocating every point of a space at once, uniformly, by one fixed rule, and the entire subject becomes the question of what a transformation preserves.

Linear algebra is the geometric tradition's most rigid, best-understood special case: only maps compatible with vector addition and scaling are admitted, and Panel II's theorem is exactly the statement that this restriction is rigid enough to collapse "the whole map" down to a finite list of vectors.

Note — Klein's Erlangen program

Klein's insight was that Euclidean geometry, projective geometry, and the (at the time, newly discovered) non-Euclidean geometries were not competing descriptions of "true" space, but different choices of transformation group applied to the same underlying question: which properties of a figure survive which class of relocations. It is the direct historical ancestor of treating a "map" as a first-class object worth classifying, rather than a mere computational tool — the same shift in attitude Panel I asked of the reader with a single-variable function.

Where calculus fits: not a fourth tradition, but an operation on the other three

Calculus needed a notion of "how does output respond to input" for maps that are neither globally linear (tradition two's tame special case) nor merely an arbitrary set function with no structure to exploit (tradition one). Panel III's forced promotion is calculus reaching, at every single point, for exactly the object tradition two had already completely solved: attach one linear map — one instance of the Erlangen-style, fully classified object — to each point of the domain, and let it vary smoothly from point to point.

Insight — what emerges

Lined up this way, calculus does not invent a new species of mapping. It performs an operation on the general notion of mapping: localization. A wildly curving relocation of space — tradition two's most general case, no longer required to be uniform — gets chopped into an infinite continuum of the one totally tame kind of relocation tradition three had already classified completely, glued together by nothing more than the requirement that neighboring linear maps vary continuously (the \(C^1\) criterion of the companion Jacobian essay's Panel II). The object a differentiable \(F\) actually possesses — one matrix per point — is a single mathematical object none of the three older traditions had a name for on its own: not a map, not a matrix, but a matrix-valued function on a space, which is precisely what differential geometry later formalized as a tensor field, and precisely what Panel VI of the companion Jacobian essay computes explicitly under the name "metric tensor."

Three questions about the same three-letter word, in increasing order of how much structure they demand: set theory asks only is it a function at all. Geometry and linear algebra ask what can this transformation preserve or discard, uniformly, everywhere at once. Calculus asks which single, everywhere-preserving transformation is this wilder map secretly imitating, right here, right now — and then asks the same question again, infinitesimally close by, where the answer is generally a little different.

Tangent Spaces

a fresh copy of a vector space, attached to every single point

A question worth asking plainly

Panel III assembled, at each point \(a\) of a domain, a linear map \(DF(a):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\). It is worth asking plainly what the "\(\mathbb R^n\)" being mapped from, specifically at the point \(a\), really is. For a flat domain sitting inside \(\mathbb R^n\) itself, this looks like a silly question — surely it is just \(\mathbb R^n\) again? The moment \(F\) is a curve's parametrization, or a surface, or — further out — a domain that is itself genuinely curved, the honest answer is subtler: it is a fresh copy of a vector space, attached specifically to the point \(a\), called the tangent space at \(a\), written \(T_aM\).

Velocity as the cleanest example

For a curve \(\gamma:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^3\), differentiable at \(t=a\), Panel III's promotion gives a linear map \(D\gamma(a):\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^3\). Fed the single basis vector of \(\mathbb R\) — the number \(1\) — it hands back one vector, \(\gamma'(a)\): the curve's velocity at that instant. That output vector does not live loosely "in \(\mathbb R^3\) in general." Conceptually it lives in the tangent space to \(\mathbb R^3\) at the specific point \(\gamma(a)\) — a distinct copy of \(\mathbb R^3\) attached to that one location, even though, for a flat ambient space, that copy happens to be identifiable with \(\mathbb R^3\) itself, which is exactly why the distinction is invisible in a first course. The distinction becomes unavoidable the moment the ambient space curves: a sphere's tangent plane at the north pole and its tangent plane at the equator are both genuine two-dimensional vector spaces, but they are not the same plane, and a vector "in" one does not automatically make sense "in" the other without an explicit rule for comparing them.

Why this matters for the essay's thesis

Every fact built so far rests on one identification: a linear map has a matrix once bases are chosen (Panel II), and the derivative is a linear map (Panel III). Tangent spaces are what make that identification precise even when the domain and codomain are curved rather than flat: \(DF(a)\) is honestly a linear map \(T_aM\to T_{F(a)}N\) between two specific, generally different, vector spaces — one attached to \(a\), the other attached to \(F(a)\) — and it only looks like "just a matrix acting on \(\mathbb R^n\)" because, for the flat domains most of calculus is first taught on, every tangent space happens to be identifiable with the same \(\mathbb R^n\), collapsing a real distinction down to an invisible one until it is genuinely needed.

Insight — a row and a column are not the same kind of object

Push this one step further and an asymmetry Panel II's notation quietly papers over becomes visible. Column \(j\) of a Jacobian is a tangent vector — it lives in the output tangent space \(T_{F(a)}N\), the image of an input basis direction. Row \(i\), for a scalar output, is the gradient \(\nabla f(a)^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f/\partial x_n\end{bmatrix}\) — an object that eats a tangent vector at \(a\) and returns a number, which is to say it lives not in \(T_aM\) itself but in its dual space. Writing both as rows and columns of the same array, and multiplying them with the same row-times-column rule, is a genuine convenience — but it papers over the fact that a vector and a covector are different kinds of object, a distinction that only stops being safely ignorable once lengths and angles enter the picture. Making that safe again is exactly what the metric tensor, built out of \(J^{\mathsf T}J\) in the companion Jacobian essay's closing panel, is for.

One computation, two names

A last thread worth pulling: switching from one basis of a vector space to another (Panel II's \(A'=P^{-1}AP\), pure linear algebra) and switching from one coordinate chart to another on a curved space (pure differential geometry) are, formula for formula, the same computation — both transform a matrix by conjugating or multiplying through by the Jacobian of the change-of-coordinates map. Linear algebra's "change of basis" and calculus's "change of coordinates" were never two ideas that happened to resemble each other; localization (Panel V) turns the first into the second automatically, the moment the basis itself is allowed to be a different one at every point.

Where It All Meets

one sentence, said in two vocabularies, and where to read each half in full

The single sentence

Linear algebra answers "what is the complete, exact behavior of a map that respects vector addition and scaling" with one clean object, the matrix — a finite parts list built from a basis (Panel II). Calculus answers "what is the best available stand-in for a map that respects nothing in particular" by admitting that no single global answer exists, and instead attaching one instance of linear algebra's answer to every point (Panel III), gluing the results together with continuity, and calling the whole assignment differentiation. Composition of either kind of map behaves identically — multiply the matrices (Panel IV) — because a calculus-flavored fact about the derivative of a composite hands off, after one clean substitution, to an algebra-flavored fact about composite linear maps that never needed a limit to prove. And the entire vocabulary both subjects reach for, "mapping," turns out, traced back through set theory, nineteenth-century transformation geometry, and analysis (Panel V), to be one idea worn three ways: relocate; relocate uniformly; relocate infinitesimally, differently at every point (Panel VI).

Insight — one line to carry away

A matrix is not "used by" calculus the way a hammer is used by a carpenter. The Jacobian is not like a matrix from linear algebra, by loose analogy — it is one, full stop, at every single point, with every theorem of linear algebra applying to it exactly and without modification. What calculus contributes is not a new kind of object, but the operation of attaching that one already-understood object to every point of a possibly-curved domain, and asking how it changes as the point does.
