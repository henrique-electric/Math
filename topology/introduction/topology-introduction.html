TOPOLOGY

Shapes Without a Ruler

why topology exists — the properties of a space that survive stretching, bending, and twisting, but never cutting or gluing

The geometry that refuses to measure

Ordinary geometry — the kind built on a ruler and a protractor — is obsessed with exact quantities: this side is 5 cm, that angle is 37°, these two triangles are congruent because every corresponding length and angle matches exactly. Move a shape, rotate it, reflect it, and geometry only calls it "the same" if every measurement survives untouched. That is an enormously useful notion of sameness, but it is also a brittle one — stretch a circle even slightly into an ellipse and, measurement by measurement, you have produced an entirely different object.

Topology asks a much looser question, and in exchange it gets to answer that question in situations where a ruler is useless or doesn't even exist. Instead of "do the measurements match," topology asks: can one shape be continuously deformed into the other — stretched, bent, twisted, compressed — without ever cutting it open and without ever gluing separate points together? Under that rule a circle and an ellipse count as identical; so do a circle and the boundary of a square. Two shapes related this way are called homeomorphic (made precise in Panel IV), and topology is, at bottom, the study of which properties survive that kind of deformation and which do not.

Insight — the coffee cup and the donut

The oldest joke in the subject is also its best one-line summary: a topologist can't tell a coffee cup from a donut. Both are a single solid blob of dough with exactly one hole running through it — the cup's hole is where the handle attaches. Squash the cup's body flat, thicken the handle, and round everything off, and you can watch it deform continuously into a donut (a torus) without a single cut and without a single new seam. The number of holes is a property that survives stretching; the number of ounces of coffee it holds is not — and topology only ever cares about properties of the first kind.

A problem the old geometry couldn't even state — Königsberg

The historical starting point predates the word "topology" by more than a century. In 1736 the city of Königsberg straddled the banks and two islands of the river Pregel, connected by seven bridges. A popular puzzle asked whether a walk existed that crossed every bridge exactly once. Leonhard Euler solved it — by proving no such walk exists — and in doing so did something genuinely new: he discarded every geometric detail of the map. The exact shape of the islands, the length of each bridge, the distance between them — none of it mattered to his argument. All that mattered was which landmasses connected to which, and by how many bridges.

Definition — Euler's insight, in modern language

Represent each landmass as a point (a vertex) and each bridge as a connecting line (an edge). A walk crossing every edge exactly once exists if and only if the number of vertices with an odd number of edges is 0 or 2. Königsberg's four landmasses all had an odd number of bridges, so no such walk exists — a fact that has nothing to do with distances or angles, only with a connection pattern.

Euler had not yet named a field, but he had located its founding move: build an argument entirely out of connectivity, throwing away every measurement a ruler could report. Decades earlier, Leibniz had speculated about a "geometry of position," analysis situs, that would need no numbers at all; Euler's bridge argument is usually read as the first real specimen of it. The subject did not receive rigorous foundations, or the name topology, until the later work of Möbius, Listing, and Riemann, culminating in Henri Poincaré's 1895 paper — titled, in Leibniz's old phrase, Analysis Situs — which is generally credited as the birth of the field in its modern form.

Why build a subject with no ruler at all?

Many important questions genuinely do not care about distance. A subway map preserves the order in which stations connect, not the geographic distances between them, and it is topologically faithful even though it is geometrically wildly distorted. Whether a function's graph has a "hole" or a "jump" is a question about the connectivity of a set of points, not about exact coordinates — the same question this site's essays on limits and derivatives circle around, asked here in its most general form. And some of the deepest facts in mathematics are true for every sphere regardless of its exact radius or bumps — for instance, that any continuous assignment of a wind direction to every point of the Earth's surface must have at least one point of exactly zero wind. Statements that broad need a framework light enough to not care about measurement at all, and topology is that framework.

Note — "rubber-sheet geometry"

Topology is often nicknamed rubber-sheet geometry: imagine every shape drawn on an infinitely stretchy sheet of rubber. You may stretch, compress, bend, and twist the sheet however you like — but never cut it, and never glue two points together that weren't already touching. Whatever survives every such deformation is a topological property. Length, area, and angle do not survive — stretch the sheet and they change freely. Connectedness, the number of holes, and whether a curve crosses itself do survive, and Panel V turns exactly those survivors into a precise toolkit.

Open Sets — The Building Block

throwing away distance itself, and keeping only the idea of a "nearby" point

Where "open" already lives, in disguise

Anyone who has worked through the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) definition of a limit has already met the idea this panel generalizes, just wearing different clothes. A set \(U\subseteq\mathbb R\) is called open if every point of \(U\) has an entire wiggle-room interval around it that still fits inside \(U\):

Definition — open set on the real line

\(U\subseteq\mathbb R\) is open if, for every \(x\in U\), there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that the whole interval \((x-\varepsilon,\,x+\varepsilon)\) is contained in \(U\).

An open interval \((a,b)\) satisfies this immediately — move a hair inward from any interior point and you still have room on both sides. A closed interval \([a,b]\) fails it at exactly its two endpoints: no interval centered at \(a\) fits entirely inside \([a,b]\), because every such interval sticks out to the left of \(a\). This single wiggle-room idea is already doing real work — it is precisely what lets an \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) limit proof talk about "getting close" without ever specifying how close is close enough in advance.

Generalizing: a topology needs no distance at all

The wiggle-room definition above still secretly depends on a ruler — it needs a distance \(\varepsilon\) to build the interval \((x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\). The genuinely general version of topology throws even that away. Instead of deriving open sets from a distance function, declare by fiat which subsets of a set \(X\) count as "open," subject to three bookkeeping rules that make the resulting structure behave the way "open" ought to behave.

Definition — topological space

A topology on a set \(X\) is a collection \(\tau\) of subsets of \(X\), called the open sets, satisfying:

(1) \(\varnothing\) and \(X\) are both in \(\tau\).
(2) the union of any collection of sets in \(\tau\) — even infinitely many — is again in \(\tau\).
(3) the intersection of any finite collection of sets in \(\tau\) is again in \(\tau\).

The pair \((X,\tau)\) is called a topological space.

Why finite intersections, but arbitrary unions?

The asymmetry between rules (2) and (3) looks arbitrary until you see what happens without it. Consider the open intervals \(\left(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n\right)\) for \(n=1,2,3,\dots\), each one open in the ordinary sense of Panel II's first definition. Intersect all of them at once:

Every one of the infinitely many intervals shares only the point \(0\) — any nonzero \(x\), however small, fails to lie in \(\left(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n\right)\) once \(n\) is large enough that \(\tfrac1n<|x|\). But \(\{0\}\) is not open: no interval centered at \(0\) is contained in a single point. So an infinite intersection of open sets can fail to be open, even though every finite intersection of them is perfectly fine. Rule (3) is written with the word "finite" precisely to keep this counterexample out — it is not a simplification made for convenience, it is a boundary drawn exactly where the pattern breaks.

Warning — arbitrary unions, but only finite intersections

Get the two rules backwards and the definition collapses. Unions never cause this problem — a union only ever adds points, so if every set in a union is open, every point gained still keeps its wiggle-room. Intersections shrink a set toward a single point as you add more of them, and the counterexample above shows that shrinking process can squeeze the wiggle-room down to exactly zero in the infinite limit.

Two extreme, and instructive, examples

The three axioms are satisfied, almost embarrassingly easily, by two topologies sitting at opposite extremes of the same set \(X\).

A basis — generating a topology from a short list

Listing every open set individually is unnecessary and usually impossible (the standard topology on \(\mathbb R\) already has uncountably many open sets). Instead, name a smaller collection \(\mathcal B\) of "basic" sets — for \(\mathbb R\), the open intervals \((a,b)\) — and declare the open sets of the topology to be exactly the sets that can be written as a union of members of \(\mathcal B\). This is called a basis, and it is how the standard topology on \(\mathbb R\) in the table above is actually built: not by listing every open set, but by listing the intervals and taking every possible union of them.

Insight — nearness without numbers

Once a topology is defined this way — an abstract list of "open" subsets satisfying three axioms — nothing about \(X\) itself needs to resemble a line or a plane at all. \(X\) could be a set of functions, a set of polynomials, or a set with no geometric picture in mind whatsoever. As long as some collection of its subsets satisfies the three axioms above, every idea in the rest of this essay — continuity, connectedness, compactness — applies to it unchanged. That generality, bought at the price of giving up distance, is the entire payoff of Panel II's definition.

Continuity, Redefined

the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) definition, rewritten with no epsilon, no delta, and no distance in sight

Recall the metric definition

The familiar definition says \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is continuous at \(a\) if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that \(|x-a|<\delta\) forces \(|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon\). Read geometrically: name any target tolerance \(\varepsilon\) around the output \(f(a)\), and a matching input tolerance \(\delta\) around \(a\) can always be found that guarantees landing inside it. Every piece of that sentence — the two absolute-value inequalities — is a distance statement.

Translating it into open-set language

Rephrase the same sentence one layer up. "\(|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon\)" says \(f(x)\) lies in the open interval \((f(a)-\varepsilon,f(a)+\varepsilon)\). "\(|x-a|<\delta\)" says \(x\) lies in the open interval \((a-\delta,a+\delta)\). So the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) condition says: for every open interval \(V\) around \(f(a)\), some open interval \(U\) around \(a\) maps entirely inside \(V\) — every point of \(U\) has its image landing in \(V\). That is exactly the statement that \(U\subseteq f^{-1}(V)\), and since every open set in \(\mathbb R\) is a union of such intervals (Panel II's basis), the condition can be pushed to its logical limit: require it for every open set at once, not interval by interval.

Definition — topological continuity

Let \((X,\tau_X)\) and \((Y,\tau_Y)\) be topological spaces. A function \(f:X\to Y\) is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open: for every \(U\in\tau_Y\),

Checking the two definitions agree on \(\mathbb R\)

Suppose \(f\) is continuous in the preimage-of-open sense. Fix \(a\) and \(\varepsilon>0\); the interval \(V=(f(a)-\varepsilon,f(a)+\varepsilon)\) is open, so \(f^{-1}(V)\) is open and contains \(a\). By Panel II's definition of open, some interval \((a-\delta,a+\delta)\subseteq f^{-1}(V)\) — which says exactly \(|x-a|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon\), the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) condition. Conversely, if \(f\) is \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\)-continuous at every point and \(U\) is any open set, then every \(a\in f^{-1}(U)\) has some open interval around \(f(a)\) inside \(U\) (since \(U\) is open), and the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) condition supplies a \(\delta\)-interval around \(a\) mapping into it — so \(f^{-1}(U)\) is open, being a union of such intervals. The two definitions are the same statement, viewed through different vocabulary.

Note — why bother with the harder-looking version

The preimage definition needs no mention of numbers, distances, or "getting close" — only the concept of an open set, which Panel II showed can exist on spaces with no distance function whatsoever. That is exactly why the preimage version, not the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) version, is the one actually used the moment a space has no natural notion of distance at all — a space of functions, a space of shapes, a space built purely from an abstract topology.

Homeomorphism, previewed

Continuity by itself is a one-directional statement — it only says forward images behave, not that the map is reversible. Chaining two continuity conditions together gives the notion this essay has been circling since Panel I:

Definition — homeomorphism

A function \(f:X\to Y\) is a homeomorphism if it is a bijection, and both \(f\) and its inverse \(f^{-1}\) are continuous. Two spaces admitting a homeomorphism between them are called homeomorphic, written \(X\cong Y\), and topology treats them as literally the same object.

Warning — a continuous bijection is not automatically a homeomorphism

Take \(X=[0,2\pi)\) and \(Y\) the unit circle, with \(f(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). This \(f\) is a continuous bijection — every angle in the half-open interval hits a distinct point of the circle exactly once. But \(f^{-1}\) is not continuous at the point \((1,0)\): points on the circle just below angle \(0\) are close to \((1,0)\) but their preimages sit near \(2\pi\), far from the preimage \(0\) of \((1,0)\) itself. The map "cuts" the circle open at one seam without ever showing it in the forward direction. (There is a clean sufficient condition that rules this failure out — a continuous bijection out of a compact space into a well-behaved target is automatically a homeomorphism — but \([0,2\pi)\) fails to be compact for exactly the reason Panel V makes precise, and Panel VIII points toward where that theorem is actually proved.)

Homeomorphism — When Two Shapes Are Really the Same

the equivalence relation topology is built around, and how to actually construct one by hand

Building one explicitly: a square's boundary and a circle

Panel I claimed a circle and the boundary of a square are topologically identical. Here is the map that proves it. Let \(\partial S\) be the boundary of the square \([-1,1]^2\), and send each point \(p\in\partial S\) radially out to the unit circle:

This is well-defined because \(\partial S\) never contains the origin (every boundary point has a coordinate of magnitude exactly \(1\), so \(\lVert p\rVert_2\ge 1>0\)), and it is continuous because it's built from continuous operations — the norm, and division by a nonvanishing continuous function. Every ray out of the origin crosses \(\partial S\) exactly once (the square is convex and contains the origin in its interior), so \(f\) is a bijection onto the circle. The inverse sends a circle point back out to the boundary along the same ray by an explicit, equally continuous scaling factor. Both directions continuous, both directions defined everywhere they need to be: \(f\) is a homeomorphism.

Why the coffee-cup joke works for any cup and any donut

The square-to-circle map above was built by hand for one specific pair of shapes. The coffee cup and the donut are harder to write an explicit formula for, but a general theorem does the work instead: the classification of compact surfaces shows that every closed surface (with no boundary and no self-intersection) is completely determined, up to homeomorphism, by two numbers — whether it can be embedded without a "twist" like a Möbius strip's, and its genus, the number of handles or holes it has. A coffee cup and a donut are both closed surfaces of genus \(1\), so the classification theorem guarantees a homeomorphism exists between them, even without ever writing one down by hand. Panel V returns to genus as the sharpest tool this essay covers for telling surfaces apart.

Homeomorphism is an equivalence relation

Three quick facts justify treating "\(\cong\)" as a genuine notion of sameness rather than a one-off trick:

Reflexive — the identity map \(X\to X\) is trivially a homeomorphism, so \(X\cong X\) always.

Symmetric — if \(f:X\to Y\) is a homeomorphism, its inverse \(f^{-1}:Y\to X\) is continuous by definition and is itself a bijection with inverse \(f\), so \(f^{-1}\) is a homeomorphism too, giving \(Y\cong X\).

Transitive — if \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\) are homeomorphisms, the composite \(g\circ f\) is a continuous bijection (compositions of continuous bijections are continuous bijections), and \((g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}\) is continuous for the same reason, so \(X\cong Z\).

Insight

An equivalence relation partitions its universe into disjoint classes, and homeomorphism is no exception: it partitions every topological space that could possibly exist into topological types — a circle and a square boundary in the same class, a coffee cup and a donut in another, a solid ball and a hollow sphere in two different ones again. Panel V exists because "which class is this space in" needs a way to answer it that doesn't require exhibiting an explicit map every single time.

Invariants — What Survives the Stretch

properties immune to any homeomorphism — the toolkit for proving two shapes are genuinely different

Why you need invariants at all

Proving two spaces are homeomorphic just takes one explicit map, as Panel IV did for the square and the circle. Proving two spaces are not homeomorphic can never be done by trying maps and failing — failing to find one proves nothing, since the right map might simply not have been tried yet. What's needed instead is a property guaranteed to be preserved by every homeomorphism, so that finding it present in one space and absent in the other rules out every possible map at once, without checking any of them individually.

Definition — topological invariant

A property \(P\) of topological spaces is a topological invariant if, whenever \(X\cong Y\), \(X\) has property \(P\) exactly when \(Y\) does. Equivalently: if \(X\) has \(P\) and \(Y\) does not, then \(X\not\cong Y\) — no homeomorphism between them can exist.

Connectedness

A space is connected if it cannot be split into two disjoint, nonempty open sets whose union is the whole space; otherwise it is disconnected. Connectedness is an invariant because the continuous image of a connected space is always connected — a homeomorphism, being continuous both ways, can never turn one connected piece into two, nor merge two pieces into one.

Example. \(\mathbb R\) is connected. \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) is not: it splits into \((-\infty,0)\) and \((0,\infty)\), two disjoint nonempty open sets whose union is the whole space. So \(\mathbb R\not\cong\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) — deleting a single point from the line genuinely changes its topological type, even though it changes no visible "shape" in the usual sense.

Compactness

A space is compact if every open cover — every collection of open sets whose union is the whole space — has a finite subcollection that still covers it. On \(\mathbb R^n\), the Heine–Borel theorem says this is equivalent to the more familiar "closed and bounded," which is exactly the hidden ingredient behind the Extreme Value Theorem from calculus: a continuous function on a compact set always attains a maximum, precisely because compactness rules out the function "running away" toward a boundary point that isn't actually included.

Example. \([0,1]\) is compact. \((0,1)\) is not: the open cover \(\left\{\left(\tfrac1n,1\right): n=2,3,4,\dots\right\}\) covers all of \((0,1)\) (every \(x\) in the interval lies in \(\left(\tfrac1n,1\right)\) once \(n\) is large enough that \(\tfrac1nfinite subcollection has a largest \(n=N\) in it, and the point \(\tfrac1{N+1}\) is left uncovered. No finite subcover exists, so \((0,1)\) is not compact — and since compactness is an invariant, \([0,1]\not\cong(0,1)\).

Genus and the Euler characteristic

For a closed surface built as a polyhedron with \(V\) vertices, \(E\) edges, and \(F\) faces, the quantity \(\chi = V-E+F\) is an invariant called the Euler characteristic — it comes out the same number no matter which polyhedral decomposition of the surface you happen to use. A sphere always gives \(\chi=2\); a torus (donut surface) always gives \(\chi=0\); in general, a surface of genus \(g\) (g handles) has \(\chi=2-2g\). Since \(\chi\) is an invariant, a sphere and a torus can never be homeomorphic — no continuous deformation changes the number of holes, so no map can turn \(2\) into \(0\).

Insight

Every "are these the same shape?" question in topology reduces to exactly one of two moves: build an explicit homeomorphism (Panel IV), or find a single invariant where the two spaces disagree (this panel). There is no third option and no in-between — either a map exists connecting them or a permanent, homeomorphism-proof obstruction rules every possible map out at once.

Worked Examples

four short proofs, each one a direct application of a definition or invariant from Panels II–V

A circle is not homeomorphic to a closed interval

Claim. \(S^1\not\cong[0,1]\).

Proof. Suppose toward contradiction that \(f:[0,1]\to S^1\) is a homeomorphism. Pick any interior point \(a\in(0,1)\) and let \(X=[0,1]\setminus\{a\}\), \(Y=S^1\setminus\{f(a)\}\). Since \(f\) restricts to a homeomorphism between \(X\) and \(Y\) (removing corresponding points from two homeomorphic spaces leaves the remainders homeomorphic — the restricted map is still a continuous bijection with continuous inverse), connectedness of \(X\) and \(Y\) must agree. But \(X=[0,a)\cup(a,1]\) splits into two disjoint nonempty open pieces — disconnected — while \(Y\), a circle with a single point removed, is a single unbroken arc — connected. Contradiction. So no such \(f\) exists. \(\blacksquare\)

This is exactly Lab 2 from Panel VI, stated as a formal theorem: one puncture disconnects the interval but never the circle, and connectedness is enough to rule out every possible homeomorphism at once.

Checking continuity directly from the preimage definition

Let \(f(x)=x^2\) on \(\mathbb R\), and test the open set \(U=(-1,4)\). By hand: \(f^{-1}(U) = \{x : -1 < x^2 < 4\}\). Since \(x^2\ge0\) always, the left inequality holds for every real \(x\); the right inequality \(x^2<4\) holds exactly when \(-2every open \(U\), not just this one, but the pattern generalizes directly: preimages of open intervals under \(x^2\) are always open, which is the content of \(x^2\) being continuous everywhere, checked with no \(\varepsilon\) or \(\delta\) in sight.

A bounded interval and the entire real line are topologically identical

Take \(f(x) = \tan\!\big(\pi(x-\tfrac12)\big)\) on the open interval \((0,1)\). As \(x\) ranges over \((0,1)\), the quantity \(\pi(x-\tfrac12)\) ranges over \(\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\), and \(\tan\) is a continuous bijection from that interval onto all of \(\mathbb R\), with continuous inverse \(\arctan\). So \(f\) is a homeomorphism, and \((0,1)\cong\mathbb R\).

This is genuinely surprising the first time you see it: a set of finite length and the entire infinite line are, topologically, the exact same object. "Length" was never a topological invariant to begin with — it depends on a ruler, and Panel I's entire premise was that topology doesn't carry one.

How to Study Topology

prerequisites, a standard path through the subject, and how to actually make the definitions stick

What you need before starting

Two habits of mind matter more than any specific fact. The first is basic comfort with sets and functions — unions, intersections, bijections, the idea of a function as a rule that relocates points rather than "computes a number" — the same reading this site's essay on functions as mappings builds for linear algebra, needed here in its most general, structure-free form. The second is the \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) habit of mind from real analysis and calculus: the discipline of pinning down "arbitrarily close" with a precise, checkable condition. Panel III of this essay leaned on that habit directly to build the preimage definition of continuity; without it, "open set" and "continuous map" both risk staying permanently vague.

The standard path through the subject

1. Point-set topology — everything in this essay, extended: product spaces, quotient spaces, separation axioms (Hausdorff spaces and stronger conditions), and the compactness theorem mentioned as a forward-pointer in Panel III's warning callout. The standard reference is James Munkres's textbook Topology, which most courses follow closely.

2. Algebraic topology — assigns algebraic objects (groups, mostly) to topological spaces, in a way that turns "are these homeomorphic" questions Panel V couldn't settle into pure algebra questions instead. The fundamental group tracks loops that can't be shrunk to a point (it immediately explains why a disk and an annulus differ — one has loops that can't contract, the other doesn't); homology generalizes the Euler-characteristic idea from Panel V into a full algebraic invariant, one for every dimension at once. Allen Hatcher's textbook Algebraic Topology is the standard entry point and is freely available from the author.

3. Differential topology and manifolds — spaces that locally look like \(\mathbb R^n\) closely enough to support calculus on them. This is where topology reconnects directly with the rest of this site: the derivative, linearized in the companion Jacobian essay as a matrix of partial rates of change, is exactly the tool that lets you do calculus on a manifold once its topology has been pinned down.

4. Specialized branches to choose between once the above is solid: knot theory (which loops in 3-dimensional space can be untangled into a circle, and which cannot); geometric topology and the classification of 3-manifolds (the setting of the Poincaré conjecture, resolved by Grigori Perelman in 2003); topological data analysis, which uses the connectedness and hole-counting ideas of Panel V directly on real-world data sets; and the role of manifold topology in general relativity, where spacetime itself is modeled as a 4-dimensional manifold.

How to actually study it

Draw pictures, constantly, despite the abstract axioms. The axioms in Panel II look like pure symbol manipulation on first contact, but topology is one of the most visual subjects in mathematics once past that first wall — every serious topologist reasons with pictures of tori, Möbius strips, and deformations far more than with the raw axioms.

Don't skip breaking the axioms by hand. Panel II's infinite-intersection counterexample is short enough to reconstruct from memory, and most of the intuition for why the axioms are shaped the way they are lives in exercises exactly like it — building small examples that satisfy two of the three rules and fail the third.

Alternate between the abstract and the concrete. Keep a running list of named spaces — the circle, the torus, the Möbius strip, the Klein bottle, real projective space — and test every new abstract definition against all of them immediately. An abstract definition with no concrete home to test itself against rarely survives contact with a real problem.

Practice Panel VII's kind of proof relentlessly. "These two spaces are not homeomorphic, and here is the invariant that proves it" is the single most diagnostic skill in the entire subject — it is the one exercise that cannot be faked by pattern-matching, because it demands actually understanding what the invariant measures and why a homeomorphism is forced to preserve it.

Insight — one line to carry away

Topology is what is left of geometry once every ruler has been taken away and only continuity remains. Panels II and III turned that idea into two precise definitions — open set, continuous map — built entirely without distance. Panel IV turned it into an equivalence relation worth naming, homeomorphism. Panel V supplied the tools for telling equivalence classes apart when no explicit map can be found. Panel VIII, this panel, is simply the map for where the resulting subject goes next.
