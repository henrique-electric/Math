PROPOSITIONAL LOGIC

What Logic Is, Before Anything Is Assumed

building a theory of correct reasoning up from raw symbols, with nothing borrowed and nothing taken on faith

The problem logic exists to solve

An argument can be wrong in two entirely different ways. It can start from a false premise — "all birds can fly; penguins are birds; therefore penguins can fly" fails because the first premise is simply untrue. Or it can start from true premises and still fail, not because anything asserted is false, but because the conclusion does not actually follow from what was assumed — the reasoning itself is broken, independent of whether any particular sentence involved happens to be true. Logic is the study of this second kind of failure and its opposite: it asks which argument forms are guaranteed to carry truth from premises to conclusion, regardless of what the premises happen to be about. It is, in a precise sense, not a subject about the world at all — it is a subject about the shape of correct inference, applicable to any subject matter whatsoever.

This is a stronger and stranger claim than it first sounds. "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal" is valid. So is "all fish are purple; Nemo is a fish; therefore Nemo is purple" — and that argument is valid despite every one of its three sentences being false. Validity has nothing to do with truth of content; it is a claim about structure: if the premises were true, the conclusion could not fail to be true along with them. Separating this structural question from the factual question of what actually is true is the single founding move of the entire subject, and everything built across the panels that follow is, one way or another, a consequence of taking that separation completely seriously.

Definition — validity, stated precisely for the first time

An argument is valid if it is logically impossible for all of its premises to be true while its conclusion is false. Validity is a property of the argument's form, checkable without knowing or caring whether the premises are actually true. An argument is sound if it is valid and its premises are actually true — soundness is where logic finally reconnects with fact, but everything through Panel XVI of this essay is about validity alone.

Why "from first principles" means starting with symbols, not sentences

Ordinary language is a treacherous material to build a theory of correct reasoning out of. English sentences carry tone, context, ambiguity, and idiom; the same words can mean different things depending on who says them and when. "Or" sometimes means "at least one," sometimes "exactly one." "If" sometimes states a genuine causal claim and sometimes states nothing more than a formal correlation. A theory built directly on top of English inherits all of that instability. The first-principles move — the one this essay commits to before doing anything else — is to throw natural language away as the object of study and replace it with a small, fully specified formal language: a fixed, finite alphabet of symbols, and a completely explicit grammar dictating which strings of those symbols count as meaningful formulas at all. Panel II builds that alphabet and grammar with the same level of rigor a programming language's specification would demand — nothing about "what a formula means" is allowed to sneak in through the syntax; meaning is added back in only afterward, in Panel III, as a separate and independently specified layer.

Insight — syntax first, semantics second, on purpose

This two-layer separation — a purely mechanical grammar (syntax) followed by a completely independent theory of what the grammatical strings mean (semantics) — is the single organizing idea of this entire essay, and it is also, not coincidentally, the exact architecture of every programming language compiler ever built: a parser checks that source code is grammatically well-formed with zero regard for what it computes, and only afterward does an evaluator or a runtime give that well-formed code meaning. Formal logic is centuries older than compilers, but it is the same idea, and modern computer science borrowed it wholesale rather than reinventing it.

Propositional logic versus the logic that comes after it

This essay is scoped, deliberately, to exactly one layer of the subject: propositional logic (also called sentential logic or the propositional calculus), the theory of whole, already-settled statements combined by connectives like "and," "or," "not," and "if... then." It does not reach inside a statement to talk about "every" or "some" member of a domain — "all primes greater than 2 are odd" is treated as one indivisible unit, not decomposed into a quantifier ranging over a set of numbers. That richer machinery is predicate logic (or first-order logic), and it is a genuine extension built on top of everything here, not a replacement for it. Restricting scope this way is not a limitation apologized for — it is exactly what makes propositional logic the right place to build a theory completely from first principles: its formal language is small enough to specify in full on one page, its semantics is genuinely mechanical (checkable, in principle, by exhaustive case analysis for any formula, no matter how complex), and its proof theory is provably both sound and complete, in a sense Panel XV states and sketches a proof of. Predicate logic keeps the sound half of that guarantee but permanently loses decidability — there is no algorithm that always determines, in finite time, whether an arbitrary first-order sentence is valid. Propositional logic is where a first-principles construction of logic can be carried all the way to the end, with nothing left assumed and nothing left unresolved.

A short history of the idea that reasoning could be formalized

Aristotle, in the 4th century BCE, produced the first systematic study of valid argument forms — the theory of the syllogism, patterns like "all A are B; all B are C; therefore all A are C" catalogued and checked for validity independent of subject matter. This was already, in embryo, the central move of this essay: form separated from content. But Aristotle's system stayed tied to a small fixed set of sentence patterns (all A are B, no A are B, some A are B, some A are not B) and never developed an algebra for combining sentences freely with connectives.

That algebra arrived over two thousand years later. George Boole, in The Laws of Thought (1854), showed that logical reasoning could be treated as literal algebra — an algebra with exactly two values, later written 0 and 1 (or F and T), obeying operations that look almost, but not quite, like ordinary arithmetic. This is the direct ancestor of Panel VII's laws of logic, and of the Boolean algebra that runs every digital circuit built since. Gottlob Frege, in the 1870s, went further and built a full symbolic notation with rigorously specified inference rules — the first genuine formal proof system in the modern sense, the direct ancestor of Panels X and XI. Charles Sanders Peirce independently developed much of the same machinery, including the truth-functional analysis of connectives this essay's Panel IV depends on. Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead, in Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), attempted to derive the whole of mathematics from a logical foundation of exactly this kind. And it was Emil Post and, independently, Ludwig Wittgenstein, around 1920–1921, who introduced the truth table as the clean, mechanical semantic device this essay uses starting in Panel III — reducing "what does this formula mean" to a finite, exhaustively checkable procedure for the first time in the subject's history.

Note — why this history matters to a "first principles" essay

It would be possible to present propositional logic as a ready-made system, definitions handed down with no motivation. The history above exists because every major definitional choice in this essay was, historically, a genuine discovery, not an arbitrary convention — Boole's insight that logic is an algebra, Frege's insight that inference could be reduced to purely mechanical symbol manipulation, and Post and Wittgenstein's insight that meaning itself could be reduced to a finite table. Building "from first principles" means retracing enough of that path that each definition in the panels ahead earns its place, rather than simply being asserted.

The two roads this essay will walk down at once

Modern logic actually offers two independent, and independently interesting, answers to "what makes an inference correct" — and a genuinely first-principles treatment has to build both, because the deepest fact in the whole subject (Panel XV) is that they turn out to agree.

Semantic road — Panels III, IV, VI

A conclusion follows from premises if every way of assigning true/false to the underlying propositions that makes all the premises true also makes the conclusion true. This is checked by exhaustive case analysis — a truth table — and never mentions "proof" or "derivation" at all. The central relation is written \(\Gamma \models \varphi\): "\(\varphi\) is a semantic consequence of \(\Gamma\)."

Syntactic road — Panels X, XI, XII

A conclusion follows from premises if it can be reached from them by a finite chain of mechanical symbol-manipulation steps — applying axioms and inference rules — with no reference to truth values at all, the same way a chess move is legal or illegal without reference to who is winning. The central relation is written \(\Gamma \vdash \varphi\): "\(\varphi\) is provable from \(\Gamma\)."

These look, on their face, like they could have turned out to be different relations — one about truth in every possible case, the other about finite chains of symbol shuffling with no semantic content at all. Panel XV proves, in full, that for propositional logic they are exactly the same relation: \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\) if and only if \(\Gamma\models\varphi\). That coincidence — soundness plus completeness — is arguably the single deepest fact this essay builds toward, and it is the reason a mechanical proof procedure is guaranteed to find a derivation for every genuinely valid argument, and never one for an invalid one.

Syntax — The Alphabet and Grammar of Formulas

a completely explicit specification of which strings of symbols are meaningful, before any of them are given meaning

The alphabet

A formal language begins with an alphabet: a fixed set of symbols, and nothing else. Propositional logic's alphabet has exactly three kinds of symbols, and nothing outside these three kinds is ever a legal symbol in a formula.

The alphabet of propositional logic

Two choices here are worth pausing on precisely because they are choices, not necessities. First, the set of propositional variables is taken to be countably infinite — there is no largest number of atomic claims a formula is allowed to mention, because a language that ran out of variables after, say, one hundred of them would be unable to express some perfectly ordinary finite arguments once they got large enough. Writing \(p_1,p_2,p_3,\dots\) rather than a fixed finite list is how a formal language commits to never running out. Second, the specific set of five connectives \(\{\neg,\wedge,\vee,\to,\leftrightarrow\}\) is generous, not minimal — Panel IX proves that a single two-place connective (NAND, or equivalently NOR) is already enough to define all five. Propositional logic is standardly presented with all five anyway because each earns its keep as a distinct, commonly needed shape of claim, not because all five are logically necessary.

Note — the alphabet says nothing about meaning yet

Nothing so far says that ¬ means "not" or that p stands for some particular sentence about the world. The alphabet is pure notation: a set of marks. Meaning is added in Panel III, as a completely separate layer built on top of the syntax defined here — and it is added to the syntax, not folded into its definition, which is exactly what makes it possible to ask meaningful syntactic questions (is this string well-formed? what is its main connective? how deep is it nested?) without first deciding what any of it means.

The alphabet fixed above is one standard choice among several; nothing about the underlying logic changes if a different textbook draws its symbols differently, and recognizing the variants on sight is a genuinely practical skill the first time an unfamiliar book is opened.

The "Polish notation" column is worth a specific note: Jan Łukasiewicz — the same logician behind Panel X's axiom system — invented a fully parenthesis-free notation where every connective is written before its arguments rather than between them (\(Cpq\) instead of \(p\to q\)), which sidesteps Panel V's entire precedence-and-associativity apparatus by construction: a prefix formula parses unambiguously with no convention needed at all, at the cost of being considerably harder for a human eye to read at a glance. Reverse Polish (postfix) notation, the same idea with connectives written last, is the exact scheme most stack-based calculators and several programming language interpreters use internally for arithmetic, for precisely the same parenthesis-free reason.

Strings, and the vast majority of them that mean nothing at all

A string over this alphabet is simply any finite sequence of these symbols, in any order, including nonsense: )p∧∧¬( is a string. So is pq→. So is ((((p. Almost every string over the alphabet is garbage — a sequence with no coherent grouping, no way to say what its "top-level" operation even is. The entire purpose of a formal grammar is to carve out, from this enormous sea of garbage, exactly the strings that are structurally meaningful — called well-formed formulas, universally abbreviated wffs — and to do it by a rule so mechanical that a computer with no understanding of logic whatsoever could check membership correctly.

The grammar, stated as a recursive definition

The set of well-formed formulas, written WFF, is defined by exactly one base clause and five recursive clauses. This is a Backus–Naur form (BNF) grammar, the identical style used to specify the syntax of programming languages.

φ, ψ ::= p (atomic — p any propositional variable) | ( ¬ φ ) (negation) | ( φ ∧ ψ ) (conjunction) | ( φ ∨ ψ ) (disjunction) | ( φ → ψ ) (conditional) | ( φ ↔ ψ ) (biconditional) Read as: "a wff is either a bare propositional variable, or one of these five shapes built by wrapping parentheses around a connective applied to wff(s) that are themselves already known, by this very definition, to be well-formed."
Definition — well-formed formula, in words

The set WFF of well-formed formulas is the smallest set of strings over the alphabet such that: (base case) every propositional variable is in WFF; and (recursive cases) if \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are in WFF, then so are \((\neg\varphi)\), \((\varphi\wedge\psi)\), \((\varphi\vee\psi)\), \((\varphi\to\psi)\), and \((\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)\). Nothing is in WFF except by finitely many applications of these clauses.

The word "smallest" in that definition is doing real work, and it is worth being explicit about why. Simply listing the base case and the five recursive clauses as things that are true of WFF is not yet enough to pin the set down uniquely — infinitely many sets of strings satisfy "contains every variable, and is closed under the five constructions," including, trivially, the set of all strings over the alphabet. Requiring WFF to be the smallest such set — equivalently, requiring every member to be reachable from the base case by finitely many applications of the recursive clauses, with nothing else admitted — is what rules out the garbage strings and makes WFF exactly what intuition wants: formulas, and only formulas, each one buildable by an explicit finite construction history.

Building a formula, one construction step at a time

Membership in WFF is not asserted; it is demonstrated, by exhibiting the exact sequence of grammar clauses used to build the string up from bare variables. Take the string \((( p\wedge q)\to(\neg r))\).

  • qbase case
  • rbase case
  • (p ∧ q)∧-clause, from 1,2
  • (¬r)¬-clause, from 3
  • ((p∧q) → (¬r))→-clause, from 4,5

Notice, too, that the formation sequence is exactly the leaves-to-root traversal of a tree, and drawing that tree is the standard, far more readable way to display a formula's structure — the subject of Panel V.

    Refusing membership is just as mechanical as granting it — a string fails to be a wff exactly when no formation sequence like the one above can be built for it, and the failure is always traceable to one specific grammar clause not being satisfiable.

    Subformulas, and the main connective

    Two structural notions recur constantly enough, in every panel from here forward, that they need names now.

    Definition — immediate subformula, subformula, main connective

    The immediate subformulas of a compound formula are the formula(s) its outermost clause was directly applied to: \((\neg\varphi)\) has the single immediate subformula \(\varphi\); \((\varphi\wedge\psi)\) has the two immediate subformulas \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\); an atomic formula has none. The subformulas of \(\varphi\) are \(\varphi\) itself, together with the subformulas of each of its immediate subformulas, applied recursively all the way down to the atoms. The main connective (or principal connective) of a compound formula is the connective applied at the very last, outermost step of its formation sequence — the one whose two (or one) immediate subformulas, evaluated first, are combined last.

    In \(((p\wedge q)\to(\neg r))\), the main connective is \(\to\), because the formula's very last construction step combined \((p\wedge q)\) and \((\neg r)\) with the conditional clause — everything else happened strictly earlier, strictly "inside." Its full list of subformulas is \(p\), \(q\), \(r\), \((p\wedge q)\), \((\neg r)\), and the whole formula itself. Identifying the main connective correctly is not a cosmetic exercise: Panel III's entire evaluation procedure, and Panel V's parsing procedure, both work by locating the main connective first and recursing into its immediate subformulas second — get the main connective wrong and every downstream computation is wrong too.

    Structural induction — proving something about every formula at once

    Because WFF is defined as the smallest set closed under the base case and five recursive clauses, it comes with a proof technique for free, in exactly the way the natural numbers' inductive definition (0, and closure under successor) comes with ordinary mathematical induction for free. This technique, structural induction, is used repeatedly starting in the very next panel, so it is worth stating once, carefully, here.

    Definition — the principle of structural induction on formulas

    To prove that every formula in WFF has some property \(P\), it suffices to prove two things: (base case) \(P(p)\) holds for every propositional variable \(p\); and (inductive step) for each of the five recursive clauses, assuming \(P\) already holds for the immediate subformula(s) involved (the induction hypothesis), show that \(P\) then holds for the compound formula built from them. If both parts are established, \(P\) holds for every member of WFF, with no exceptions — because every member of WFF is, by the "smallest set" clause of its own definition, reachable from atoms by finitely many applications of the five clauses, and the two parts above show \(P\) survives every single one of those steps.

    This is not an abstract formality kept for its own sake. Panel III's definition of truth-under-a-valuation is only well-defined at all because of unique readability, itself proved by structural induction (sketched below); Panel VII's laws of logic are proved, in the cases that go beyond a finite truth table, by structural induction on formula complexity; and Panel XV's soundness theorem is a structural induction on the length of a proof, a close relative of this same technique. Structural induction is, in a real sense, the mathematical machine that makes every "for every formula whatsoever" claim in this essay provable rather than merely plausible.

    Unique readability — why the parentheses are non-negotiable

    One fact has been used silently already and deserves to be stated and defended explicitly: every compound wff has exactly one main connective, at exactly one position in the string, decomposing it into exactly one pair of immediate subformulas (or one, for negation). This sounds obvious, but obviousness is precisely what a first-principles treatment refuses to accept without proof — and it is not automatic. It is possible to design a grammar careless enough that some string can be parsed two structurally different ways, and a grammar with that flaw is useless for logic, because "the truth value of a formula" (Panel III) would stop being well-defined the moment a single string admitted two different formation histories with two different answers.

    Warning — this is exactly why the grammar mandates full parenthesization

    Strip every parenthesis out of \(((p\wedge q)\to(\neg r))\) and the bare string p∧q→¬r remains genuinely ambiguous on its own — nothing in the string itself says whether \(\wedge\) or \(\to\) applies first. (Panel V resolves this ambiguity for informal, human-readable writing via a fixed precedence convention — the same convention arithmetic uses to make 2+3×4 unambiguous without writing 2+(3×4) every time.) The formal grammar above sidesteps the whole issue by brute force: every recursive clause wraps its result in a fresh pair of parentheses, so the fully official syntax never actually produces an ambiguous string in the first place. Precedence is a convenience layered on top for readability; full parenthesization is the mechanism that makes the underlying theory watertight.

    The unique readability theorem states this guarantee formally: every non-atomic wff \(\varphi\) has a unique main connective, and hence a unique decomposition into immediate subformulas via a unique one of the five recursive clauses. The proof is a structural induction, and its key lemma is a fact about balanced parentheses.

    The balance lemma, proved in full

    For a string \(s\), define its parenthesis balance \(\beta(s)\) by scanning left to right, adding \(+1\) for every ( and \(-1\) for every ), and ignoring every other symbol.

    Lemma — balance

    For every wff \(\varphi\): (i) \(\beta(\varphi)=0\); and (ii) for every proper, non-empty prefix \(s\) of \(\varphi\) (that is, \(s\) is the first \(k\) symbols of \(\varphi\) for some \(00\) if \(\varphi\) is non-atomic, and no proper prefix exists at all if \(\varphi\) is atomic.

    Proof, by structural induction on \(\varphi\) (Panel II's own induction principle, used on itself for the first time).

    Base case, \(\varphi=p\): a bare variable contains no parentheses at all, so \(\beta(p)=0\) trivially, and it has no proper non-empty prefix worth checking (a single-symbol string's only prefixes are itself and the empty string) — clause (ii) holds vacuously.

    Inductive step, \(\varphi=(\neg\psi)\): by the induction hypothesis, \(\beta(\psi)=0\) and every proper prefix of \(\psi\) has positive balance (or \(\psi\) is atomic). Every proper prefix of \(\varphi\) is either just the opening ( alone (balance \(+1>0\)), or (¬ followed by a proper prefix of \(\psi\) (balance \(1+\beta(\text{that prefix of }\psi)\), which is \(>1>0\) if \(\psi\) is non-atomic by the induction hypothesis, or exactly \(1>0\) if \(\psi\) is atomic and the prefix is all of \(\psi\)) — every case stays strictly positive. The full string \(\varphi\) itself has balance \(1+\beta(\psi)-1=0+0=0\), confirming (i).

    Inductive step, \(\varphi=(\psi\wedge\chi)\) (the other three binary clauses are identical in shape): by the induction hypothesis, \(\beta(\psi)=0=\beta(\chi)\), and every proper prefix of each has positive balance. A proper prefix of \(\varphi\) is either the opening ( alone; or ( followed by a proper prefix of \(\psi\) (balance \(1+(\text{positive})>0\)); or ( followed by all of \(\psi\) followed by ∧ (balance \(1+0=1>0\)); or that, followed by a (possibly empty, possibly proper, possibly complete) portion of \(\chi\) not reaching the final closing parenthesis (balance stays \(1+\beta(\text{that portion})\geq 1\), since a portion of \(\chi\) that is a proper prefix has non-negative balance and \(\chi\) itself has balance exactly 0, so any partial portion up to and including all of \(\chi\) keeps the running total at or above \(1\), always \(>0\) short of the very last symbol). The complete string \(\varphi\) has balance \(1+0+0-1=0\), confirming (i). ∎

    Insight — this is the lemma that makes "the main connective" a safe thing to define

    Suppose, for contradiction, a wff \(\varphi\) could be decomposed two structurally different ways — say as \((\psi_1\wedge\chi_1)\) and also as \((\psi_2\to\chi_2)\), with \(\psi_1\neq\psi_2\) as strings. The balance lemma says \(\psi_1\) (a wff in its own right) has balance exactly 0 and every proper prefix of it has strictly positive balance — meaning the position where \(\psi_1\) ends is pinned down uniquely, as the first point after the opening parenthesis where the running balance returns to exactly the level it needs to signal "a complete wff has just closed." That position cannot simultaneously be where \(\psi_2\) ends unless \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are the same string, contradicting the assumption. The connective symbol immediately following that uniquely-pinned-down position is therefore forced to be the same symbol in both readings too. No wff admits two different decompositions — exactly the unique readability theorem, now resting on a fully written-out proof rather than an appeal to intuition.

    Semantics — Giving the Symbols Meaning

    a second, completely independent layer on top of the syntax: what a well-formed formula actually says, and how to compute it

    Truth values and valuations

    Semantics starts with a two-element set of truth values, \(\mathbb{B}=\{T,F\}\) — the formal home of the bivalence principle: every proposition is assigned exactly one of these two values, never both, never neither, never a third option. Meaning is attached to a formula by first attaching meaning to its atoms.

    Definition — valuation (truth assignment)

    A valuation (or truth assignment) is a function \(v\) from the set of propositional variables to \(\mathbb{B}\), assigning a truth value \(v(p)\in\{T,F\}\) to every variable \(p\). A valuation is a completely free choice — nothing about the grammar of Panel II constrains which variables get T and which get F; a valuation is exactly the raw data needed before any formula built from those variables can be evaluated at all.

    A valuation, by itself, only settles the truth of atomic formulas. What makes propositional logic compositional — its single most important semantic feature — is that a valuation on the atoms alone, plus a fixed recursive rule, is enough to settle the truth of every compound formula ever built from them, no matter how deeply nested.

    Note — two values is a choice, not a law of nature

    Nothing about formal logic in the abstract forces exactly two truth values. Three-valued logics add a middle value — commonly read as "unknown" — and are genuinely used in database theory, where a query field can be legitimately missing rather than true or false; some are used to model vagueness (is a 5 o'clock shadow "bald"?) with a continuum of values between 0 and 1 instead of a sharp cutoff. This essay's entire construction — the truth tables of Panel IV, the tautology/contradiction split of Panel VI, the proof theory of Panels X and XI, the completeness theorem of Panel XV — is built on exactly two values and would need to be rebuilt, not merely extended, for any of these alternatives. Bivalence is asserted once, right here, and then relied on silently everywhere else; a genuinely first-principles reading of this essay should notice that every later panel is quietly cashing in this one early commitment.

    Extending a valuation to every formula, recursively

    Given a valuation \(v\), its extension to the whole of WFF (still written \(v\), by convention, once the extension is defined — some textbooks write \(\overline v\) or \([\![\cdot]\!]_v\) to keep the two functions visually distinct) is defined by structural recursion, mirroring the grammar clause for clause.

    v(p) = the value already assigned by the valuation (base case) v(¬φ) = T iff v(φ) = F v(φ∧ψ) = T iff v(φ) = T and v(ψ) = T v(φ∨ψ) = T iff v(φ) = T or v(ψ) = T (or both) v(φ→ψ) = T iff v(φ) = F or v(ψ) = T v(φ↔ψ) = T iff v(φ) and v(ψ) have the same truth value

    Every clause on the right refers only to the truth values of \(\varphi\)'s and \(\psi\)'s immediate subformulas — never to anything outside them, never to any other formula floating around, and never to anything about what the symbols "intuitively" mean beyond this rule. This is the formal content of the claim, made informally back in Panel II, that a compound formula's truth is completely determined by its atomic pieces: it is determined, mechanically, by this recursive definition and nothing else. A connective defined this way — its output determined completely by the truth values of its inputs, with no other information involved — is called truth-functional, and it is a genuinely restrictive property: "because," in the causal sense ("it rained because the pressure dropped"), is not truth-functional, since two true statements can be causally connected or not regardless of their individual truth values. Every one of the five connectives fixed by this grammar is truth-functional by construction — that is exactly what the table above specifies, and it is why Panel IV can characterize each connective completely by a small finite table with no further commentary required.

    Note — this recursion is well-defined only because of Panel II's unique readability theorem

    Defining \(v(\varphi\wedge\psi)\) in terms of \(v(\varphi)\) and \(v(\psi)\) is only a legitimate definition if every compound formula decomposes into its immediate subformulas in exactly one way — otherwise "the" values \(v(\varphi)\) and \(v(\psi)\) being referred to would not be uniquely determined by the string \((\varphi\wedge\psi)\) alone, and the recursion could silently disagree with itself depending on which decomposition was used. This is precisely the guarantee Panel II's unique readability theorem supplies, and it is the reason that theorem was proved before semantics was ever introduced, rather than being left as an afterthought.

    Working an evaluation by hand, bottom-up

    Take \(\varphi = ((p\wedge q)\to(\neg r))\) again, under the valuation \(v(p)=T\), \(v(q)=F\), \(v(r)=T\). Evaluation proceeds by working the formation sequence from Panel II forward — computing the truth value of each subformula only after the truth values of everything it depends on are already known.

    v(p) = T given v(q) = F given v(r) = T given v(p∧q) = T ∧ F = F (clause: T iff both T) v(¬r) = ¬T = F (clause: T iff r is F) v((p∧q)→(¬r)) = F → F = T (clause: T iff antecedent is F, or consequent is T — here the antecedent p∧q is already F, so it's T regardless of the consequent: vacuous truth, Panel IV)

    The whole formula evaluates to T under this particular valuation. Under a different valuation it might evaluate to F — evaluation is always relative to a valuation, and "the truth value of \(\varphi\)," asked without specifying one, is not yet a meaningful question for a contingent formula (Panel VI names this category precisely).

    Truth tables as exhaustive enumeration

    A formula in \(n\) distinct propositional variables has exactly \(2^n\) possible valuations restricted to those variables — each variable independently T or F, and the choices multiply. A truth table is nothing more than an organized listing of all \(2^n\) of them, together with the resulting value of the formula (and, conventionally, of every intermediate subformula, as scaffolding) on each one. This is where the syntax of Panel II and the semantics of this panel meet their first genuinely mechanical payoff: because evaluation is a finite recursive computation (Panel II's structural induction guarantees the recursion in this panel always terminates, since every recursive call is on a strictly shorter formula) and because there are only finitely many valuations to check when only finitely many variables are involved, every question about a single formula's truth-theoretic behavior is, in principle, answerable by an entirely mechanical, terminating procedure — build the table, read off the answer. Panel VI names the three possible outcomes; Panel XVI returns to this fact and asks how expensive that mechanical procedure actually is.

    Insight — variables that don't appear are simply irrelevant

    If a formula \(\varphi\) contains no occurrence of the variable \(s\), then \(v(\varphi)\) and \(v'(\varphi)\) are guaranteed equal for any two valuations \(v,v'\) that agree on every variable \(\varphi\) actually does contain, regardless of what \(v\) and \(v'\) do to \(s\). This is provable by a one-line structural induction (the recursive clauses above only ever consult the values of variables actually occurring in the subformulas being combined), and it is the formal reason a truth table for \(\varphi\) only ever needs columns for the variables genuinely present in \(\varphi\) — padding the table with an irrelevant extra variable would just double every row without changing a single entry in the final column.

    Satisfaction, models, and semantic entailment

    Three more pieces of vocabulary, all built directly on top of the evaluation function \(v\), carry the rest of this essay's semantic side.

    Definition — satisfaction, model, entailment

    A valuation \(v\) satisfies a formula \(\varphi\), written \(v\models\varphi\), if \(v(\varphi)=T\). Such a \(v\) is called a model of \(\varphi\). A valuation satisfies a set of formulas \(\Gamma\) if it satisfies every member of \(\Gamma\) simultaneously. A set \(\Gamma\) of formulas (semantically) entails a formula \(\varphi\), written \(\Gamma\models\varphi\), if every valuation that satisfies all of \(\Gamma\) also satisfies \(\varphi\) — equivalently, if there is no valuation making every member of \(\Gamma\) true while making \(\varphi\) false.

    This single relation, \(\Gamma\models\varphi\), is the formal, fully rigorous replacement for the informal notion of a "valid argument" from Panel I: \(\Gamma\) is the set of premises, \(\varphi\) is the conclusion, and \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) says exactly that it is impossible for every premise to be true while the conclusion is false — Panel I's definition of validity, now stated with zero appeal to intuition, entirely in terms of the recursive evaluation function built in this panel. Writing \(\models\varphi\) with no premises at all (equivalently, \(\varnothing\models\varphi\)) says \(\varphi\) is true under every valuation whatsoever, with no assumptions needed to force it — Panel VI gives that special case its own name.

    Precedence, Associativity, and Parse Trees

    how a human-readable formula with no parentheses still names exactly one wff from Panel II's official grammar

    The gap between formal syntax and how anyone actually writes a formula

    Panel II's grammar produces fully parenthesized strings — every single application of every connective wrapped in its own pair of parentheses, with zero ambiguity ever possible, at the cost of formulas that become close to unreadable past three or four connectives: \((((p\wedge q)\vee(\neg r))\to((\neg p)\vee s))\). No one, including professional logicians, actually writes formulas this way by hand. What is written instead is an abbreviated notation that drops "safe" parentheses according to a fixed convention — and the entire content of this panel is specifying that convention precisely enough that it, too, becomes unambiguous, even though it looks nothing like the official grammar on the page.

    Precedence — an order of operations for connectives

    Exactly the way \(2+3\times4\) is universally read as \(2+(3\times4)\) because multiplication binds tighter than addition, the five connectives carry a standard precedence, from tightest-binding to loosest.

    TIGHTEST LOOSEST ¬ > ∧ > ∨ > → > ↔ Example: ¬p ∧ q → r ∨ s officially parses, one connective at a time, as: 1. ¬p negate first — tightest binding 2.     Associativity — what happens when the same connective repeats

    Precedence alone does not resolve a string like \(p\to q\to r\) — both connectives are \(\to\), so precedence between different connectives says nothing about how two occurrences of the same one should group. This is settled by a separate, per-connective associativity convention.

    Because \(\to\) is not associative in the algebraic sense — \((p\to q)\to r\) and \(p\to(q\to r)\) are genuinely different formulas, disagreeing on some rows of their truth table — the right-associativity convention is not a harmless bookkeeping choice the way it is for \(\wedge\) and \(\vee\). Take \(p=F,\,q=T,\,r=F\). Right-grouped: \(p\to(q\to r) = F\to(T\to F) = F\to F = T\) — vacuously true, since the outer antecedent \(p\) is false. Left-grouped: \((p\to q)\to r = (F\to T)\to F = T\to F = F\) — the inner conditional \(p\to q\) is vacuously true, and that true antecedent then forces the outer conditional to inherit \(r\)'s falsity. The two groupings disagree — T against F — on this single assignment, so they are genuinely different formulas, and a bare chain of \(\to\) with no parentheses would be silently ambiguous without a fixed convention for which grouping is meant. Right-associativity is exactly that fixed convention.

    Parse trees — the readable picture of a formation sequence

    Panel II's formation sequence for a formula is a list; the same information, drawn as a tree with atoms as leaves and connectives as internal nodes, is far easier to read at a glance, and it is the standard way logicians actually think about a formula's structure.

    Formula: ((¬p) ∧ q) → (r ∨ s) → ╱ ╲ ∧ ∨ ╱ ╲ ╱ ╲ ¬ q r s │ p Reading the tree bottom-up is exactly Panel III's evaluation procedure: leaves get their value from the valuation; each internal node's value is computed from its children's values, using that node's connective's clause; the root's value is the value of the whole formula.

    The root of the tree is, by definition, the main connective from Panel II — everything the unique readability theorem guarantees about "the" main connective being well-defined is exactly the guarantee that this tree has one unambiguous shape and not several competing ones. Every one of Panel III's evaluations in this essay, and every truth table row anywhere in it, is silently doing a bottom-up traversal of a tree that looks like this one.

    Insight — precedence and parse trees are the same fact, seen from two directions

    Precedence rules are a compact set of instructions for reconstructing the parse tree from a parenthesis-free string; the parse tree is the object those instructions are reconstructing. Neither is more fundamental than Panel II's official grammar — both are conveniences for humans built on top of it, and both are checkable against it: a claimed parse tree is correct exactly when reading its formation sequence bottom-up, then fully parenthesizing every step, reproduces a string the Panel II grammar actually generates.

    Tautology, Contradiction, Contingency, and Satisfiability

    Three categories, one truth table column

    Panel III defined \(v(\varphi)\) for a single valuation \(v\); building the full truth table means computing \(v(\varphi)\) for every valuation over \(\varphi\)'s variables, and the final column that results falls into exactly one of three mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive categories.

    \(p\vee\neg p\) is the law of the excluded middle: whatever \(p\) claims, either \(p\) or its denial holds, and Panel III's clause for \(\vee\) guarantees the disjunction is true regardless of which disjunct wins, since \(v(p)\) and \(v(\neg p)\) can never both be F under bivalence. \(p\wedge\neg p\) is the law of non-contradiction read as a formula: it is forced false on every row, since \(v(p)\) and \(v(\neg p)\) can never both be T either. These two laws are not independent discoveries — they are the same fact, that bivalence assigns each atom exactly one of T or F, viewed through \(\vee\) on one side and \(\wedge\) on the other; Panel IX's duality principle makes that mirroring completely explicit.

    Satisfiability — the single most important semantic property in the essay

    Definition — satisfiable, unsatisfiable

    A formula \(\varphi\) is satisfiable if it has at least one model — some valuation \(v\) with \(v(\varphi)=T\). It is unsatisfiable if it has none. A set of formulas \(\Gamma\) is satisfiable if some single valuation makes every member of \(\Gamma\) true simultaneously.

    Satisfiability sits underneath all three categories above as the more primitive notion, and every one of them reduces to it.

    The first row deserves to be sat with for a moment, because it is the exact idea Panel XVI's automated reasoning and Panel XI's ¬-Introduction rule both run on: proving \(\varphi\) is a tautology and proving \(\neg\varphi\) is unsatisfiable are, provably, the identical task, just phrased with the negation moved to opposite sides. Every tautology-checking procedure in this essay from this point forward — hand truth tables, the natural deduction reductio pattern, and the DPLL algorithm in Panel XVI — is, under the hood, a procedure for detecting unsatisfiability of \(\neg\varphi\).

    Reducing entailment to unsatisfiability

    Panel III defined \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) directly: every valuation satisfying \(\Gamma\) also satisfies \(\varphi\). There is an exactly equivalent, and computationally far more useful, restatement.

    Insight — the refutation lemma

    \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) holds if and only if \(\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi\}\) is unsatisfiable.

    Proof. Suppose \(\Gamma\models\varphi\), and suppose toward contradiction some valuation \(v\) satisfies every member of \(\Gamma\) as well as \(\neg\varphi\). Then \(v\) satisfies \(\Gamma\), so by \(\Gamma\models\varphi\), \(v(\varphi)=T\) — but \(v(\neg\varphi)=T\) forces \(v(\varphi)=F\) by Panel III's clause for negation, a contradiction. So no such \(v\) exists, i.e. \(\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi\}\) is unsatisfiable. Conversely, suppose \(\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi\}\) is unsatisfiable, and let \(v\) be any valuation satisfying all of \(\Gamma\). Since \(\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi\}\) has no model, \(v\) cannot also satisfy \(\neg\varphi\); so \(v(\neg\varphi)=F\), meaning \(v(\varphi)=T\). As \(v\) was an arbitrary model of \(\Gamma\), \(\Gamma\models\varphi\). ∎

    This one lemma is the reason every automated theorem prover for propositional logic — and, in Panel XVI, every practical SAT solver — is built to search for unsatisfiability rather than to search for validity directly: assume the premises, assume the denial of the desired conclusion, and hunt for a contradiction. Find one, and the entailment is proved; this is exactly the shape of the reductio ad absurdum proof technique formalized in Panel XI and used constantly from Panel XIV onward.

    Every row is T: \(\varphi\) is a tautology. Notice what makes it work — on every single row, at least one of \(p\to q\), \(q\to p\) is vacuously true, because the two conditionals share the same two atoms in reversed order, and an atom is either T or F, so one direction or the other always has a false antecedent. This is worth remembering as a pattern: "for any two claims, at least one implies the other" is a tautology purely because there are only two truth values to distribute between \(p\) and \(q\) — bivalence, again, doing the load-bearing work.

    Note — the standard decision procedure, and its cost

    Sorting any single formula into tautology / contradiction / contingency, or checking any single formula for satisfiability, is completely mechanical: build the truth table, and read off the answer, exactly as done above. What is not free is time — a formula in \(n\) variables has \(2^n\) rows, so this procedure, while always correct and always terminating, becomes impractical well before \(n\) reaches even 50. Whether some fundamentally faster general procedure exists is the P versus NP question, and Panel XVI is where this essay returns to it in earnest.

    Logical Equivalence and the Algebra of Formulas

    when two different-looking formulas are secretly making the same claim, and how to prove it without a truth table

    Logical equivalence, defined precisely

    Definition — logical equivalence

    Two formulas \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are logically equivalent, written \(\varphi\equiv\psi\), if \(v(\varphi)=v(\psi)\) for every valuation \(v\) — equivalently, if \(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi\) is a tautology (Panel VI), equivalently again, if \(\{\varphi\}\models\psi\) and \(\{\psi\}\models\varphi\) both hold (Panel III).

    Three equivalent formulations of the same fact, stated so that whichever one is most convenient in a given proof can be reached for without re-deriving the connection each time. Equivalence, unlike entailment, is symmetric by construction — and it is, in fact, an equivalence relation in the full technical sense of that phrase, which is worth actually checking rather than assuming from the name alone.

  • Reflexive: φ ≡ φv(φ)=v(φ) trivially, for every v
  • Symmetric: φ≡ψ ⟹ ψ≡φ"v(φ)=v(ψ) for all v" is symmetric in φ,ψ as written
  • Transitive: φ≡ψ and ψ≡χ ⟹ φ≡χv(φ)=v(ψ)=v(χ) for every v, chaining the two hypotheses

    • An equivalence relation partitions its domain into disjoint classes of mutually equivalent members — here, the domain is every wff in the language (infinitely many strings), and \(\equiv\) partitions them into equivalence classes, each one containing every possible syntactic dressing-up of a single underlying truth-functional meaning. Panel VIII's normal forms are, precisely, a way of picking one canonical representative out of each class.

    Two ways to establish φ ≡ ψ

    The definition licenses brute force directly: build both truth tables, check every row matches. This always works and is always finite, but the row count doubles with each additional variable — a formula in six variables already needs 64 rows checked by hand, correct but tedious and error-prone. The second method, standard in practice past three or four variables, is algebraic: chain together known equivalences — the laws of logic below — transforming \(\varphi\) into \(\psi\) directly, the same way algebra simplifies \(2(x+3)-x\) into \(x+6\) without re-deriving arithmetic at every single step.

    The substitution lemma — why chaining laws is actually valid

    Chaining laws together, as in the worked proof below, replaces a subformula deep inside a larger formula with something logically equivalent to it, and claims the whole larger formula is unaffected. That this is legitimate is not obvious from the definition of \(\equiv\) alone — the definition only directly compares two whole formulas, said nothing yet about formulas nested inside other formulas — and it is worth stating and proving as its own lemma before leaning on it.

    Lemma — substitution of equivalents

    If \(\alpha\equiv\beta\), and \(\chi\) is a formula containing \(\alpha\) as a subformula, and \(\chi'\) is obtained from \(\chi\) by replacing that occurrence of \(\alpha\) with \(\beta\), then \(\chi\equiv\chi'\).

    Proof sketch (structural induction on χ, Panel II). Base case: if \(\chi\) itself is the atom being replaced, \(\chi=\alpha\), \(\chi'=\beta\), and \(\chi\equiv\chi'\) is exactly the hypothesis \(\alpha\equiv\beta\). Inductive step: if \(\chi=\neg\chi_1\) and the replacement happens inside \(\chi_1\), the induction hypothesis gives \(\chi_1\equiv\chi_1'\), i.e. \(v(\chi_1)=v(\chi_1')\) for every \(v\); then \(v(\neg\chi_1)=v(\neg\chi_1')\) follows immediately from Panel III's clause for negation, applied to equal inputs. The four binary cases (\(\wedge,\vee,\to,\leftrightarrow\)) are identical in structure: the induction hypothesis makes one child's value provably equal in both versions, and Panel III's clause, being a genuine function of its two inputs, then guarantees equal outputs. ∎

    This lemma is what actually licenses every step of the worked algebraic proof below — each line only ever replaces one small piece of the formula using a known law, and the lemma guarantees that replacing a piece by something equivalent never changes the truth-functional meaning of the whole, no matter how deeply that piece is buried in the formula's parse tree.

    The duality principle

    Definition — logical duality

    Given a formula built only from \(\wedge\), \(\vee\), and \(\neg\), its dual \(\varphi^{d}\) is formed by swapping every \(\wedge\leftrightarrow\vee\) and every \(T\leftrightarrow F\), leaving variables and negations untouched. If \(\varphi\equiv\psi\) (both built from \(\{\wedge,\vee,\neg\}\) only), then \(\varphi^{d}\equiv\psi^{d}\).

    Look back at the laws table: Identity, Domination, Idempotent, Commutative, Associative, Distributive, De Morgan's, and Absorption are all listed in matched pairs precisely because each half of a pair is the other's dual — proving one half by truth table certifies the other half via duality, with no second truth table required. The underlying reason duality holds at all is exactly Panel III's semantic clauses for \(\wedge\) and \(\vee\): \(v(\varphi\wedge\psi)=T\) iff both are T is the mirror image, with T and F everywhere swapped, of \(v(\varphi\vee\psi)=F\) iff both are F — the two connectives are, in the precise sense duality captures, the same operation viewed through opposite ends of bivalence. This exact pairing structure resurfaces, unchanged in shape, as a fact about set complements in the companion discrete mathematics foundations essay: \(\wedge\leftrightarrow\cap\), \(\vee\leftrightarrow\cup\), \(\neg\leftrightarrow\) complement, \(T\leftrightarrow U\), \(F\leftrightarrow\varnothing\) — three appearances, in three different vocabularies, of one underlying algebraic fact.

    Normal Forms — Every Formula Has a Canonical Shape

    DNF and CNF: two standard shapes any formula can be rewritten into, built directly off its own truth table

    Literals, and the two canonical shapes

    Definition — literal, DNF, CNF

    A literal is a propositional variable or its negation (\(p\) or \(\neg p\)) — nothing else. A formula is in disjunctive normal form (DNF) if it is a disjunction of conjunctions of literals: \((\ell_{11}\wedge\cdots)\vee(\ell_{21}\wedge\cdots)\vee\cdots\). A formula is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) if it is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals: \((\ell_{11}\vee\cdots)\wedge(\ell_{21}\vee\cdots)\wedge\cdots\). Each inner disjunction in a CNF is called a clause.

    The two shapes are exact duals of one another (Panel VII) — swap every \(\wedge\) with \(\vee\) in a DNF and the result is shaped like a CNF, and vice versa — which is already a strong hint that neither shape is more fundamental than the other; the choice between them, in practice, is purely about which downstream tool consumes the result (Panel XVI's SAT solvers and Panel XII's resolution rule are built to consume CNF specifically).

    Minterms and maxterms — the fully specified building blocks

    A minterm over variables \(p_1,\dots,p_n\) is a conjunction containing every one of the \(n\) variables exactly once, each either bare or negated: for \(n=3\), \(p\wedge\neg q\wedge r\) is one of the \(2^3=8\) possible minterms. A minterm is satisfied by exactly one valuation — the one matching its pattern of bars and non-bars — and false on every other one of the \(2^n-1\) remaining valuations. A maxterm is the dual notion: a disjunction containing every variable exactly once, each bare or negated, false under exactly one valuation and true on every other.

    The existence theorem — every formula has a DNF and a CNF

    Theorem — normal form existence

    Every formula \(\varphi\) that is not a contradiction is logically equivalent to a disjunction of minterms — its canonical DNF (also called sum-of-minterms or the full disjunctive normal form). Every formula that is not a tautology is logically equivalent to a conjunction of maxterms — its canonical CNF (product-of-maxterms).

    Proof (constructive, straight from the truth table). For the DNF direction: build \(\varphi\)'s truth table. For every row where \(\varphi\) evaluates to T, form the minterm matching exactly that row — variable \(p_i\) appears bare if the row has \(v(p_i)=T\), negated if \(v(p_i)=F\). Let \(\varphi^{*}\) be the disjunction of every minterm built this way. By construction, a valuation \(v\) satisfies \(\varphi^{*}\) exactly when \(v\) matches one of the T-rows of \(\varphi\)'s table — because a minterm is satisfied by exactly the one valuation it was built from, and a disjunction of minterms is satisfied exactly when at least one disjunct is, i.e. exactly when \(v\) is one of the rows selected. That is exactly the condition for \(v(\varphi)=T\) as well, so \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi^{*}\) agree on every valuation: \(\varphi\equiv\varphi^{*}\). (If \(\varphi\) is a contradiction, no row is T, and the construction produces an empty disjunction — by convention, the constant \(F\), still correctly equivalent to \(\varphi\).) The CNF direction is the exact dual argument, building one maxterm per F-row instead. ∎

    A faster route to CNF — algebraic, not truth-table

    Building a canonical form from a truth table is guaranteed correct but scales as badly as the truth table itself. In practice, CNF is usually reached algebraically instead, by a fixed three-step recipe using only the laws from Panel VII.

    STEP 1 — eliminate → and ↔, using: p→q ≡ ¬p∨q (implication law) p↔q ≡ (¬p∨q)∧(¬q∨p) (biconditional law, implication law twice) STEP 2 — push ¬ inward until it touches only atoms, using: ¬¬p ≡ p (double negation) ¬(p∧q) ≡ ¬p∨¬q (De Morgan) ¬(p∨q) ≡ ¬p∧¬q (De Morgan) STEP 3 — distribute ∨ over ∧ until the result is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals, using: p∨(q∧r) ≡ (p∨q)∧(p∨r) (distributive law)

    Running this recipe on \(\neg(p\wedge q)\to r\): Step 1 removes \(\to\): \(\neg\neg(p\wedge q)\vee r\). Step 2 pushes \(\neg\) inward, cancelling the double negation: \((p\wedge q)\vee r\). Step 3 distributes \(\vee\) over \(\wedge\): \((p\vee r)\wedge(q\vee r)\) — already a CNF, in two clauses rather than a canonical form's much larger sum, obtained with three algebra steps and zero truth table. The dual recipe (eliminate \(\to,\leftrightarrow\); push \(\neg\) inward; distribute \(\wedge\) over \(\vee\) instead) produces a DNF the same way.

    Canonical forms are unique; general normal forms are not

    Warning — "a CNF" is not "the CNF"

    The canonical CNF of a non-tautology — one maxterm per falsifying row, every variable in every clause — is unique up to reordering clauses and literals within a clause, because it is read off directly, and only, from the truth table. An arbitrary CNF, like the two-clause result above, is emphatically not unique: \((p\vee r)\wedge(q\vee r)\) is a perfectly correct CNF of the same formula, and so, separately, is its full five-maxterm canonical expansion, and so are others besides. Finding the smallest equivalent CNF or DNF for a given formula is a genuinely hard combinatorial problem in general — Karnaugh maps and the Quine–McCluskey algorithm are the standard hand and machine techniques for small numbers of variables, and both are entire topics in their own right, outside this essay's scope.

    A small example where the size gap is dramatic

    Take \(\varphi=p\leftrightarrow q\), the biconditional itself. Its truth table is true on two of the four rows (\(p{=}q{=}T\) and \(p{=}q{=}F\)), so the canonical DNF is:

    Canonical DNF: (p∧q) ∨ (¬p∧¬q) — 2 minterms, matches Panel VII's biconditional law exactly Canonical CNF: (p∨¬q) ∧ (¬p∨q) — 2 maxterms, one per falsifying row (p≠q)

    Here the canonical and minimal forms happen to coincide — two variables is small enough that there was nowhere further to shrink. The gap becomes visible starting around four or five variables, where a canonical form can carry a dozen or more minterms while an algebraically-simplified equivalent collapses to two or three clauses; Panel XVIII's majority-voter example is a concrete case where three variables already produces a canonical DNF with four minterms next to a minimized form with only three short conjuncts — small numbers, but already a real difference in size, and the gap only widens as variable count grows.

    The same \(p\leftrightarrow q\) truth table, laid out as a Karnaugh map — the grid-based minimization tool the "Warning" box above named but didn't show — makes the minterm groupings visually obvious rather than algebraic:

    q=T q=F ┌──────┬──────┐ p=T │ T │ F │ ├──────┼──────┤ p=F │ F │ T │ └──────┴──────┘ The two T-cells sit on the diagonal, not adjacent — a Karnaugh map groups only geometrically adjacent T-cells into a single simplified term, and diagonal cells are never adjacent, which is exactly the visual signal that p↔q has no further simplification available: its minimal DNF genuinely does need both minterms kept separate.

    For formulas whose T-cells do cluster into adjacent blocks, a Karnaugh map lets a two- to four-variable minimization be read off by eye in seconds — the geometric version of exactly the algebraic law (Panel VII's absorption and distributive laws, chained) that collapsed the majority-voter's canonical DNF down to three conjuncts in Panel XVIII. It stops being practical by hand somewhere past four or five variables, which is precisely where the Quine–McCluskey algorithm's tabular, fully mechanical version of the same idea takes over.

    Why CNF specifically matters past this panel

    CNF is not just one of two symmetric options — it is the standard input format for essentially every practical piece of automated propositional reasoning built since the 1960s. Panel XII's resolution rule is stated for, and only for, pairs of CNF clauses. Panel XVI's DPLL algorithm and every modern SAT solver descended from it operate exclusively on CNF, because a clause (a disjunction of literals) is falsified by exactly one partial assignment, making "this clause is already violated, backtrack" a cheap, local check — the entire efficiency story of modern SAT solving depends on that locality, and it is only available once a formula has been converted to CNF using exactly the recipe given above.

    Functional Completeness — How Few Connectives Actually Suffice

    Panel VIII's construction, reread as a proof that three connectives are already more than enough — and that one, alone, is too

    What "enough connectives" means, made precise

    Definition — truth function, functional completeness

    An \(n\)-ary truth function is any function \(\mathbb B^n\to\mathbb B\) — equivalently, any conceivable truth table on \(n\) inputs, with no restriction on how many rows come out T. A set of connectives \(S\) is functionally complete (or adequate) if, for every \(n\) and every one of the \(2^{2^n}\) possible \(n\)-ary truth functions, some formula built using only connectives from \(S\) and \(n\) variables realizes it.

    This is a much stronger demand than it might first sound: it says \(S\) must be able to reproduce every conceivable truth table, not merely the ones with familiar names. There are already 16 distinct binary truth functions (\(2^{2^2}=16\)) and 256 distinct ternary ones — completeness of \(S\) means all of them, at every arity, without exception.

    {¬, ∧, ∨} is complete — this is exactly what Panel VIII already proved

    Panel VIII's normal form existence theorem constructs, for an arbitrary truth table, a DNF realizing it — built from nothing but \(\neg\), \(\wedge\), and \(\vee\). That construction did not assume anything about the formula it started with beyond "here is some target truth table"; it works identically starting from a bare truth table with no formula behind it at all. Read that way, the theorem already is the completeness proof for \(\{\neg,\wedge,\vee\}\): given any truth table whatsoever, the canonical-DNF recipe manufactures a formula in \(\neg,\wedge,\vee\) that realizes it, row by row, by construction.

    Trimming the set further

    Once \(\{\neg,\wedge,\vee\}\) is known complete, showing a smaller set is complete is pure algebra: define each connective being dropped in terms of the smaller set, then invoke Panel VII's substitution lemma to say any formula using the dropped connective can be systematically rewritten without it.

    Warning — {→} alone is not complete, even though it looks close

    It is tempting to think \(\to\) alone should suffice, since \(\{\neg,\to\}\) is adequate and \(\to\) seems to be doing most of the work. It is not: every formula built purely from \(\to\) and propositional variables, with no negation and no constant available, evaluates to T whenever every variable is T (a quick structural induction on the grammar confirms this — the base case is trivial, and \(\to\)'s clause outputs T whenever its consequent is T, which the induction hypothesis guarantees). That means no \(\to\)-only formula can ever realize the constant-F function, or negation, or any truth function that is false on the all-T row — an entire, permanently unreachable slice of truth-table space. Adding back either \(\neg\) or a genuine falsehood constant \(\bot\) closes the gap: \(\{\to,\bot\}\) is complete, since \(\neg p\equiv p\to\bot\) recovers negation, and Gottlob Frege's original 19th-century system, referenced in Panel I, was built on exactly this pair.

    Down to a single connective — NAND alone

    The most striking reduction goes all the way to one binary connective. Recall NAND from Panel IV: \(p\uparrow q \equiv \neg(p\wedge q)\), true on every row except both-T.

    Insight — {↑} is functionally complete, in two short equivalences

    \(\neg p \equiv p\uparrow p\) — feeding the same input to both sides of NAND negates it (both-T becomes the only excluded case, and with \(p=q\) that excluded case is exactly "\(p\) is T," so the output is T exactly when \(p\) is F). And \(p\wedge q \equiv (p\uparrow q)\uparrow(p\uparrow q)\) — NAND applied to \(p\uparrow q\) with itself negates \(p\uparrow q\), and negating a negation (Panel VII, double negation) recovers \(p\wedge q\) directly. Since \(\{\neg,\wedge\}\) is already known adequate, and both of its members are now shown expressible using \(\uparrow\) alone, \(\{\uparrow\}\) by itself is functionally complete: every one of the infinitely many truth functions at every arity is realizable using nothing but repeated NAND.

    Building ¬p, p∧q, and p∨q from NAND alone: ¬p = p ↑ p p ∧ q = (p↑q) ↑ (p↑q) p ∨ q = (p↑p) ↑ (q↑q) [De Morgan, rewritten in NAND: ¬(¬p∧¬q)] Chained as a tiny "circuit": p ──┬───[NAND]───┐ │ ├──[NAND]── p∧q q ──┴───[NAND]────┘

    The remaining two connectives from Panel IV follow the identical strategy, substituting the NAND-built pieces just derived directly into their known \(\{\neg,\wedge,\vee\}\) definitions. \(p\to q\equiv\neg p\vee q\) (Panel VII's implication law), so substituting \(\neg p=p\uparrow p\) into the \(\vee\)-from-NAND pattern above gives \(p\to q\equiv\big((p\uparrow p)\uparrow(p\uparrow p)\big)\uparrow(q\uparrow q)\) — an entirely mechanical substitution, four NAND gates deep, and correct by construction rather than by any fresh check. \(p\leftrightarrow q\equiv(p\to q)\wedge(q\to p)\) (Panel VII's biconditional law) then reuses that same \(\to\)-construction twice — once for \(p\to q\), once more with the roles swapped for \(q\to p\) — and combines the two results with one more application of the \(\wedge\)-from-NAND pattern already given. Every one of Panel IV's eight connectives, without a single exception, reduces this way to some fixed, finite, entirely mechanical chain of NAND gates — which is exactly the sense in which "\(\{\uparrow\}\) is functionally complete" is a statement about every truth function whatsoever, not merely the handful with standard names.

    NOR (\(p\downarrow q\equiv\neg(p\vee q)\)) is complete by the exact dual argument: \(\neg p\equiv p\downarrow p\), and \(p\vee q\equiv(p\downarrow q)\downarrow(p\downarrow q)\). \(\{\uparrow\}\) and \(\{\downarrow\}\) are, in fact, the only two single binary connectives that are functionally complete on their own — every other one of the sixteen binary truth functions fails to generate at least one other truth function no matter how it's chained with copies of itself.

    Note — this is not a curiosity; it is why real computer chips exist the way they do

    A logic gate is a physical device computing one truth function on electrical signals; a NAND gate is \(\uparrow\), built out of a handful of transistors. Because \(\{\uparrow\}\) alone is complete, an entire processor — every adder, every multiplexer, every register, ultimately every instruction it executes — can be fabricated by wiring together enormous numbers of one single repeated component, rather than needing five or six physically different gate designs manufactured side by side. This is a direct, load-bearing engineering consequence of the purely abstract fact proved two paragraphs up.

    Post's theorem — the complete classification, cited rather than reproved

    The question "which sets of connectives are functionally complete?" was answered completely, for every possible set, by Emil Post in 1941. Post's theorem identifies five special classes of truth functions — those preserving falsity (\(f(F,\dots,F)=F\)), those preserving truth (\(f(T,\dots,T)=T\)), the monotone functions (never producing T from F by only strengthening inputs), the self-dual functions (\(f\) and its dual, Panel VII, always agree), and the affine (linear, XOR-and-constants-only) functions — and proves a set of connectives \(S\) is functionally complete if and only if \(S\) is not entirely contained inside any one of those five classes.

    Proof Theory I — Axiomatic (Hilbert-Style) Systems

    deriving truths by pure, mechanical symbol manipulation — never once consulting a truth table

    A second, completely independent notion of "follows from"

    Everything since Panel III has been semantic: \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) is defined in terms of valuations, models, and truth tables — checking it means, ultimately, exhaustively considering cases. Proof theory asks a different question with a superficially similar answer: is there a finite chain of purely mechanical rewriting steps — no truth values consulted anywhere — that gets from \(\Gamma\) to \(\varphi\)? This section builds the oldest style of answer to that question, the axiomatic (or Hilbert-style) proof system: a short list of formula patterns declared, by fiat, to be starting points, plus a single rule for combining them.

    The system — three axiom schemas and one rule

    Because Panel IX proved \(\{\neg,\to\}\) functionally complete, the entire system can be stated using only those two connectives — \(\wedge\), \(\vee\), and \(\leftrightarrow\) are simply notational abbreviations for the \(\{\neg,\to\}\) formulas Panel IX exhibited, and every result proved here transfers to them automatically via Panel VII's substitution lemma. This is the system published by Jan Łukasiewicz, essentially the streamlined descendant of Frege's original 1879 system.

This is the only rule of inference in the entire system — every other move available in a Hilbert-style proof is simply substituting concrete formulas into one of the three axiom schemas above (producing an axiom instance), which requires no justification beyond "this matches the pattern."

    The deduction theorem — recovering conditional proof

    The derivation above is unpleasant partly because there is no built-in way to temporarily assume something and reason from it — every line has to already be an axiom, a premise, or an MP consequence of earlier lines. The deduction theorem proves that this limitation is only apparent: reasoning "assume \(\varphi\), derive \(\psi\), conclude \(\varphi\to\psi\)" is always recoverable inside the system, even though the system's official rules never mention temporary assumptions at all.

    Theorem — the deduction theorem

    \(\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi\) if and only if \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi\).

    Proof. (⟸) is immediate: given \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi\), the same derivation is still valid from \(\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\) (a derivation from a smaller premise set remains a derivation from any larger superset), and appending \(\varphi\) (now legitimately a premise) followed by one application of MP yields \(\psi\).

    (⟹) is the substantial direction. Suppose \(\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi\) via a derivation \(\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n=\psi\). Prove, by induction on \(i\), that \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_i\) for every line in the derivation:

    • \(\psi_i\) is an axiom instance or a member of \(\Gamma\): then \(\Gamma\vdash\psi_i\) outright (it needs no help from \(\varphi\)), and \(\Gamma\vdash\psi_i\to(\varphi\to\psi_i)\) is itself an instance of A1; MP gives \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_i\).
    • \(\psi_i\) is \(\varphi\) itself: then \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_i\) is exactly \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\varphi\), already established above (a fixed theorem, provable from no premises at all, hence certainly from \(\Gamma\)).
    • \(\psi_i\) follows by MP from earlier \(\psi_j\) and \(\psi_k=(\psi_j\to\psi_i)\): the induction hypothesis gives both \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_j\) and \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to(\psi_j\to\psi_i)\). A2 supplies \(\Gamma\vdash\big(\varphi\to(\psi_j\to\psi_i)\big)\to\big((\varphi\to\psi_j)\to(\varphi\to\psi_i)\big)\); two applications of MP then give \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_i\).

    Every line of the original derivation is covered by one of these three cases, so by induction \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi_i\) holds all the way through \(i=n\), where \(\psi_n=\psi\) — giving \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi\) exactly as claimed. ∎

    Insight — this is the formal license behind Panel XIV's "conditional proof"

    Every mathematics student is taught, usually with no justification beyond "this is how you prove an if-then statement," to prove \(\varphi\to\psi\) by assuming \(\varphi\) and deriving \(\psi\). The deduction theorem is the reason that method is actually valid rather than merely traditional: it is a theorem about the axiomatic system, proved from nothing but A1, A2, and MP, guaranteeing that any informal "assume \(\varphi\)… therefore \(\psi\)… hence \(\varphi\to\psi\)" argument can always, in principle, be mechanically unwound into an assumption-free derivation of \(\varphi\to\psi\) in the style used to prove \(\varphi\to\varphi\) above. Panel XI's natural deduction system builds this license directly into its rules, rather than deriving it as a theorem about a more primitive system — which is exactly why it feels so much closer to how mathematicians actually write proofs.

    The deduction theorem in action — deriving HS as a theorem of the axiomatic system

    With the deduction theorem available, a second worked derivation becomes almost effortless — proving hypothetical syllogism, Panel XII's HS rule, holds in the Hilbert system as well as in natural deduction, without touching A1, A2, or A3 directly at all.

    Proof Theory II — Natural Deduction

    the same provability relation, rebuilt around explicit assumption and discharge — the system that actually resembles how proofs get written

    Why a second proof system at all

    Gerhard Gentzen introduced natural deduction in 1934, motivated by exactly the discomfort Panel X's five-line proof of \(\varphi\to\varphi\) is meant to produce: Hilbert-style systems are minimal and easy to prove things about, but painful to prove things in. Natural deduction trades the previous system's three axiom schemas for a larger set of rules — typically one introduction rule and one elimination rule per connective — built around a genuinely new primitive move: temporarily assuming a formula, reasoning inside that assumption, and then discharging it, closing off the assumption and drawing a conclusion that no longer depends on it. This is not a new logical idea layered on top of Panel X's system — Panel XV notes, and Panel X's deduction theorem already makes plausible, that both systems prove exactly the same theorems. It is the same relation \(\vdash\), presented in a shape that matches how the deduction theorem shows proofs could always have been organized.

    The rules, connective by connective

    Each rule is named for its connective and its direction: introduction rules show how to derive a formula with that connective as its main connective (Panel II); elimination rules show what can be extracted once such a formula is already available. \(\bot\) (falsum, "absurdity") is used as a stand-in for "a contradiction has been reached" — concretely, having both some \(\chi\) and \(\neg\chi\) available.

    Warning — DNE (and A3 from Panel X) is where "classical" logic makes its one genuinely optional commitment

    Every rule above except double negation elimination is accepted by intuitionistic logic as well as classical logic — a rival foundational system that refuses to accept a proof of \(\varphi\) unless it constructs one, and specifically rejects "assume \(\varphi\) is false, derive absurdity, conclude \(\varphi\)" as a way of establishing \(\varphi\) itself (only \(\neg\varphi\) via ¬I, never plain \(\varphi\) via DNE). This is not a pedantic footnote: the law of the excluded middle, \(p\vee\neg p\), which Panel VI simply declared a tautology by truth table, turns out to require DNE to derive syntactically — the worked proof below uses it in the very last line — and is not a theorem of intuitionistic natural deduction (drop DNE from the rule list, and \(p\vee\neg p\) becomes underivable, even though every other rule stays available). Classical logic — the system this entire essay is built in, matching Panel III's bivalence assumption — is exactly intuitionistic logic plus DNE (equivalently, plus Panel X's A3, plus LEM itself as a free-standing axiom — all three additions are provably interchangeable). This essay commits to classical logic throughout because Panel III committed to bivalence in the very first paragraph of semantics; the intuitionistic alternative exists and is a serious, actively studied system, but it is a genuinely different logic, not a stricter dialect of this one.

    Reading a Fitch-style derivation

    The standard notation for a natural deduction proof — Fitch notation — draws each temporarily-assumed formula with a vertical bar marking the scope of its assumption; everything inside the bar may use it, and the bar closes (the assumption is discharged) at the exact line the corresponding introduction rule fires.

    Natural deduction and the axiomatic system prove the same things

    Insight — two systems, one relation ⊢

    Panel X's Hilbert-style system and this panel's natural deduction system are provably equivalent: \(\Gamma\vdash_{\text{Hilbert}}\varphi\) if and only if \(\Gamma\vdash_{\text{ND}}\varphi\), for every \(\Gamma\) and \(\varphi\). One direction is exactly what Panel X's deduction theorem proof pattern generalizes into: every natural deduction rule can be simulated by a fixed short combination of A1, A2, A3, and MP (→I already is the deduction theorem's method, restated as a primitive rule instead of a derived theorem). The other direction goes by translating each axiom instance into a short natural deduction derivation and noting MP is already →E verbatim. Because both systems compute the identical relation \(\vdash\), every result from here forward — Panel XII's rules of inference, Panel XIV's proof techniques, Panel XV's soundness and completeness theorems — can be, and in textbooks routinely is, stated for either system interchangeably. This essay uses natural deduction from this point on purely for readability, with the Panel X system standing behind it as the leaner, more easily analyzed foundation the metatheory of Panel XV is actually proved about.

    The Standard Rules of Inference

    a curated catalogue of named, reusable argument forms — each one a theorem about ⊢, invoked in a single step rather than re-derived every time

    What a "rule of inference" actually is

    Every rule catalogued in this panel is, formally, nothing more than a claim of the shape \(\{\Gamma\}\vdash\varphi\) for some fixed schematic \(\Gamma\) and \(\varphi\) — a theorem about the proof system built in Panels X and XI, provable once, in full, using nothing but axioms (or primitive natural deduction rules) and modus ponens. Naming it and adding it to a reference catalogue is exactly the same move as naming a lemma in ordinary mathematics: instead of re-deriving hypothetical syllogism from A1–A3 and MP every single time an argument needs it (Panel VII did that once, at real length), an argument can now simply cite "HS" and move on, with the citation backed by a proof that was done, in full rigor, exactly one time. This is precisely how ordinary mathematical proof-writing actually behaves — a proof rarely descends to primitive axioms; it cites theorems, each one independently justified elsewhere — and this panel is the propositional-logic version of that standard mathematical practice.

    Four that are already primitive

    Four entries in the table need essentially no derivation at all, because Panel XI's natural deduction rules already state them directly: MP is exactly →E; Add is exactly ∨I; Simp is exactly ∧E; Conj is exactly ∧I. Cataloguing them by their traditional Latin-derived names is purely a matter of matching the vocabulary used in most textbooks and in ordinary mathematical writing, where "by addition" or "by simplification" are common citations.

    Modus Tollens — denying the consequent to deny the antecedent

    MT is the "backwards" partner of MP: instead of affirming the antecedent to get the consequent, it denies the consequent to get the antecedent's denial.

    1 │ p assumption (for ¬I) │ 2 │ q →E, (p→q, premise), 1 │ 3 │ ⊥ ¬E, (¬q, premise), 2 │ 4 ¬p ¬I, discharging 1–3

    "If the sprinklers ran, the lawn is wet; the lawn is not wet; therefore the sprinklers did not run" is MT in plain English — and it is worth contrasting immediately with the invalid pattern "the lawn is wet, therefore the sprinklers ran," which affirms the consequent rather than denying it and is formalized as a named fallacy in Panel XIII.

    Disjunctive Syllogism — eliminating a disjunct by denying it

    1 │ p assumption (∨E on p∨q, case p) │ 2 │ ⊥ ¬E, (¬p, premise), 1 │ 3 │ q ⊥E (ex falso), 2 │ 4 │ q assumption (∨E on p∨q, case q — trivial) │ 5 q ∨E, (p∨q, premise), discharging 1–3 and 4–4

    "The keys are in my coat or on the table; they're not in my coat; so they're on the table." Panel XIII's affirming a disjunct fallacy is the tempting but invalid near-relative of this rule — it tries to eliminate a disjunct by affirming the other one instead of denying it, which fails precisely because \(\vee\) is inclusive (Panel IV).

    Constructive Dilemma — case analysis across two conditionals at once

    1 │ (p→q) ∧ (r→s) premise │ 2 │ p ∨ r premise │ 3 │ p→q ∧E, 1 │ 4 │ r→s ∧E, 1 │ 5 │ │ p assumption (∨E on 2, case p) │ │ 6 │ │ q →E, 3, 5 │ │ 7 │ │ q ∨ s ∨I, 6 │ 8 │ │ r assumption (∨E on 2, case r) │ │ 9 │ │ q ∨ s ∨I, →E(4, 8) │ 10 q ∨ s ∨E, 2, discharging 5–7 and 8–9

    Destructive Dilemma is not an independent construction — it drops straight out of CD once Panel VII's contrapositive law is applied to both conditionals first: \(p\to q\) and \(r\to s\) contrapose to \(\neg q\to\neg p\) and \(\neg s\to\neg r\); feeding those two conditionals, plus the premise \(\neg q\vee\neg s\), into CD directly yields \(\neg p\vee\neg r\) — DD is simply CD run on the contrapositives, not a sixth primitive idea.

    In English: "If it rains, the game moves indoors; if it's too windy, the game is cancelled outright. Either it will rain or it will be too windy. So either the game moves indoors or it's cancelled outright" is CD directly, with \(p=\)"it rains," \(q=\)"game moves indoors," \(r=\)"too windy," \(s=\)"game cancelled" — two independent conditionals, a disjunction of their antecedents as a premise, and a disjunction of their consequents as the guaranteed conclusion, exactly the shape the derivation above proves valid for any four propositions whatsoever.

    Formal Fallacies — Invalid Forms That Look Valid

    Panel XII catalogued the argument shapes guaranteed to preserve truth; this panel catalogues their near-miss cousins that aren't

    What makes a fallacy "formal"

    A formal fallacy is an argument form that is invalid (Panel I) purely because of its structure — no fact about the world is needed to refute it, only a single counterexample valuation making every premise true and the conclusion false, exactly as Panel VI's satisfiability machinery is built to exhibit. This is a narrower, more mechanical target than the everyday sense of "fallacy" — informal fallacies like circular reasoning or appeals to authority are failures of content or of unstated assumptions, not failures of form, and they sit outside what a formal system like this one can certify or refute at all. Every fallacy below is refuted the same mechanical way every rule in Panel XII was verified: by truth table, needing nothing else.

    Definition — the counterexample method

    To show an argument form \(\Gamma\nvdash\varphi\) (equivalently, \(\Gamma\not\models\varphi\) by Panel XV's soundness and completeness), it suffices to exhibit one valuation making every member of \(\Gamma\) true and \(\varphi\) false. This is the exact dual of Panel VI's satisfiability-based proof method: proving validity searches for a guarantee across every row; refuting validity only ever needs to produce one bad row.

    Proof Techniques — the Everyday Toolkit, Derived From Scratch

    every standard method of mathematical proof, traced back to the exact rule or law of this essay that actually licenses it

    Direct proof

    To prove \(p\to q\): assume \(p\), reason forward, arrive at \(q\), conclude \(p\to q\). This is not a separate technique invented by textbooks — it is exactly Panel XI's \(\to\)I rule (equivalently, Panel X's deduction theorem), used with mathematical content substituted in for the schematic \(\varphi,\psi\).

    Worked example — if n is even, then n² is even

    Claim: for every integer \(n\), if \(n\) is even then \(n^2\) is even. Proof. Assume \(n\) is even. By definition, \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\). Then \(n^2=(2k)^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)\), which is \(2\) times the integer \(2k^2\) — so \(n^2\) is even. Since assuming \(n\) even led to \(n^2\) even, \(n\text{ even}\to n^2\text{ even}\). ∎ — the propositional skeleton is exactly →I: assume \(p\) ("n is even"), derive \(q\) ("n² is even"), discharge to \(p\to q\).

    Proof by contraposition

    To prove \(p\to q\): prove \(\neg q\to\neg p\) instead, and invoke Panel VII's contrapositive law (\(p\to q\equiv\neg q\to\neg p\)) to conclude the original. This technique earns its keep exactly when \(\neg q\) gives more concrete, workable information than \(p\) does.

    Worked example — if n² is even, then n is even

    Claim: for every integer \(n\), if \(n^2\) is even then \(n\) is even. A direct proof stalls immediately — "\(n^2\) is even" gives no obvious algebraic handle on \(n\) itself. Proof, by contraposition. Assume \(n\) is not even, i.e. \(n\) is odd: \(n=2k+1\) for some integer \(k\). Then \(n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1\), which is odd. So \(\neg(n\text{ even})\to\neg(n^2\text{ even})\) — and by the contrapositive law, \(n^2\text{ even}\to n\text{ even}\), exactly the original claim. ∎

    Proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum)

    To prove \(p\): assume \(\neg p\), derive \(\bot\), and — as Panel XI's own derivation of the law of the excluded middle showed in full — this technique needs the classical DNE rule specifically: \(\neg\)I alone only licenses concluding \(\neg\neg p\) from a contradiction under \(\neg p\); one further application of DNE is what actually recovers \(p\) itself.

    Worked example — √2 is irrational

    Claim: \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. Proof. Assume, toward contradiction, that \(\sqrt2\) is rational: \(\sqrt2=a/b\) for integers \(a,b\) with \(b\neq0\) and \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) (the fraction in lowest terms — every rational number admits such a representation). Squaring, \(2=a^2/b^2\), so \(a^2=2b^2\), so \(a^2\) is even, so — by the immediately preceding worked example's contrapositive-proved theorem — \(a\) itself is even: \(a=2m\) for some integer \(m\). Substituting, \((2m)^2=2b^2\), so \(4m^2=2b^2\), so \(b^2=2m^2\), so \(b^2\) is even, so \(b\) is even too. But then both \(a\) and \(b\) are even, contradicting \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). The assumption that \(\sqrt2\) is rational leads to a contradiction; therefore \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. ∎ — note how the proof leans on the previous panel's contraposed theorem as a cited lemma, exactly the "cite, don't re-derive" discipline Panel XII's rule catalogue was built around.

    Proof by cases

    To prove \(\chi\) from a premise known to be \(p\vee q\) (or, more commonly, from an exhaustive case split licensed by the law of the excluded middle itself): prove \(p\to\chi\) separately, prove \(q\to\chi\) separately, and combine with Panel XI's \(\vee\)E rule. The exhaustiveness of "every integer is even or odd" is not a fact about integers alone — it is an instance of \(p\vee\neg p\), the very formula Panel XI spent a full reductio deriving from first principles, now doing quiet load-bearing work in an entirely different, concrete proof.

    Worked example — n² + n is even, for every integer n

    Claim: for every integer \(n\), \(n^2+n\) is even. Proof, by cases on parity (licensed by excluded middle: every integer is even or odd, with no third option). Case 1 — n is even: \(n=2k\), so \(n^2+n=4k^2+2k=2(2k^2+k)\), even. Case 2 — n is odd: \(n=2k+1\), so \(n^2+n=(2k+1)^2+(2k+1)=4k^2+4k+1+2k+1=4k^2+6k+2=2(2k^2+3k+1)\), even. Both cases give an even result, and the cases are exhaustive, so \(n^2+n\) is even for every integer \(n\). ∎

    Biconditional proof — two directions, cited from Panel VII

    To prove \(p\leftrightarrow q\): prove \(p\to q\) and \(q\to p\) as two entirely separate arguments (each by whatever technique above suits it), then invoke the biconditional law to conjoin them. This is not a shortcut around genuine work — it is, provably by Panel VII, exactly as much work as the claim demands, since \(p\leftrightarrow q\) genuinely does encode two independent directional facts, and Panel IV's evaluation clause for \(\leftrightarrow\) requires both to hold simultaneously before the whole biconditional is true.

    Soundness, Completeness, and Compactness

    the theorems proving that Panel I's two roads — provable, and true in every case — were, all along, the same road

    Two theorems, stated side by side

    Panel I promised that the syntactic relation \(\vdash\) (Panels X, XI) and the semantic relation \(\models\) (Panel III) would turn out to coincide. That promise is two separate theorems, proved by genuinely different methods, and propositional logic is the setting where both are not just true but provable in full, with nothing swept under the rug.

    Soundness

    \(\Gamma\vdash\varphi \implies \Gamma\models\varphi\)

    Every provable formula really is true in every model of its premises. A proof system that failed soundness would be worthless — it could "prove" things that are simply false.

    Completeness

    \(\Gamma\models\varphi \implies \Gamma\vdash\varphi\)

    Every semantically valid consequence has a syntactic proof. A proof system that failed completeness would be too weak — some genuine truths would be permanently out of its reach.

    Soundness — provability never outruns truth

    Soundness is proved by structural induction on the length of a derivation, using Panel X's axiomatic system, where the argument is shortest.

    Proof sketch — soundness

    Two facts, each independently checkable, do the entire job. (1) Every axiom instance is a tautology. Take A1, \(\varphi\to(\psi\to\varphi)\): if \(v(\varphi)=F\) the whole conditional is vacuously true (Panel IV); if \(v(\varphi)=T\), then \(\psi\to\varphi\) has a true consequent, so it too is true, making the outer conditional true again. Every row is covered — A1 is a tautology, and A2 and A3 submit to the identical style of case check. (2) MP preserves semantic entailment: if \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) and \(\Gamma\models\varphi\to\psi\), then for any valuation \(v\) satisfying \(\Gamma\), \(v(\varphi)=T\) and \(v(\varphi\to\psi)=T\), which by Panel III's clause for \(\to\) forces \(v(\psi)=T\) as well — so \(\Gamma\models\psi\). Now induct on the position \(i\) of each line \(\psi_i\) in a derivation of \(\varphi\) from \(\Gamma\): if \(\psi_i\) is an axiom instance, \(\Gamma\models\psi_i\) by (1) (a tautology is entailed by anything); if \(\psi_i\in\Gamma\), \(\Gamma\models\psi_i\) trivially; if \(\psi_i\) follows by MP from two earlier lines, \(\Gamma\models\psi_i\) by (2) and the induction hypothesis on those two earlier lines. Every line is covered, so in particular the last line, \(\varphi\) itself, satisfies \(\Gamma\models\varphi\). ∎

    Completeness — truth never outruns provability

    Completeness is the harder direction, and the classical route for propositional logic specifically — due, in essence, to Emil Post (1921) and cleaned up by László Kalmár (1935) — is more constructive and more elementary than the Henkin-style argument first-order logic needs: it works by literally building a derivation directly out of a formula's own truth table, one row at a time.

    Kalmár's Lemma

    Let \(\varphi\) be a formula in variables \(p_1,\dots,p_n\), and let \(v\) be any valuation. For each \(i\), write \(p_i^{v}\) for the literal \(p_i\) if \(v(p_i)=T\) and \(\neg p_i\) if \(v(p_i)=F\); write \(\varphi^{v}\) for \(\varphi\) itself if \(v(\varphi)=T\) and \(\neg\varphi\) if \(v(\varphi)=F\). Then: \(p_1^{v},\dots,p_n^{v}\;\vdash\;\varphi^{v}\).

    In words: fixing every atom's truth value exactly as \(v\) says, and asserting each one as a labeled premise (\(p_i\) if \(v\) makes it true, \(\neg p_i\) if \(v\) makes it false), is already enough to formally derive \(\varphi\) itself if \(v\) happens to make \(\varphi\) true, or to formally derive \(\neg\varphi\) if \(v\) makes \(\varphi\) false — with no truth table lookup involved in the derivation itself, only in choosing which of \(\varphi,\neg\varphi\) to target. The proof is a structural induction on \(\varphi\) (Panel II): the base case, \(\varphi=p_i\), is immediate (the premise \(p_i^v\) already is the needed conclusion \(\varphi^v\)); the case \(\varphi=\psi_1\to\psi_2\) splits on \(v(\psi_1)\) and \(v(\psi_2)\) and, in each of the four combinations, either the induction hypothesis on \(\psi_1\) or on \(\psi_2\) directly hands over a proof of \(\varphi^v\) using nothing more than Panel X's A1 and MP (for instance, if \(v(\psi_1)=F\), the induction hypothesis gives \(\vdash\neg\psi_1\) from the premises, and \(\neg\psi_1\vdash\psi_1\to\psi_2\) is a short standing lemma about \(\to\), giving \(\varphi^v=\psi_1\to\psi_2\) directly); the other connectives follow the same pattern.

    From the lemma to full completeness, for tautologies

    Suppose \(\varphi\) is a tautology: \(v(\varphi)=T\) for every valuation \(v\), so Kalmár's Lemma gives \(p_1^v,\dots,p_n^v\vdash\varphi\) for every single one of the \(2^n\) valuations — in particular, it holds no matter what is assumed about \(p_n\) specifically, with \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v\) fixed. Take the two valuations agreeing on \(p_1,\dots,p_{n-1}\) but disagreeing on \(p_n\): both \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v,p_n\vdash\varphi\) and \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v,\neg p_n\vdash\varphi\) hold. By the deduction theorem (Panel X), \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v\vdash p_n\to\varphi\) and \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v\vdash\neg p_n\to\varphi\); combined with \(\vdash p_n\vee\neg p_n\) (Panel XI's own derivation of excluded middle) and Panel XIV's proof-by-cases pattern (∨-elimination), this yields \(p_1^v,\dots,p_{n-1}^v\vdash\varphi\) — one fewer premise needed than before, with \(p_n\) discharged entirely. Repeating this discharge step \(n\) times, once per variable, eventually removes every premise: \(\vdash\varphi\). ∎

    Insight — completeness is why "just check the truth table" was never a lesser method

    Kalmár's proof does not invoke any exotic machinery — every step is Panel X's deduction theorem, Panel XI's excluded-middle derivation, and Panel XIV's case-splitting, chained together mechanically once per row of the truth table. This is the precise sense in which building a truth table and building a formal derivation were secretly doing the same underlying work all along: completeness guarantees a derivation always exists whenever the table says "tautology" on every row, and the proof above is, quite literally, a recipe for building that derivation directly out of the table's rows, one row-elimination step at a time.

    Consistency, and satisfiability rejoin each other

    Definition — consistency

    A set of formulas \(\Gamma\) is (syntactically) consistent if \(\Gamma\nvdash\bot\) — no contradiction is derivable from it — and inconsistent otherwise.

    Soundness and completeness together force consistency and satisfiability to be the same property, seen from the two different sides of \(\vdash\) and \(\models\): if \(\Gamma\) is inconsistent, \(\Gamma\vdash\bot\), so by soundness \(\Gamma\models\bot\), meaning no valuation satisfies \(\Gamma\) — \(\Gamma\) is unsatisfiable. Conversely, if \(\Gamma\) is unsatisfiable, then (Panel VI's refutation lemma, with \(\varphi=\bot\)) \(\Gamma\models\bot\), so by completeness \(\Gamma\vdash\bot\) — \(\Gamma\) is inconsistent. Consistent and satisfiable are, for propositional logic, provably the exact same property, and the next theorem is the reason that coincidence is worth stating explicitly rather than treating as an obvious synonym-swap.

    Compactness — an infinite question, settled by finite proofs

    Theorem — compactness

    A (possibly infinite) set of formulas \(\Gamma\) is satisfiable if and only if every finite subset of \(\Gamma\) is satisfiable.

    Proof. One direction is immediate: if \(\Gamma\) itself has a model \(v\), that same \(v\) satisfies every subset of \(\Gamma\), finite or not. The substantial direction runs through the consistency/satisfiability coincidence just established. Suppose \(\Gamma\) is unsatisfiable; by that coincidence, \(\Gamma\) is inconsistent, meaning \(\Gamma\vdash\bot\) — and a derivation, by Panel X's own definition, is a finite sequence of formulas, so it cites only finitely many members of \(\Gamma\) as premises, however large \(\Gamma\) itself is. Let \(\Gamma_0\subseteq\Gamma\) be that finite set of cited premises; the very same derivation already witnesses \(\Gamma_0\vdash\bot\), so \(\Gamma_0\) is inconsistent, hence (the coincidence again) unsatisfiable. So: if \(\Gamma\) is unsatisfiable, some finite subset \(\Gamma_0\) is already unsatisfiable — the contrapositive of exactly the claim. ∎

    A concrete instance of the contrapositive direction, over infinitely many variables \(p_1,p_2,p_3,\dots\): let \(\Gamma=\{p_1\}\cup\{\neg p_5\}\cup\{p_n\to p_{n+1}: n=1,2,3,\dots\}\) — infinitely many formulas (one implication for every natural number \(n\)), asserting \(p_1\), denying \(p_5\), and chaining every \(p_n\) to the next. \(\Gamma\) is unsatisfiable: \(p_1\) together with \(p_1\to p_2\), \(p_2\to p_3\), \(p_3\to p_4\), \(p_4\to p_5\) (four applications of MP, Panel XII) forces \(p_5\), directly contradicting \(\neg p_5\). Compactness guarantees a finite subset already carries this contradiction, and indeed one does — exactly the six formulas actually cited: \(\{p_1,\ p_1\to p_2,\ p_2\to p_3,\ p_3\to p_4,\ p_4\to p_5,\ \neg p_5\}\subseteq\Gamma\) is already unsatisfiable on its own, with every other one of the infinitely many other implications in \(\Gamma\) (\(p_5\to p_6\), \(p_6\to p_7\), …) playing no role in the contradiction at all.

    Note — why this feels almost too easy, and why that's fine

    Compactness is a genuinely deep theorem in first-order logic, where it is usually proved with heavier machinery (ultraproducts, or a careful Henkin construction) because first-order derivations are more intricate objects. The propositional proof above looks almost trivial by comparison, and it is — but not because the underlying fact is shallow; it is because propositional derivations are already guaranteed finite by Panel X's own definition, so "only finitely many premises get used" was never in doubt in the first place. Compactness for propositional logic is real evidence for how much simpler, and how much more completely closed, the propositional case is compared to everything built on top of it.

    Decidability, SAT, and the Limits of Mechanical Reasoning

    every question this essay has asked is answerable by an algorithm — the remaining question is how expensive that algorithm has to be

    Propositional logic is decidable

    Definition — decidability

    A problem is decidable if there exists an algorithm that, given any instance, always terminates in finite time with the correct yes/no answer.

    Panel III's truth table method already is such an algorithm for every question this essay has posed about a single formula: is \(\varphi\) a tautology (Panel VI)? satisfiable? does \(\Gamma\models\varphi\) (Panel VI's refutation lemma reduces this to unsatisfiability of a single formula)? Build the finite table, \(2^n\) rows for \(n\) variables, read the answer off the final column — the procedure always terminates (finitely many rows) and is always correct (Panel III's semantics is exactly what the table computes). Propositional logic is decidable, full stop, and this is precisely the fact Panel I flagged as the thing predicate logic permanently loses when quantifiers are added (a consequence of the Church–Turing undecidability results of 1936, entirely outside this essay's scope).

    SAT — the same question, asked about cost instead of possibility

    Definition — SAT

    SAT (the Boolean satisfiability problem): given a formula \(\varphi\) in CNF (Panel VIII), determine whether \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

    Decidability settles whether an algorithm exists; it says nothing about how long that algorithm has to run. The truth table method needs \(2^n\) rows for \(n\) variables — for \(n=100\), a formula smaller than routine industrial verification problems, that is more rows than there are atoms in the observable universe. Whether SAT admits some fundamentally faster algorithm — one running in time polynomial in \(n\), rather than exponential — is one of the most consequential open questions in all of mathematics and computer science.

    P versus NP, in one line each
    ClassDefinition
    Pproblems solvable by an algorithm running in time polynomial in the input size
    NPproblems whose proposed solutions can be verified in polynomial time, even if finding one might take far longer
    co-NPproblems whose negative answers have polynomial-time-verifiable certificates — TAUTOLOGY (Panel VI's own question, "is φ true on every valuation?") is co-NP-complete, the exact mirror of SAT
    PSPACEproblems solvable using an amount of memory polynomial in the input size, with no time limit at all — contains both P and NP, and is generally believed to be strictly larger than either

    SAT is obviously in NP: given a claimed satisfying assignment, checking it (Panel III's evaluation procedure) takes time linear in the formula's size — verification is cheap, even though the truth table method for finding an assignment (or confirming none exists) is exponential. Whether SAT — or, equivalently by the theorem below, every problem in NP — is also in P is the P versus NP question, one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems, open since it was first posed in essentially this form in the early 1970s, carrying a one-million-dollar prize for a correct resolution either way.

    Theorem — the Cook–Levin theorem (1971)

    SAT is NP-complete: it is in NP, and every other problem in NP can be transformed into a SAT instance in polynomial time. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm for SAT would immediately yield a polynomial-time algorithm for every problem in NP — proving P = NP outright — and, conversely, proving no such algorithm for SAT can exist would prove P ≠ NP.

    NP-complete
    P vs NP: open

    Stephen Cook and, independently, Leonid Levin proved this in 1971, making SAT the very first problem ever shown NP-complete — every other NP-complete problem known today is, in the end, provably exactly as hard as SAT, and the standard method for proving a new problem NP-complete is still, five decades later, to reduce SAT (or another already-known NP-complete problem) into it. A first-principles essay on propositional logic runs, without ever intending to, straight into the doorway of one of the deepest open questions in mathematics.

The Cook–Levin theorem, and the decades of further reductions it kicked off, is precisely the discovery that this surface dissimilarity is misleading: each of these problems can be systematically translated into a SAT instance (a set of variables encoding, say, "vertex v gets color c," and a set of clauses encoding the constraints), solvable or unsolvable in exact lockstep with the original. Propositional logic, built in this essay from nothing but an alphabet and five grammar clauses, turns out to be an equally valid encoding target for graph coloring, scheduling, and package-weight optimization — a genuinely surprising amount of unrelated-looking mileage out of Panel II's opening definitions.

    DPLL — search that is smarter than a truth table, still exponential in the worst case

    Nobody actually builds a full \(2^n\)-row truth table to test satisfiability in practice. The standard algorithm, due to Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam, George Logemann, and Donald Loveland (1962), searches the space of partial assignments directly, pruning branches the moment they provably cannot succeed.

    DPLL(clause set Δ): if Δ is empty → SATISFIABLE if Δ contains the empty clause → UNSATISFIABLE UNIT PROPAGATION: if some clause has exactly one unassigned literal ℓ (a "unit clause"), set ℓ := T, simplify every clause accordingly, repeat PURE LITERAL ELIMINATION: if some variable appears with only one polarity across all of Δ (never negated, or never bare), set it to satisfy every clause it's in BRANCH: otherwise, pick some unassigned variable p; recursively try DPLL(Δ with p := T); if that fails, recursively try DPLL(Δ with p := F)

    Unit propagation and pure literal elimination are exactly Panel XII's disjunctive syllogism and its close relatives, applied automatically at industrial scale; the branch step is where the exponential worst case still lives — DPLL is faster in practice than brute-force enumeration by an enormous margin on real-world instances, but no known variant of it (nor any other known algorithm) avoids exponential blowup on adversarially constructed formulas, exactly as the open P vs NP question predicts would have to be the case if P ≠ NP.

    A trace, run by hand

    Let \(\Delta_0=\{p,q\},\{\neg p,r\},\{\neg q,\neg r,s\},\{\neg s\}\).

    Δ₀ = {p,q} {¬p,r} {¬q,¬r,s} {¬s} UNIT PROPAGATE ¬s (only literal in {¬s}): s := F {¬q,¬r,s} loses its now-false literal s → {¬q,¬r} Δ₁ = {p,q} {¬p,r} {¬q,¬r} no remaining unit clause; no pure literal (p, q, and r each appear both bare and negated across Δ₁) — must BRANCH BRANCH: try p := T {p,q} is now satisfied, drop it {¬p,r} loses its false literal ¬p → {r} Δ₂ = {r} {¬q,¬r} UNIT PROPAGATE r (only literal in {r}): r := T {¬q,¬r} loses its now-false literal ¬r → {¬q} Δ₃ = {¬q} UNIT PROPAGATE ¬q (only literal in {¬q}): q := F Δ₄ = { } ← empty clause set: SATISFIABLE Model found: p=T, q=F, r=T, s=F

    Checking that model against the original four clauses directly: \(\{p,q\}\) is satisfied by \(p\); \(\{\neg p,r\}\) is satisfied by \(r\); \(\{\neg q,\neg r,s\}\) is satisfied by \(\neg q\); \(\{\neg s\}\) is satisfied outright. One branch, three unit propagations, and the search never needed to backtrack — the typical case on real instances, even though nothing in the algorithm's definition rules out needing to explore both sides of many branches on a harder input.

    Resolution refutation — Panel XII's rule, run to exhaustion

    Theorem — resolution is refutation-complete

    A set of CNF clauses is unsatisfiable if and only if the empty clause can be derived from it by repeated application of Panel XII's resolution rule alone.

    The empty clause is, by convention, unsatisfiable by definition — an empty disjunction has nothing that could make it true, dual to Panel VIII's note that an empty conjunction (canonical DNF of a contradiction) defaults to \(F\). Resolution refutation is, in essence, DPLL viewed as a proof system rather than a search procedure: every branch DPLL closes off as a dead end corresponds to a resolution step combining two clauses down to a shorter one, and modern industrial SAT solvers — built on CDCL, conflict-driven clause learning, a direct descendant of DPLL — are, under the hood, resolution theorem provers, routinely handling formulas with millions of variables drawn from hardware verification, software model checking, and cryptanalysis, despite the Cook–Levin theorem guaranteeing no worst-case shortcut can exist for all of them at once.

    Note — where this essay's mechanical thread ends

    Panel III promised that every semantic question about a single formula was, in principle, mechanically decidable; this panel has made good on that promise twice over — once trivially, via the truth table, and once seriously, via an entire live subfield of computer science built to make the mechanical procedure fast in the cases that actually arise in practice. The one thing decidability was never able to promise, and the one thing Cook and Levin's theorem forecloses barring a resolution of P vs NP, is that the mechanical procedure could ever be made fast in every case — and that gap, between "an algorithm exists" and "an algorithm exists that is always fast," is where this essay's first-principles construction of propositional logic finally runs out of things it can settle on its own.

    Example 1 — checking an everyday argument for validity, two ways

    Argument: "If the museum is open, then admission is free or a discount applies. Admission is not free. Therefore, if the museum is open, a discount applies." Let \(p=\)"the museum is open," \(q=\)"admission is free," \(r=\)"a discount applies." Symbolized: premises \(p\to(q\vee r)\) and \(\neg q\); conclusion \(p\to r\).

    Semantic check (Panel III, VI)

    Suppose \(v\) satisfies both premises: \(v(p\to(q\vee r))=T\) and \(v(q)=F\). To show \(v(p\to r)=T\): if \(v(p)=F\), the conclusion is vacuously true. If \(v(p)=T\), the first premise forces \(v(q\vee r)=T\); since \(v(q)=F\), it must be \(v(r)=T\) carrying the disjunction — so \(v(p\to r)=T\) either way. Every model of the premises is a model of the conclusion: \(\{p\to(q\vee r),\neg q\}\models p\to r\).

    Syntactic proof (Panel X, XI, XII)

  • p → (q∨r)premise
  • ¬qpremise
  • passumption, for →I
  • q ∨ r→E, 1, 3
  • rDS, 4, 2
  • p → r→I, discharging 3–5

    • Two completely different procedures — one exhaustive case analysis over valuations, one finite chain of cited rules — arriving at the identical conclusion, exactly as Panel XV's soundness and completeness theorems guarantee they always must.

    Example 2 — a real number-theoretic proof, symbolized at each step

    Claim: for every integer \(n\), if \(n^2\) is divisible by 3, then \(n\) is divisible by 3.

    Proof, by contraposition (Panel XIV) with proof by cases (Panel XIV) inside it. The contrapositive to prove instead is: if \(n\) is not divisible by 3, then \(n^2\) is not divisible by 3. Assume \(3\nmid n\). Every integer leaves remainder 0, 1, or 2 on division by 3 (an instance of the law of the excluded middle applied three ways, Panel XI); since \(3\nmid n\) rules out remainder 0, exactly two cases remain, and they are exhaustive by that same fact.

    Case \(n=3k+1\): \(n^2=9k^2+6k+1=3(3k^2+2k)+1\) — remainder 1, so \(3\nmid n^2\).

    Case \(n=3k+2\): \(n^2=9k^2+12k+4=3(3k^2+4k+1)+1\) — remainder 1 again, so \(3\nmid n^2\).

    Both cases give \(3\nmid n^2\), and the cases exhaust every possibility once \(3\nmid n\) is assumed. So \(3\nmid n\to 3\nmid n^2\); by the contrapositive law (Panel VII), \(3\mid n^2\to 3\mid n\) — the original claim. ∎ Every visible move in this proof — "by contraposition," "by cases," the exhaustiveness of the two remaining cases — is a direct citation of a specific panel above, not an unexplained leap.

    Example 3 — from a truth table to a minimal circuit

    Design a majority voter: a circuit outputting true exactly when at least two of three inputs \(p,q,r\) are true (the standard building block of triple modular redundancy in fault-tolerant hardware). The canonical DNF (Panel VIII), read off the four rows where at least two inputs are T, is \((p\wedge q\wedge r)\vee(p\wedge q\wedge\neg r)\vee(p\wedge\neg q\wedge r)\vee(\neg p\wedge q\wedge r)\) — correct, but far larger than necessary.

    Absorbing the two clauses sharing \(p\wedge q\) (\(p\wedge q\wedge r\) and \(p\wedge q\wedge\neg r\)) via the negation and identity laws collapses them to \(p\wedge q\) alone; repeating the same absorption against the other two minterms yields the compact, well-known identity:

    majority(p,q,r) ≡ (p∧q) ∨ (q∧r) ∨ (p∧r)

    Panel IX's functional completeness result says this — like every truth function — is realizable from NAND gates alone. Converting just the first disjunct as a representative: \(p\wedge q\equiv(p\uparrow q)\uparrow(p\uparrow q)\); the other two conjuncts convert identically, and the three resulting NAND-built conjunctions are then combined with two more NAND-built disjunctions (\(a\vee b\equiv(a\uparrow a)\uparrow(b\uparrow b)\)) to finish the circuit — a real, fabricable majority voter, built from one repeated gate type, traced directly back to Panel VIII's construction theorem and Panel IX's NAND-completeness proof.

    Example 4 — a scheduling conflict, found by resolution

    Two adjacent regions on a map, \(X\) and \(Y\), must be colored differently using only red and blue; let \(x=\)"\(X\) is red," \(y=\)"\(Y\) is red." "Different colors" becomes two CNF clauses (Panel VIII): \(\{x,y\}\) (at least one is red) and \(\{\neg x,\neg y\}\) (not both red). Now suppose a separate constraint additionally forces \(X\) red and, independently, forces \(Y\) red too: two more unit clauses, \(\{x\}\) and \(\{y\}\). Is the combined constraint set satisfiable?

    Clause set Δ: {x,y} {¬x,¬y} {x} {y} Resolution refutation (Panel XII, XVI): {x} and {¬x,¬y} resolve on x → {¬y} {¬y} and {y} resolve on y → {} (the empty clause)

    Deriving the empty clause proves, by Panel XVI's refutation-completeness theorem, that \(\Delta\) is unsatisfiable — the two "forced red" constraints are jointly incompatible with the adjacency constraint, exactly as expected, and the two-step resolution trace above is a miniature, fully hand-checkable version of what an industrial CDCL solver does automatically, at far larger scale, when it detects an unsatisfiable set of scheduling, verification, or configuration constraints.

    Example 5 — a knights-and-knaves puzzle, solved by satisfiability

    On an island, every inhabitant is either a knight, who always tells the truth, or a knave, who always lies — never both, never neither, a bivalence assumption (Panel III) transplanted directly into a puzzle. Inhabitant A says: "We are both knaves." What are A and B?

    Let \(a=\)"A is a knight" and \(b=\)"B is a knight." A's statement symbolizes as \(\neg a\wedge\neg b\) ("A is a knave and B is a knave"). The knight/knave rule itself — a speaker's statement is true exactly when the speaker is a knight — becomes a biconditional constraint that any valid world must satisfy: \(\varphi = a\leftrightarrow(\neg a\wedge\neg b)\).

    Exactly one row satisfies \(\varphi\) — Panel VI's language applies directly: \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, with a unique model, \(a=F,b=T\). A is a knave, and B is a knight. The reasoning behind the winning row is worth stating in words, since it is the classic solution to this puzzle: if A were a knight, A's statement would have to be true, meaning A is a knave — an immediate contradiction, ruling out row 1 and row 2 without even reaching them by table.     What these five examples have in common

    None of the five problems above started out looking like propositional logic — an everyday argument about museum admission, a fact about integers modulo 3, a hardware design constraint, a map-coloring conflict, and a logic puzzle about liars. Symbolizing each one was the genuine first step every time, not a formality tacked on afterward, and it is worth naming the pattern explicitly: once a problem is correctly translated into propositions and connectives, this essay's Panels II through XVI take over completely, and the original subject matter — museums, integers, circuits, maps, islands — never has to be thought about again until the final answer is translated back. That translate-solve-translate-back shape is, in miniature, exactly what Panel XVI's SAT-based encodings of graph coloring and scheduling do at industrial scale, and it is the single most transferable skill this essay has to offer past its own subject matter.

    E · Ordinary mathematical proof

    Problem E1. Prove: for all integers \(a,b\), if \(a\) and \(b\) are both odd, then \(a+b\) is even.

    Solution (direct proof). Assume \(a,b\) are both odd: \(a=2j+1\), \(b=2k+1\) for integers \(j,k\). Then \(a+b=2j+1+2k+1=2(j+k+1)\), which is \(2\) times an integer — even. Since assuming both odd led directly to \(a+b\) even, the conditional is proved. ∎

    Problem E2. Prove: there is no smallest positive rational number.

    Solution (proof by contradiction). Suppose, toward contradiction, that some positive rational \(q\) is the smallest one. Then \(q/2\) is also a positive rational number (half a positive number is still positive, and the rationals are closed under division by 2), and \(q/20\). But this contradicts \(q\) being the smallest positive rational — \(q/2\) is smaller still. The assumption fails; no smallest positive rational exists. ∎ Notice the propositional skeleton is pure ¬I (Panel XI): assume \(\neg\varphi\) ("some \(q\) is smallest"), derive \(\bot\) (a rational both smallest and not-smallest), conclude \(\neg\neg\varphi\), and DNE closes the last step exactly as it did for excluded middle.

    F · Normal forms and functional completeness

    Problem F1. Convert \(p\leftrightarrow(q\vee r)\) to CNF using the three-step recipe from Panel VIII.

  • p ↔ (q∨r)start
  • ≡ (p→(q∨r)) ∧ ((q∨r)→p)step 1: biconditional law
  • ≡ (¬p∨q∨r) ∧ (¬(q∨r)∨p)step 1 continued: implication law, both conjuncts
  • ≡ (¬p∨q∨r) ∧ ((¬q∧¬r)∨p)step 2: De Morgan's, pushing ¬ onto (q∨r)
  • ≡ (¬p∨q∨r) ∧ (¬q∨p) ∧ (¬r∨p)step 3: distribute (¬q∧¬r)∨p over ∧

    • Three clauses, each a disjunction of literals — a valid CNF, ready for Panel XVI's resolution or DPLL machinery.

    Problem F2. Show \(\{\downarrow\}\) (NOR alone) is functionally complete, by the dual of Panel IX's NAND argument.

    Solution. \(\neg p\equiv p\downarrow p\) (NOR of \(p\) with itself: true exactly when neither disjunct — meaning \(p\) — is true, i.e. when \(p\) is false). \(p\vee q\equiv(p\downarrow q)\downarrow(p\downarrow q)\) (negating \(p\downarrow q=\neg(p\vee q)\) recovers \(p\vee q\), via the identity just proved). Since \(\{\neg,\vee\}\) is already known adequate (Panel IX), and both members are now expressed using \(\downarrow\) alone, \(\{\downarrow\}\) is functionally complete. ∎

    G · Resolution and satisfiability

    Problem G1. Using resolution, determine whether \(\Delta=\{\{\neg p,q\},\{\neg q,r\},\{p\},\{\neg r\}\}\) is satisfiable.

    {p} and {¬p,q} resolve on p → {q} {q} and {¬q,r} resolve on q → {r} {r} and {¬r} resolve on r → {} (empty clause)

    Solution. The empty clause is derived, so by Panel XVI's refutation-completeness theorem \(\Delta\) is unsatisfiable — the four constraints \(p\), \(p\to q\) (in clause form \(\neg p\vee q\)), \(q\to r\), and \(\neg r\) chain together via hypothetical syllogism into \(p\to r\), which directly conflicts with the forced \(p\) and \(\neg r\).

    Problem G2. Is \(\Delta=\{\{p,q\},\{\neg p,q\},\{p,\neg q\}\}\) satisfiable? If so, exhibit every model.

    Solution. Check all four valuations directly. \(p{=}F,q{=}F\): clause \(\{p,q\}\) is \(F\vee F=F\) — fails. \(p{=}T,q{=}F\): clause \(\{\neg p,q\}\) is \(F\vee F=F\) — fails. \(p{=}F,q{=}T\): clause \(\{p,\neg q\}\) is \(F\vee F=F\) — fails. \(p{=}T,q{=}T\): \(\{p,q\}=T\), \(\{\neg p,q\}=T\), \(\{p,\neg q\}=T\) — all three satisfied. \(\Delta\) is satisfiable, with a unique model: \(p=T,q=T\).

    H · Capstone problems — every panel at once

    Problem H1. The argument "\((p\vee q)\to r\); \(\neg r\); therefore \(\neg p\wedge\neg q\)" — verify its validity three completely independent ways: semantically, syntactically by natural deduction, and by resolution refutation.

    Solution — semantic (Panel III, VI). Suppose \(v\) satisfies both premises: \(v((p\vee q)\to r)=T\) and \(v(r)=F\). Since the conditional is true and its consequent \(r\) is false, its antecedent must also be false (Panel IV's table): \(v(p\vee q)=F\), so \(v(p)=F\) and \(v(q)=F\), giving \(v(\neg p\wedge\neg q)=T\). Entailment holds.

    Solution — natural deduction (Panel X, XI, XII, VII).

    Problem H2. Give a full natural deduction derivation of \((p\to q)\vee(q\to p)\) — the tautology Panel VI verified by truth table but never proved syntactically.

    Problem H3. Prove there are infinitely many primes — Euclid's theorem, roughly 300 BCE, and one of the oldest reductio ad absurdum proofs in mathematics.

    Solution (proof by contradiction, with proof by cases nested inside it). Suppose, toward contradiction, there are only finitely many primes, forming a complete list \(p_1,p_2,\dots,p_n\). Let \(N=p_1p_2\cdots p_n+1\). By the excluded middle, \(N\) is either prime or composite (every integer greater than 1 is one or the other). Case \(N\) is prime: \(N\) is larger than every \(p_i\) in the list, so \(N\) is a prime not among \(p_1,\dots,p_n\) — but the list was assumed complete. Contradiction. Case \(N\) is composite: \(N\) has some prime factor \(q\). Dividing \(N\) by any \(p_i\) from the list leaves remainder exactly \(1\) (since \(N\) is \(1\) more than a multiple of \(p_i\)), so no \(p_i\) divides \(N\) — meaning \(q\) is not among \(p_1,\dots,p_n\) either, again a prime missing from the supposedly complete list. Contradiction. Both cases — exhaustive, by excluded middle — contradict the assumption that \(p_1,\dots,p_n\) is a complete list of primes. So no finite list is complete: there are infinitely many primes. ∎

    Frequently Confused Ideas

    a dozen mix-ups this essay has tried to head off in passing, collected in one place and answered directly
    Confusion — "p → q means p causes q"

    No. \(\to\) is truth-functional (Panel III): its output depends only on the truth values of \(p\) and \(q\), never on any causal, temporal, or explanatory relationship between them. "If the Eiffel Tower is in Paris, then 2+2=4" is a true conditional — both sides happen to be true, so the table says T — despite the two sides having nothing whatsoever to do with each other. Ordinary English "if... then" often does smuggle in a causal claim; the symbol \(\to\) deliberately does not, and treating the two as interchangeable is the single most common source of confusion when first meeting formal logic.

    Confusion — "p → q being vacuously true when p is false feels like a loophole"

    It only feels that way if \(\to\) is read as a causal claim (see above). Read instead as a promise or a contract — "if you finish the report, you get a bonus" — a false antecedent means the contract was never actually invoked: you didn't finish the report, so nothing has happened that could possibly violate what was promised, and the promise is correctly scored as unbroken. Panel IV's warning box works through this with "if pigs can fly, I am the Pope" in more detail; the short version is that vacuous truth is not a strange exception bolted onto the definition, it is what falls out automatically from requiring \(p\to q\) to be false in exactly the one case where a true antecedent fails to deliver.

    Confusion — "⊢ and ⊨ are just two names for the same thing, so why build two systems?"

    They are extensionally the same relation for propositional logic — Panel XV's whole point — but they are not the same idea, and the fact that they coincide is a genuine theorem, not a triviality. \(\models\) is defined by exhaustively checking every valuation; \(\vdash\) is defined by a finite chain of symbol manipulations that never once consults a truth value. Nothing in either definition guarantees in advance that they pick out the same set of formulas — plenty of formal systems in mathematics have a provability notion strictly weaker than their intended semantics (incomplete systems), and some have a provability notion that proves things that aren't semantically valid (unsound systems). Propositional logic having both soundness and completeness, proved in full in Panel XV, is a substantive, hard-won result about this specific system — not a definitional inevitability.

    Confusion — "doesn't Gödel's incompleteness theorem mean logic itself is incomplete?"

    Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) are about specific, much stronger formal systems — ones expressive enough to encode arithmetic on the natural numbers, meaning first-order logic plus axioms for \(+,\times\), and induction. They say those particular systems cannot be both consistent and complete. Propositional logic, the system this entire essay builds, is not expressive enough to encode arithmetic at all — it has no numbers, no quantifiers, no induction — and Panel XV's completeness theorem for it is a completely different, fully proved, unconditional result, standing in no tension with Gödel whatsoever. "Logic is broken" is not what Gödel showed; "arithmetic is powerful enough to talk about its own provability, and that self-reference has consequences" is closer, and it simply does not apply to the system built here.

    Confusion — "if NAND alone is enough (Panel IX), why does anyone bother with the other seven connectives?"

    Functional completeness is a claim about expressive power, not about convenience or readability. Every formula in this entire essay could, in principle, be rewritten using nothing but \(\uparrow\), the way any computer program could in principle be written in raw machine code — but \(p\wedge q\equiv(p\uparrow q)\uparrow(p\uparrow q)\) is already harder to read than \(p\wedge q\), and the gap only widens for larger formulas. The five (or eight) named connectives exist because each captures a distinct, commonly needed shape of English claim compactly; \(\{\uparrow\}\)'s completeness is a statement about the floor of what's possible, not a recommendation for how to actually write anything.

    Confusion — "isn't 'valid' just a fancy word for 'true'?"

    No, and mixing the two up is worth guarding against explicitly. Truth (Panel III) is a property of a single formula under a single valuation. Tautology (Panel VI) is a property of a single formula, true under every valuation. Validity (Panel I) is a property of an argument — a set of premises together with a conclusion — asking whether every valuation satisfying the premises also satisfies the conclusion. A valid argument can have a false conclusion (if a premise is false); a true formula under one valuation can be false under another; only a tautology is "true no matter what" in the single-formula sense, and only a sound argument (valid, with actually-true premises) guarantees an actually-true conclusion. Four related but genuinely distinct ideas, easy to blur together, each with its own precise definition somewhere above.

    Confusion — "English 'or' obviously means one or the other, not both — why is ∨ inclusive?"

    English "or" is genuinely ambiguous, and context resolves it differently in different sentences — Panel IV's soup-or-salad example leans exclusive, its French-or-Spanish placement-exam example leans inclusive, and native speakers use both constantly without noticing the switch. Mathematics cannot tolerate an ambiguous primitive symbol, so it resolves the ambiguity by permanent fiat: \(\vee\) is always inclusive, and the genuinely exclusive sense gets its own dedicated symbol, \(\oplus\), precisely so a formula never has to lean on context the way English does.

    Confusion — "if propositional logic is decidable (Panel XVI), why do SAT solvers need to exist at all — just build the truth table"

    Decidable means an algorithm is guaranteed to terminate with a correct answer — it says nothing about how long that algorithm takes. The truth table method is a perfectly correct decision procedure that needs \(2^n\) rows for \(n\) variables, and \(2^{100}\) already exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. Decidability is a floor ("some algorithm exists at all"), not a ceiling ("a fast algorithm exists") — and whether a fast one exists for SAT specifically is exactly the open P vs NP question Panel XVI closes on.

    Confusion — "why does Panel II insist on a fully formal grammar — can't you just tell if something 'looks like' a formula?"

    Usually, yes, by eye. The formal grammar exists for the cases where eyeballing fails, and more importantly, for building anything on top of the notion of formula that needs to never be wrong — a compiler-style parser, a proof checker, an automated SAT solver. "Looks like a formula" is not a specification a computer program can implement, and it is not precise enough to support the unique readability theorem Panel III's entire recursive semantics depends on. The formal grammar is overkill for reading a formula by eye; it is exactly the right amount of rigor for everything built in Panels III through XVI.

    Confusion — "a tautology and a theorem sound like the same thing — are they?"

    They start as different ideas from different halves of the essay — tautology (Panel VI) is semantic, defined by truth tables; theorem (Panel X, a formula \(\varphi\) with \(\vdash\varphi\)) is syntactic, defined by derivations. Panel XV's soundness and completeness theorems together prove these two sets of formulas are, for propositional logic, exactly identical — every tautology is a theorem and every theorem is a tautology — which is precisely why, in casual conversation past Panel XV, the two words end up used almost interchangeably. Before that theorem is proved, though, "tautology" and "theorem" name two conceptually separate properties that merely happen, provably, to coincide.

    Confusion — "classical logic feels like the only sensible logic — what does Panel XI's talk of 'intuitionistic logic' even mean?"

    Classical logic (this essay, throughout) commits to bivalence from Panel III onward and, correspondingly, accepts DNE and the law of the excluded middle unconditionally. Intuitionistic logic is a genuinely coherent rival system, built by mathematicians who found "assume ¬φ, derive a contradiction, therefore φ" philosophically unsatisfying as a way of establishing φ when no actual construction of φ was ever produced — it keeps every rule in Panel XI except DNE. Neither system is a corrupted version of the other; they are two different, internally consistent commitments about what counts as a proof, and this essay's choice of classical logic is exactly that — a choice, made explicitly in Panel III and never hidden, not a default assumed without comment.

    Confusion — "what did going all the way back to raw symbols in Panel II actually buy you?"

    Every later theorem in this essay depends on some fact established purely from the syntax — unique readability underwrites Panel III's semantics being well-defined at all; the grammar's five clauses are exactly what structural induction, used constantly from Panel VII onward, inducts over; Panel X's derivations are checkable by a machine with zero understanding of meaning precisely because "is this a legal proof step" reduces to pattern-matching against the grammar and the axiom schemas. None of this is available to an approach that starts from "a formula is whatever looks like a formula" — the payoff of Panel I's first-principles commitment is not philosophical tidiness for its own sake, it is that everything built afterward, all the way through Panel XVI's SAT solvers, rests on foundations that were actually checked rather than assumed.

    Confusion — "modus ponens and the implication law look like they're saying the same thing — aren't p→q≡¬p∨q and 'from p and p→q, infer q' the same fact twice?"

    They live in entirely different categories despite the surface resemblance. The implication law (Panel VII) is a claim of logical equivalence between two formulas — \(p\to q\) and \(\neg p\vee q\) have the same truth value on every valuation, full stop, no premises or inference involved. Modus ponens (Panel X, XI, XII) is a rule of inference — a license to move from two already-established formulas to a third one inside a derivation. One is a fact about a static pair of formulas; the other is a dynamic step a proof is allowed to take. It is possible (and this essay's Panel X system does exactly this) to have MP as a rule while never mentioning \(\vee\) at all, and it is possible to have every equivalence law in Panel VII's table without any proof theory built on top of it whatsoever — the two ideas are logically independent, even though, confusingly, both involve the symbol \(\to\).

    Confusion — "if resolution (Panel XII, XVI) only needs one rule, why does Panel XII bother cataloguing eleven of them?"

    Resolution is complete for a very specific job — refuting an unsatisfiable set of CNF clauses (Panel XVI's refutation-completeness theorem) — and it is genuinely all a machine needs for that one job, which is exactly why SAT solvers are built around it alone. It is a poor fit for the very different job Panel XII's full catalogue exists for: producing a human-readable derivation of a conclusion from premises, structured the way a person actually reasons, step by nameable step. A resolution refutation of a moderately complex argument is typically an unreadable pile of resolved clauses with no correspondence to the original English premises at all; a hypothetical-syllogism-then-modus-tollens derivation, by contrast, reads almost like the English argument it symbolizes. Different tools, optimized for different consumers of the proof — a machine that only needs a yes/no answer, versus a person who needs to follow the reasoning.

    Confusion — "if an argument's premises are all false, can it still be valid?"

    Yes, and this is precisely Panel I's opening example doing its job: "all fish are purple; Nemo is a fish; therefore Nemo is purple" is valid despite every single sentence in it being false, because validity only asks about the conditional relationship between premises and conclusion — if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true as well — never whether they actually are. An argument with false premises can be valid (as above) or invalid (almost anything can follow from false premises being combined carelessly); false premises settle nothing about validity either way. What false premises do rule out is soundness (Panel I's other definition), which additionally demands the premises be actually true — and it is soundness, not validity, that guarantees an actually true conclusion.

    Confusion — "this essay proved completeness for propositional logic (Panel XV) — doesn't that mean every true mathematical statement is provable?"

    No — and the gap is exactly the gap between propositional logic and full mathematics. Panel XV's completeness theorem says every propositional tautology (true under every valuation of its propositional variables) is provable in the propositional proof system. It says nothing about number theory, set theory, or any richer mathematical statement — those live in far more expressive systems (first-order logic with axioms for arithmetic, or stronger), where the earlier "Confusion" box on Gödel already explained why completeness in that unrestricted sense provably fails. Propositional logic is complete because it is expressively narrow enough — no quantifiers, no numbers, no induction — for completeness to be achievable at all; the moment a system gains enough expressive power to talk about its own provability, Gödel's theorems say that achievability is permanently lost. Completeness here is a genuine, hard-won result about a genuinely limited system, not a preview of something available everywhere in mathematics.

    Confusion — "Panel XI dropped DNE to get intuitionistic logic — does that mean intuitionistic logic is somehow 'weaker,' with fewer theorems across the board?"

    Weaker in exactly one specific sense — it proves a strict subset of what classical logic proves, since every intuitionistic derivation is automatically a classical one (all its rules are on the classical list too) but not vice versa, as Panel XI's Derivation 3 versus Derivation 4 comparison showed directly: excluded middle needs DNE, De Morgan's hard direction doesn't. It is not "weaker" in the sense of being less rigorous, less useful, or less studied — intuitionistic logic underlies the entire discipline of type theory and much of modern proof-assistant software (Coq, Agda, Lean), precisely because its proofs, unlike classical reductio proofs, are required to carry an explicit construction or witness inside them, which happens to be exactly what a computer program needs to extract. "Fewer theorems, but every theorem computationally meaningful" is closer to the right way to hear "weaker" here than "worse."

    Glossary

    Proposition
    A declarative sentence with exactly one of two truth values, T or F.
    Well-formed formula (wff)
    A string built from the alphabet of Panel II by finitely many applications of the grammar's base case and five recursive clauses.
    Structural induction
    The proof technique licensed by WFF's recursive definition: prove a property for atoms, then show each grammar clause preserves it.
    Valuation
    A function assigning T or F to every propositional variable, extended to all formulas by Panel III's recursive clauses.
    Model
    A valuation that satisfies (makes true) a given formula or set of formulas.
    Semantic entailment (Γ⊨φ)
    Every valuation satisfying Γ also satisfies φ.
    Tautology
    A formula true under every valuation.
    Contradiction
    A formula false under every valuation; equivalently, unsatisfiable.
    Contingency
    A formula true under some valuations and false under others.
    Satisfiable
    Having at least one model.
    Logical equivalence (φ≡ψ)
    Agreeing in truth value under every valuation.
    Duality
    The ∧↔∨, T↔F swap that turns any {∧,∨,¬}-law into another valid law.
    Literal
    A propositional variable or its negation.
    DNF / CNF
    Disjunction of conjunctions of literals / conjunction of disjunctions (clauses) of literals.
    Minterm / maxterm
    A conjunction / disjunction containing every variable exactly once, bare or negated.
    Functional completeness
    A set of connectives able to express every possible truth function.
    Axiomatic (Hilbert-style) system
    A proof system built from axiom schemas plus modus ponens as the sole inference rule.
    Deduction theorem
    Γ,φ⊢ψ if and only if Γ⊢φ→ψ — the formal license for conditional proof.
    Natural deduction
    A proof system built around explicit assumption and discharge, with introduction/elimination rules per connective.
    Reductio ad absurdum
    Assuming ¬φ, deriving a contradiction, and (via classical DNE) concluding φ.
    Rule of inference
    A named, independently-proved argument form (MP, MT, HS, DS, and others) citable in one step.
    Formal fallacy
    An invalid argument form refutable by a single counterexample valuation.
    Soundness
    Γ⊢φ implies Γ⊨φ — nothing false is ever provable.
    Completeness
    Γ⊨φ implies Γ⊢φ — nothing true is ever unprovable.
    Compactness
    A set of formulas is satisfiable iff every finite subset of it is.
    Decidable
    Answerable by an algorithm guaranteed to terminate with the correct answer.
    SAT
    The decision problem: is a given CNF formula satisfiable?
    NP-complete
    In NP, and at least as hard as every other problem in NP (Cook–Levin, for SAT specifically).
    Resolution
    From p∨q and ¬p∨r, infer q∨r — refutation-complete for CNF clause sets.

    A shortest equivalent normal form; not unique in general, unlike the canonical form it's shrunk from.

    A grid layout of a truth table that makes adjacent, mergeable minterms visually obvious.
    Post's theorem
    A set of connectives is functionally complete iff it isn't contained in any of five special classes of truth function.
    Hilbert-style derivation
    A finite sequence of formulas, each an axiom instance, a premise, or an MP consequence of two earlier lines.
    Kalmár's Lemma
    p₁ᵛ,…,pₙᵛ ⊢ φᵛ for every valuation v — the constructive engine behind Panel XV's completeness proof.
    Consistent
    A set of formulas from which ⊥ cannot be derived; provably the same property as satisfiable.
    DPLL
    A backtracking search algorithm for SAT, built on unit propagation and pure literal elimination.
    CDCL
    Conflict-driven clause learning — the resolution-based engine behind modern industrial SAT solvers.
    P, NP
    Polynomial-time solvable; polynomial-time verifiable. Whether P = NP is open.
    Cook–Levin theorem
    SAT is NP-complete — the first problem ever proved so, in 1971.
    Intuitionistic logic
    Classical logic minus DNE — every proof must carry an explicit construction, not just a contradiction of the alternative.
    Peirce's law
    ((p→q)→p)→p — classically valid, but not derivable without DNE-strength reasoning.
    Post's lattice
    The complete map of every closed class of Boolean functions, underlying Post's completeness criterion.
    Karnaugh map adjacency
    Only cells differing in exactly one variable's value may be grouped into a single simplified term.
    Refutation lemma
    Γ⊨φ iff Γ∪{¬φ} is unsatisfiable — the semantic mirror of proof by contradiction.
    Formation sequence
    The explicit list of grammar-clause applications that builds a wff up from bare variables.
    Principal (main) connective
    The connective applied at a formula's very last, outermost construction step.

    Note — where propositional logic's story continues from here

    This essay stopped, deliberately, at the boundary Panel I drew in its very first panel: whole, already-settled propositions, combined by truth-functional connectives, with nothing said about "every" or "some." Predicate logic is the next stop — quantifiers, domains of discourse, and the loss of decidability this essay's Panel XVI flagged as the price of that added expressive power — and it inherits every tool built here wholesale: its own semantics is built directly on top of Panel III's valuation machinery, its own proof theory extends Panels X and XI's rules with two more pairs of introduction/elimination rules for ∀ and ∃, and Panel XV's soundness theorem for predicate logic is a routine generalization of the propositional argument given in full here. Nothing in this essay is thrown away once quantifiers arrive — it becomes the base case of a larger induction.
