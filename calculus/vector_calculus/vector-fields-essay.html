VECTOR FIELDS

Vector Fields

a function that hands back an arrow instead of a number — and the two families of arrows that organize everything that follows

From a number at each point to an arrow at each point

A scalar field is the familiar kind of function: at every point of the plane it hands you back a single number. Temperature \(T(x,y)\) on a weather map is a scalar field — at each spot, one number, a color on the map. A vector field asks a different question at each point: not "how much," but "how much, and in which direction." Wind doesn't just have a speed at each location — it has a direction. Gravity near a planet doesn't just have a strength — it points somewhere. At every point, instead of a single number, you get an arrow.

Definition — vector field

A vector field on a region \(D\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) is a function \(\mathbf F:D\to\mathbb R^2\) assigning to each point \((x,y)\) a vector

where \(P\) and \(Q\), the components, are ordinary scalar functions of \((x,y)\). Nothing here is exotic — a vector field is just two scalar fields, glued together and read as one arrow rather than two separate numbers. The three-dimensional version is identical in spirit: \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=\big(P,Q,R\big)\).

Concrete pictures worth keeping in mind: the velocity of wind at every point of the atmosphere, the velocity of water at every point of a river, the gravitational pull at every point around a planet, the electric field around a charge, and — the purely mathematical example that turns out to run the whole show — the gradient of a hill's height function, \(\nabla h\), an arrow at every point telling you which way is steepest uphill and by how much.

Drawing a vector field

You can't draw the arrow at literally every point — there are infinitely many. The convention is to sample a grid of points, and at each one draw a short arrow starting there, pointing in the direction of \(\mathbf F\) at that point, with a length representing (often only roughly, for legibility) its magnitude. This is the picture the interactive lab in Panel VI draws for you directly.

Two families worth recognizing on sight

Almost every vector field you'll meet is some blend of two archetypes. Learning to recognize them by eye is worth more than any formula in this essay.

Family 1 — gradient fields. If \(f(x,y)\) is a scalar function (think: a height, a temperature, a potential), its gradient \(\nabla f = (f_x,f_y)\) is itself a vector field. At every point, \(\nabla f\) points in the direction \(f\) increases fastest, its length measures how steep that increase is, and — this is the picture to hold onto — it is always perpendicular to the level curve of \(f\) through that point. Take \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\), a bowl-shaped surface. Its level curves are concentric circles, and \(\nabla f=(2x,2y)\) is a field of arrows pointing straight outward, radially, growing longer farther from the center — exactly perpendicular to those circles everywhere.

Insight

Picture a topographic map's contour lines. Water doesn't flow along a contour line — a contour line is a path of constant elevation, so flowing along it does no downhill work at all. Water flows across contour lines, perpendicular to them, straight down the steepest available slope. That's not a coincidence or a metaphor: a gradient field's arrows are, quite literally, always perpendicular to the level curves of its potential, for exactly this reason.

Family 2 — rotational fields. Take \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x)\). At the point \((1,0)\) it points straight up, \((0,1)\); a quarter-turn away at \((0,1)\) it points straight left, \((-1,0)\). At every point, the arrow is perpendicular to the line from the origin to that point — tangent to the circle through it — producing a swirling, pinwheel pattern with no center of "outflow" anywhere. Fields like this arise from rotating fluid, from the magnetic field circling a current-carrying wire, from anything with a genuine sense of spin built in.

Note — why these two families matter so much

Family 1 fields are called conservative, and they turn out to make integration along a path almost embarrassingly easy — Panel III shows why. Family 2 fields are, generically, not conservative, and integrating along a path in them behaves in a genuinely different way — Panels II and V work out exactly how. Learning to tell these two families apart at a glance, and knowing precisely how to integrate honestly along a path in either case, is the entire content of this essay.

Flow lines

One more piece of intuition, used implicitly throughout: a flow line (or streamline) of \(\mathbf F\) is a curve that is, at every one of its points, tangent to \(\mathbf F\) there. Picture a leaf dropped into a river — the path it traces out is a flow line of the water's velocity field. Vector fields aren't studied in isolation; the natural question to ask of one is always "what happens as something moves through it, along some path?" That question — made precise — is exactly the subject of Panel II.

Integrating Along a Path

work, one small step at a time — the same chop-sample-sum-limit maneuver, now dotted against a direction

The physical question that forces this definition

Start with the simplest possible case, from introductory physics: a constant force \(\mathbf F\) pushes an object through a straight displacement \(\mathbf d\). The work done is

Only the component of \(\mathbf F\) along the direction of motion contributes; a force perpendicular to the motion does zero work, however strong it is. But real fields vary from point to point, and real paths curve. The line integral is nothing more than what happens when you force this one idea — dot the field against the direction of travel — to survive both of those complications at once.

Insight

This is the same recurring maneuver behind every integral you've ever computed — chop, sample, multiply, sum, take a limit — just applied to something new: instead of multiplying a height by a width, or a height by a patch of area, each infinitesimal contribution here is a dot product between the field and the direction the curve happens to be heading at that instant. The definition isn't inventing a new kind of limit; it's re-running the familiar one with a new kind of "multiply."

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x)\), the swirl field from Panel I, and let \(C\) be the unit circle traced once counterclockwise: \(\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)\), \(t\in[0,2\pi]\), so \(\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t)\). Then

Read the geometry behind that number: at every point of the circle, \(\mathbf F\) is tangent to the circle and pointing the same way you're traveling — so every infinitesimal piece of work is positive, and they all add up rather than cancel. Reverse the direction of travel — same circle, clockwise — and \(\mathbf r'(t)\) flips sign, so the whole integral flips sign too: \(-2\pi\).

Warning — orientation matters

Unlike an ordinary arc-length integral of a scalar function, \(\int_C f\,ds\), which doesn't care which way you traverse \(C\), the line integral of a vector field is orientation-dependent: reversing the direction of travel negates the answer, \(\int_{-C}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = -\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\). This is one of the most common sources of sign errors — always fix a direction of travel before computing.

What survives, for free, from being a sum of dot products

Because the line integral is built as a limit of sums, it inherits several facts immediately, with no extra machinery: linearity in \(\mathbf F\); additivity over a curve split into consecutive pieces, \(\int_{C_1+C_2}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_{C_1}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r+\int_{C_2}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\); and independence from the choice of parametrization, provided the new parametrization traces the same curve in the same direction, only possibly at a different speed — a quick chain-rule check confirms the extra speed factor introduced by reparametrizing cancels exactly, the same way it does in an ordinary 1D substitution.

Conservative Fields

when the field is secretly a slope map, the line integral collapses to two numbers: where you started, and where you ended

A puzzle from the last example

Panel II's worked example should feel slightly unsettling. The swirl field pushed something all the way around a loop, back to exactly where it started — and yet the total work done was \(2\pi\), not zero. If \(\mathbf F\) were a real physical force, that looks like getting energy for free just by going in a circle. Some fields, it turns out, never let that happen: send them around any closed loop at all, and the net work is always exactly zero. These are the conservative fields, and understanding why they behave this way — and exactly how much easier they are to integrate — is the payoff of this panel.

Insight — where the name comes from

Physics defines force as \(\mathbf F=-\nabla U\) for a potential energy \(U\) (the sign is a convention, not a new idea). The theorem then says the work done by the force is \(W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = -\big(U(B)-U(A)\big)=-\Delta U\). Since the work-energy theorem gives \(W=\Delta(\text{kinetic energy})\), combining the two says \(\Delta(\text{KE})+\Delta U=0\) — total mechanical energy is conserved. The line-integral property proved above (path independence, coming next) isn't the origin of the name "conservative" — it's the mathematical shadow that energy conservation casts onto the geometry of paths.

Testing for a Potential

a two-line arithmetic check that almost decides everything, and the topological catch that keeps it from being the whole story

From path-independence to an actual potential

Panel III showed conservative \(\implies\) path-independent. The converse is also true, and constructing it explicitly is worth doing once, because the construction is itself illuminating.

Insight — three equivalent statements

On a connected open domain \(D\), the following are equivalent: (1) \(\mathbf F\) is conservative on \(D\); (2) \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) is the same for every path \(C\) between any fixed pair of endpoints in \(D\); (3) \(\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0\) for every closed loop \(C\) in \(D\). (2) and (3) are the same fact stated two ways: two paths sharing endpoints, one run forward and the other in reverse, concatenate into a single closed loop, and the loop's integral is exactly the difference of the two paths' integrals.

To check \(\partial f/\partial x = P\): compare \(f(x+h,y)\) to \(f(x,y)\) by choosing the path to \((x+h,y)\) to be any path to \((x,y)\), followed by a short horizontal segment of length \(h\). The difference \(f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)\) is then just the line integral over that one short horizontal piece, which by continuity of \(P\) is \(\approx P(x,y)\cdot h\) for small \(h\). Dividing by \(h\) and letting \(h\to0\) gives exactly \(\partial f/\partial x = P\); the symmetric argument with a vertical segment gives \(\partial f/\partial y=Q\). Hence \(\nabla f = (P,Q)=\mathbf F\).

Note

This is the multivariable cousin of the other half of the ordinary Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — "\(f(x)=\int_a^x g(t)\,dt\) has \(f'=g\)" — now with paths standing in for intervals. It only works because path-independence lets "the integral accumulated so far" be treated as a genuine function of the endpoint alone, with no leftover dependence on the route to keep track of.

The practical test: matching cross-partials

Constructing a potential by integrating along arbitrary paths is correct but impractical. There is a fast arithmetic shortcut. If \(\mathbf F=(P,Q)=\nabla f\) and \(f\) has continuous second partial derivatives, then \(P=f_x\) and \(Q=f_y\), and mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function don't care about order (Clairaut's theorem):

The cross-partials test

If \(\mathbf F=(P,Q)\) is conservative (with reasonably smooth \(P,Q\)), then necessarily \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\) everywhere on the domain. So: if the two sides ever disagree at some point, \(\mathbf F\) is definitely not conservative. For the swirl field \(\mathbf F=(-y,x)\): \(\partial P/\partial y = -1\) while \(\partial Q/\partial x = 1\) — they disagree everywhere, confirming what the nonzero loop integral in Panel II already showed.

The condition is necessary; whether it's also sufficient depends on the shape of the domain:

Definition — simply connected

A connected open region \(D\) is simply connected if every closed loop drawn in \(D\) can be continuously shrunk down to a single point without ever leaving \(D\). A disk qualifies; the whole plane qualifies. A disk with a single point removed does not — a loop encircling the missing point is snagged on the hole and can't shrink past it. An annulus fails for the same reason.

Sufficiency — with a hypothesis that matters

If \(D\) is simply connected and \(\partial P/\partial y=\partial Q/\partial x\) everywhere on \(D\), then \(\mathbf F\) is conservative on \(D\). The proof is deferred to Panel V, where Green's theorem makes it a two-line argument — and the "simply connected" hypothesis turns out to be exactly load-bearing, not fine print, as the example below shows.

Insight — what's actually going on

This \(\mathbf F\) is \(\nabla\theta\), where \(\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\) is the polar angle. Locally — say, anywhere away from the negative \(x\)-axis — \(\theta\) is a perfectly good, single-valued, smooth function, and \(\nabla\theta=\mathbf F\) exactly there: a local potential genuinely exists almost everywhere. But \(\theta\) cannot be made single-valued on all of \(D\) at once: walk once counterclockwise around the origin, and \(\theta\) climbs by a full \(2\pi\), landing you back at the same point but at a "different" angle value than you left with. There's no way to choose one consistent global branch — and the nonzero loop integral is exactly what's detecting that failure.

Circulation & Green's Theorem

what a nonzero loop integral is measuring, built up literally from a grid of tiny squares

Circulation

The value of \(\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) around a closed curve \(C\) is called the circulation of \(\mathbf F\) around \(C\) — physically, the net tendency of the field to sweep something around that loop. Drop a tiny paddle-wheel into a fluid whose velocity field has nonzero circulation around some point, and the wheel will actually spin; drop it into a field with zero circulation there, and it won't, however fast the fluid nearby happens to be moving in a straight line.

Definition — (2D scalar) curl

Curl is exactly the quantity discovered above: circulation per unit area, in the limit of a shrinking loop. It isn't a formula to memorize — it's the honest leftover once a tiny loop's own zeroth-order contribution cancels against itself.

From tiny squares to Green's theorem, by telescoping

Tile a region \(D\) with a fine grid of these small counterclockwise rectangles, and sum the circulation around every single one. Any edge shared between two neighboring interior cells gets traversed once by each of the two cells that share it — and, because both cells are oriented counterclockwise, in opposite directions. Those two contributions cancel exactly. The only edges that survive the sum are the ones lying on the outer boundary of \(D\), which belong to just one cell each.

Note — reconciling the vortex field

Revisit Panel IV's vortex field on \(D=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\): \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf F=0\) everywhere it's defined, but the unit circle's interior — the disk it would naturally bound — contains the missing origin, so that disk is not entirely contained in \(D\). Green's theorem's hypothesis fails for exactly that loop, so the argument above simply doesn't apply, and the nonzero circulation found earlier is no contradiction at all.

Non-conservative fields still integrate — they just remember the path

None of this means a non-conservative field's line integral is broken or ill-defined; Panel II already computed one directly, by definition, with no shortcut needed. What Green's theorem adds is a second, entirely different shortcut — specifically for closed loops — that trades a possibly awkward boundary integral for a (often much easier) area integral, whether or not the field is conservative anywhere.

The Big Picture

two different collapses from a boundary-less integral down to boundary data — and why that's the same trick, twice

Two "collapses," one shape

Step back from the specific formulas. The gradient theorem (Panel III) took an integral along an entire curve and collapsed it down to information at just the curve's two endpoints. Green's theorem (Panel V) took an integral over an entire two-dimensional region and collapsed it down to information along just the region's boundary curve. Both are instances of a single pattern, and so, it turns out, is the very first Fundamental Theorem of Calculus you ever learned:

1D FTC: ∫_a^b f'(x) dx = f(b) − f(a) boundary of [a,b] = {a, b} Gradient theorem: ∫_C ∇f · dr = f(B) − f(A) boundary of curve C = {A, B} Green's theorem: ∬_D curl F dA = ∮_{∂D} F · dr boundary of region D = curve ∂D same shape every time: ∫(a derivative-like quantity) over a piece = (the original quantity) evaluated on just its boundary
Insight

This is not a coincidence of notation. All three are special cases of a single, far more general fact — the generalized Stokes' theorem — which says, roughly, that integrating a "derivative" of something over a region always equals integrating the original thing over just the region's edge. The classical Stokes' theorem (curl, over a curved surface in 3D) and the Divergence theorem are the next two instances up the dimensional ladder; none of them require a new idea beyond the one built by hand in this essay, only more bookkeeping.

A practical checklist

Faced with an unfamiliar line integral, the questions worth asking, in order:

1. Is the field conservative? Check the cross-partials (Panel IV); if they match and the domain is simply connected, find the potential by direct antidifferentiation and just subtract its values at the two endpoints (Panel III). Done — the shape of the path never mattered.

2. If not, is the curve closed? If so, and the region it bounds lies entirely inside the domain where \(\mathbf F\) is defined, Green's theorem (Panel V) trades the line integral for a double integral of \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf F\) — often the easier computation by far.

3. Otherwise — an open curve, a genuinely non-conservative field — there is no shortcut, and none is needed: parametrize the curve and compute \(\int_a^b \mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) directly, exactly as defined from scratch in Panel II. That definition never stopped being true; it's simply the fallback every shortcut in this essay was built to avoid falling back on.
