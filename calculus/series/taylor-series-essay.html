TAYLOR SERIES

From Slope to Curvature

how much of a function, far from a point, is already hiding in its derivatives at that one point

An infinite, but purely local, dossier

Suppose you're handed an enormous file on a function \(f\): not just its value at one point \(a\), but the value of every one of its derivatives there too — \(f(a)\), \(f'(a)\), \(f''(a)\), \(f'''(a)\), and so on, forever. That's an infinite pile of numbers, but every single one of them is a strictly local fact — a statement about how \(f\) behaves in an infinitesimally small neighborhood of \(a\), and nothing else. The question this essay chases is startling once you actually ask it out loud: how much of \(f\), arbitrarily far away from \(a\), is already secretly determined by that local pile? The answer, remarkably often, is all of it.

Note — picking up a dangling thread

The companion Derivative essay closed by noting that local linearity — the tangent-line approximation \(f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h\) — is "only its opening move," with each additional derivative buying one more order of accuracy. This essay is that longer expansion, carried out to its logical end.

Zeroth order: the crudest possible guess

Start with the least ambitious approximation imaginable: pretend \(f\) is constant near \(a\), \(f(x)\approx f(a)\). This uses exactly one fact — the value \(f(a)\) — and it is correct at exactly one point, \(x=a\), and drifts away from \(f\) immediately on either side, at a rate governed entirely by how fast \(f\) is actually changing.

First order: local linearity, revisited

The fix is the one the Derivative essay already made: use the slope too. \(f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) is the tangent line — it matches \(f\) in height and in slope at \(a\), and consequently tracks \(f\) for noticeably longer before drifting off, at least as long as \(f\) doesn't bend much. But a straight line has zero curvature everywhere, and most functions worth studying bend. The tangent line eventually peels away from any curve that curves, no matter how good the match was right at \(a\).

Insight — the question this whole essay chases

What if you don't stop at slope? A polynomial has as many "knobs" as it has terms — degree \(n\) gives \(n+1\) coefficients to tune. If a straight line can be forced to match \(f\)'s height and slope at \(a\), what stops a quadratic from also being forced to match its curvature there — and a cubic from matching whatever comes after curvature? Nothing does. Taking this all the way, matching every derivative \(f\) has at \(a\), is the entire content of the Taylor series. Local linearity was "match derivative \(0\) and derivative \(1\)." What follows is "match every derivative there is."

The open question this essay answers in three stages

Write the next approximation as \(P_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + c_2(x-a)^2\), with \(c_2\) left as an unknown to be pinned down. What value of \(c_2\) makes \(P_2''(a)\) equal to \(f''(a)\) — the one condition that would make \(P_2\) match \(f\)'s curvature, not just its height and slope? Panel II answers this by brute-force differentiation, and the pattern it finds generalizes to every order, all at once, with no extra cleverness required. Panel III then asks the question that separates a cute algebraic trick from a genuinely powerful theorem: once you let the degree run off to infinity, does the resulting infinite sum actually converge back to \(f\)? The answer is a qualified yes — qualified in a way that turns out to matter a great deal.

Deriving the Formula

why the coefficients are forced to be f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k! — and why the factorial is not decoration

Setting up the matching conditions

Look for a polynomial of degree \(n\), written in the most convenient possible form — powers of \((x-a)\) rather than powers of \(x\), since every derivative will eventually be evaluated at \(x=a\):

The goal, stated precisely: choose the coefficients \(c_0,\dots,c_n\) so that \(P_n^{(j)}(a) = f^{(j)}(a)\) for every \(j=0,1,\dots,n\) — the polynomial and \(f\) agree not just in value at \(a\), but in every derivative up to order \(n\).

What differentiating (x−a)ᵏ, j times, actually does

Differentiate \((x-a)^k\) once: you get \(k(x-a)^{k-1}\). Differentiate it \(j\) times: you get \(k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)(x-a)^{k-j}\), provided \(j\le k\) — a product of \(j\) descending integers times a surviving power of \((x-a)\). Now evaluate at \(x=a\):

Two cases, and both are forced. If \(j<k\), a positive power \((x-a)^{k-j}\) survives, and it vanishes the instant \(x=a\) is substituted. If \(j>k\), the term dies even sooner — differentiating a degree-\(k\) polynomial more than \(k\) times always gives the zero function outright. Only the single case \(j=k\) survives evaluation at \(a\) with a nonzero result, and that result is exactly \(k(k-1)\cdots1 = k!\) — the descending product runs from \(k\) all the way down to \(1\), with nothing left over.

Insight — why the factorial is forced, not decorative

The factorial in Taylor's formula is not a normalization chosen for taste. It is the literal, mechanical byproduct of differentiating a power \(j\) times: each of the \(j\) derivatives brings down one more descending factor, and by the time you've differentiated \((x-a)^k\) exactly \(k\) times, you've multiplied together every integer from \(k\) down to \(1\). Dividing by \(k!\) later is simply undoing that bookkeeping — canceling the exact residue that \(k\)-fold differentiation leaves behind, so that what's left is honestly \(f^{(k)}(a)\) and nothing else.

Uniqueness: there is no other polynomial that would work

Suppose \(Q_n\) is some other degree-\(n\) polynomial that also matches \(f\)'s value and first \(n\) derivatives at \(a\). Let \(D=P_n-Q_n\), a polynomial of degree at most \(n\). Since both \(P_n\) and \(Q_n\) agree with \(f\) in every derivative up to order \(n\) at \(a\), \(D^{(j)}(a)=0\) for every \(j=0,\dots,n\). Write \(D(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n d_k(x-a)^k\); the identical argument above gives \(D^{(j)}(a)=j!\,d_j\), so \(j!\,d_j=0\) for every \(j\), and since \(j!\ne0\) this forces every \(d_j=0\). Hence \(D\equiv0\) and \(Q_n=P_n\) — the Taylor polynomial isn't one convenient choice among several; it's the only degree-\(n\) polynomial that could possibly do the job.

Note — a proof that will be reused

Nothing about this uniqueness argument mentioned \(f\) at all — it's a statement purely about polynomials written in powers of \((x-a)\): such a polynomial is completely determined by its derivatives at \(a\), with no redundancy and no ambiguity anywhere. Panel V returns to this exact argument, unchanged, to prove that the powers \((x-a)^k\) are linearly independent — the same computation, read as a fact about vectors instead of a fact about approximation.

Does the series actually converge back to f?

Now let \(n\to\infty\). Two entirely separate questions are hiding here, and it's easy to conflate them. First: does the numerical series \(\sum_k \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k\) converge at all, for a given \(x\)? That's an ordinary series-convergence question, answerable by the ratio test on the coefficients, and it defines a radius of convergence \(R\) — the series converges (absolutely) for \(|x-a|<R\) and diverges for \(|x-a|>R\). Second, and entirely different: if it converges, does it converge to \(f(x)\) — or to something else?

Warning — smooth is not the same as analytic

Let \(f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}\) for \(x\ne0\) and \(f(0)=0\). This function is \(C^\infty\) — infinitely differentiable everywhere, including at \(0\) — and every single derivative at the origin is exactly zero: \(f^{(k)}(0)=0\) for every \(k\ge0\) (provable by induction: each derivative has the form of a polynomial in \(1/x\) times \(e^{-1/x^2}\), and the exponential decay overwhelms any polynomial growth as \(x\to0\)). The Taylor series of \(f\) at \(0\) is therefore the identically-zero function — it converges everywhere, trivially, to \(0\). But \(f(x)\ne0\) for every \(x\ne0\). The series converges; it just doesn't converge to \(f\), anywhere except the single point \(x=0\) itself.

Definition — analytic vs. merely smooth

A function is analytic at \(a\) if it equals its own Taylor series in some neighborhood of \(a\) — equivalently, if \(R_n(x)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) for \(x\) near \(a\). A function can be \(C^\infty\) (infinitely differentiable, so that every \(f^{(k)}(a)\) exists and the series can be formally written down) without being analytic — \(e^{-1/x^2}\) at \(0\) is the standard witness. Every analytic function is smooth; the converse fails, and it fails for a structural reason, not a pathological one: the derivatives at a single point are, in general, simply not enough information to reconstruct the whole function, and Taylor's theorem is exactly the tool that tells you when they are.

Why a Power Series at All

what's actually gained by trading a function for a sequence of numbers

Turning a differentiable object into an arithmetic one

A computer, at the hardware level, can add, subtract, and multiply. It has no built-in circuit for "take the sine of," "raise \(e\) to the," or "take the natural log of" — those are transcendental operations, defined by limits or differential equations, not by finite arithmetic. Every numerical evaluation of \(\sin(0.7)\) or \(e^{0.3}\) that a machine ever actually performs bottoms out in evaluating a polynomial — typically a truncated Taylor series, or a closely related polynomial tuned by the same underlying idea. The power series isn't a curiosity sitting next to the "real" definition of these functions; for computational purposes, it effectively is the definition, because it's the only form built entirely out of operations a machine can execute directly.

Compressing an uncountable amount of information into a countable list

Knowing a general function \(f\) completely means knowing \(f(x)\) for every one of uncountably many values of \(x\) — an function is, in the worst case, an unimaginably large, unlistable object. Taylor's theorem says something startling about analytic functions specifically: that entire infinite amount of information is already compressed into a single countable sequence, \(f(a), f'(a), f''(a),\dots\) — a list you genuinely could write down, one entry at a time. This is the essay's opening question, answered: for an analytic \(f\), the local pile of derivatives at one point contains the whole function, everywhere within the radius of convergence, and representing \(f\) as a power series is simply making that compression explicit and usable.

Series algebra replaces limit computations

Once a function is written as a power series, calculus operations on it become algebra. Term-by-term differentiation and integration of a power series, within its radius of convergence, reproduce the true derivative and antiderivative exactly:

Panel VIII cashes this in directly: once \(\sin x\)'s series is known, \(\cos x\)'s series falls out by differentiating term by term — no new limit is ever computed, only arithmetic on exponents and coefficients already in hand. This is the same closure property that makes the power series method for differential equations work: substitute an unknown series \(\sum a_k x^k\) into an ODE, differentiate term by term, and match coefficients order by order. A differential problem — genuinely hard, involving limits — becomes a recursive algebraic one, solvable by pure bookkeeping. (The companion differential equations essay develops the calculus side of this machinery in full; the point here is narrower: it's the series representation specifically that turns "solve a differential equation" into "solve a recursion.")

Rigidity: a function pinned down by a shadow of itself

Because Panel II proved the coefficients are forced — there is no other polynomial, hence no other series, that could match \(f\)'s derivatives at \(a\) — an analytic function is extraordinarily rigid. If two functions, both analytic on a connected interval, agree on any small sub-interval (or even just on a sequence of points converging to some point in that interval), their Taylor series at that point are identical, and rigidity propagates: the set of points where all their derivatives agree is both open (by the local Taylor argument) and closed (by continuity), so on a connected domain it's everything. Two analytic functions that agree almost anywhere are forced to agree everywhere they're both defined. This is completely false for merely smooth functions — a smooth bump can be identically zero on one interval and nonzero immediately outside it — which is exactly the extra structure analyticity buys.

A doorway into the complex plane

A power series makes just as much sense with a complex variable as a real one, provided \(|z-a|\) stays inside the radius of convergence — nothing in \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) required \(x\) to be real. This is, very concretely, how \(\sin\), \(\cos\), and \(\exp\) get extended to complex arguments in the first place: not by some new definition, but by plugging a complex number into the same real Taylor series and letting the arithmetic run. Panel VIII's worked payoff — Euler's formula, \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\) — is a genuine consequence of representing these three functions as power series, one that is completely invisible if you only ever think of them as "ratios of triangle sides" or "the function equal to its own derivative."

The Linear Algebra Underneath

Taylor coefficients are coordinates — read off by a completely different mechanism than usual

Polynomials form a vector space, and the powers of (x−a) are a basis

The set of all polynomials is a vector space: add two polynomials, or scale one by a constant, and the result is still a polynomial. The companion Functions as Mappings essay develops exactly what a basis means for a vector space — a spanning, linearly independent set whose linear combinations reach every vector in the space, each in exactly one way. The powers \(\{1,(x-a),(x-a)^2,(x-a)^3,\dots\}\) form precisely such a basis for the space of polynomials, and Panel II already proved the one fact needed to know this:

Insight — the uniqueness proof was a linear-independence proof all along

Panel II showed: if a degree-\(n\) polynomial \(D(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n d_k(x-a)^k\) has \(D^{(j)}(a)=0\) for every \(j=0,\dots,n\), then every \(d_j=0\). Read \(D\equiv0\) as a function instead of "\(D\) matches zero derivatives," and this is exactly the definition of linear independence: the only linear combination of the powers \((x-a)^k\) that produces the zero polynomial is the trivial one. The identical computation that pinned down Taylor's coefficients in Panel II is, read differently, the proof that the monomial basis is genuinely a basis.

Coefficient extraction as a linear functional

For a genuine polynomial, the coordinates \(c_k\) relative to this basis are just its coefficients. The leap Taylor's theorem makes is applying the same basis to a function \(f\) that isn't a polynomial at all — and asking what its "coordinates" ought to be. The answer Panel II already derived is that the \(k\)-th coordinate is extracted by a specific operation: differentiate \(k\) times, evaluate at \(a\), divide by \(k!\).

Define, for each \(k\), the map \(c_k:C^\infty(I)\to\mathbb R\) by \(c_k(f)=\dfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\). This is a linear functional — a linear map out of a function space and into the scalars — because differentiation itself is linear: \((f+g)^{(k)}=f^{(k)}+g^{(k)}\) and \((\lambda f)^{(k)}=\lambda f^{(k)}\), by induction from the sum and constant-multiple rules the companion Derivative essay derives directly from the limit definition. Linearity of \(k\)-fold differentiation makes \(c_k\) linear immediately:

The Taylor map, and its kernel

Bundle every \(c_k\) into a single map sending a function to its entire sequence of coordinates:

Since each coordinate of \(T(f)\) is a linear functional of \(f\), \(T\) itself is a linear map — precisely the "matrix as a recipe" idea from the companion Functions as Mappings essay, one level up: instead of a finite matrix built from where finitely many basis vectors land, \(T\) is an infinite family of coordinate-extracting functionals, one per basis vector \((x-a)^k\), applied to \(f\).

Insight — Panel III's warning, reframed as a kernel

What is \(\ker T\) — the set of functions every one of whose coordinates is zero? Exactly the functions all of whose derivatives vanish at \(a\), and Panel III already exhibited a nonzero one: \(e^{-1/x^2}\). Linear algebra has a name for a linear map with a nontrivial kernel — it isn't injective, meaning distinct inputs can share the same output; here, both \(e^{-1/x^2}\) and the zero function map under \(T\) to the same all-zero sequence. Restricted to the subspace of analytic functions, though, \(T\) is injective: if \(T(f)=0\) for analytic \(f\), then \(f\) equals its own Taylor series near \(a\) — which is identically zero — so \(f\equiv0\) near \(a\), and Panel IV's rigidity argument extends this to \(f\equiv0\) on all of a connected domain. \(T\) is a genuine linear isomorphism from analytic functions onto the ring of convergent power series; it merely fails to be one on the much larger space of smooth functions, and \(e^{-1/x^2}\) is living proof of exactly how it fails.

Functional Analysis

what convergence means for an infinite sum of functions, and differentiation as an operator with its own matrix

From formal algebra to an actual limit

Panel V treated coefficients as algebra — coordinates in a basis. Making \(\sum_k c_k(x-a)^k\) genuinely equal \(f\) requires a notion of limit for a sequence of functions, which is where linear algebra hands off to functional analysis. The relevant statement, standard for power series, is that convergence is uniform on compact subintervals of the radius of convergence: for every \(r<R\),

— the worst-case error over the whole sub-interval shrinks to zero, not merely the error at each individual \(x\) separately. This upgrade from pointwise to uniform convergence is exactly what makes the space of convergent power series behave well: it's what licenses swapping limits with derivatives and integrals term by term, the operation Panel IV leaned on twice.

Note — a genuine normed space

The set of functions analytic near \(a\) and convergent on a disk of radius \(r\), equipped with the sup norm \(\|f\|=\sup_{|x-a|\le r}|f(x)|\), is a normed vector space, and it is complete in that norm — a Banach space, in the vocabulary the companion Jacobian essay uses when it extends differentiability itself from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to arbitrary Banach spaces via the Fréchet derivative. Taylor's theorem, in that language, is the statement that the map \(f\mapsto(P_n)_{n\ge0}\) converges in exactly this norm — not some looser, harder-to-use notion of closeness.

more variables, wider domains, and power series with no calculus in them at all

Higher terms continue the pattern with third derivatives arranged into a rank-\(3\) tensor, and so on; the bookkeeping grows, but the underlying idea, matching every derivative there is at one point, is unchanged.

Analytic continuation: chaining disks together

A power series centered at \(a\) is only guaranteed to represent \(f\) inside its radius of convergence — but nothing stops you from picking a new center \(b\) inside that disk, recomputing a fresh Taylor series there, and getting a new disk of convergence that typically pokes out past the first one. Chaining such disks together, one overlapping the next, can extend a function's known domain far beyond where it was originally defined — the mechanism behind extending functions like the Riemann zeta function or the Gamma function past their original defining formulas. Panel IV's rigidity theorem is what makes this well-defined at all: any two chains of disks that reach the same new point are forced, by uniqueness, to agree there.

Power series with no calculus in them whatsoever

Strip away convergence and derivatives entirely, and a power series is still useful as pure bookkeeping: a generating function is a formal power series whose coefficients are a sequence you actually care about, with no claim that the sum converges to anything in particular. The Fibonacci numbers \(F_0=0,F_1=1,F_2=1,F_3=2,\dots\) have the closed-form generating function

and manipulating the series algebraically — the same "series algebra instead of new limits" idea from Panel IV — extracts facts about the sequence (growth rate, closed formulas, identities between terms) that would be far harder to find by manipulating the recursion \(F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}\) directly. This is Panel V's thesis taken to its most literal extreme: a power series, at bottom, is nothing but a vector's coordinates in the monomial basis, and that idea has content even when no function or limit is anywhere in sight.

Insight — one line to carry away

A Taylor series is what happens when "the tangent line is the best local linear approximation" is refused as a stopping point — every derivative at one point turned into one coordinate of \(f\), in a basis built entirely out of powers of \((x-a)\), extracted not by projection but by the one tool that basis's own structure actually offers: differentiate, evaluate, divide by a factorial that exists purely to undo what the differentiating just did.
