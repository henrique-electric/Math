DOUBLE INTEGRAL

The Double Integral

what "accumulation" means once there are two directions to accumulate along

The one thing an integral is actually for

Set aside the mechanics — antiderivatives, bounds, dx — for a moment. Underneath every integral is a single recurring maneuver: chop the domain into pieces small enough that the integrand is nearly constant on each one, multiply, add them all up, and watch what happens as the pieces shrink to nothing. That maneuver doesn't care how many dimensions the domain has. \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) runs it along a line. The double integral runs the exact same maneuver over a patch of the plane.

The geometric payoff shifts along with the dimension. In one variable, \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is the signed area trapped between a curve and the x-axis. In two variables, a function \(f(x,y)\) traces out a surface hovering over the xy-plane, and the double integral is the signed volume trapped between that surface and a region \(D\) of the plane.

Informally

Cover the region \(D\) with a fine grid of small rectangles. Over each rectangle, the surface is nearly flat, so the sliver of volume above it is nearly a rectangular box: base area times height. Add up every box. As the grid gets finer, that sum should settle on a single number — the true volume. That number is \(\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA\).

ONE DIMENSION TWO DIMENSIONS chop [a,b] into strips chop D into little rectangles strip width Δx, height f(x*) cell area ΔA, height f(x*,y*) sum of thin rectangles sum of thin prisms ↓ ↓ Σ f(x*)Δx → area Σ f(x*,y*)ΔA → volume

What makes this worth a whole essay, rather than a one-line analogy, is that almost nothing about the definition changes — but several things that were automatic in one dimension stop being automatic. There is no longer a single natural "direction" to sweep in; the boundary of \(D\) can be an arbitrary curve rather than two endpoints; and the very question "does the limit exist" now has to survive a much richer space of ways to chop up a 2D region. Chasing down exactly what still works, and why, is the substance of what follows.

Why not just integrate twice, naively?

It's tempting to skip straight to "just integrate with respect to y, then with respect to x" — and for well-behaved functions on rectangles, that instinct turns out to be exactly right (this is Fubini's theorem, Panel III). But stating it as the definition would be circular and fragile: it wouldn't explain why the order doesn't matter, it wouldn't survive regions with curved boundaries, and it wouldn't tell you when the whole enterprise is even legitimate. The honest definition has to start from the same partition-and-limit machinery that defines the 1D integral, run in two dimensions at once. That's Panel II.

Insight

Every genuinely new idea about double integrals — Fubini's theorem, general regions, the change-of-variables formula — is really an answer to the question "how do I turn a 2D accumulation problem into something I can compute with 1D tools?" The double integral itself is the honest, dimension-agnostic definition; everything else is a computational bridge back to single-variable calculus.

Signed volume: what negative values mean

The word "volume" above is doing a specific, signed job, exactly as it does for the single integral. Where \(f(x,y)\ge0\), each Riemann-sum term \(f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}\) is the volume of an honest box sitting above the plane. Where \(f(x,y)<0\), that same term is negative: the surface dips below the xy-plane, and the "box" contributes negative volume. So \(\iint_D f\,dA\) is really (volume above the plane) − (volume below the plane), a net signed quantity — it only equals a literal, physical volume once you already know \(f\ge0\) on all of \(D\). This matters the moment \(f\) represents something that can go negative, such as a velocity, a charge density, or a difference of two heights.

Two equally rigorous routes to the same limit

Panel II states integrability as "the Riemann sums converge, for every partition and every sample choice." There's a second, equivalent way to say the same thing that never has to quantify over sample points at all: bound the sum above and below using the best-case and worst-case heights available on each cell, and require those two bounding sums to squeeze together. This is the Darboux formulation, and it is not a stylistic alternative — it is the version that actually makes the proofs in Panel II and Panel III tractable, rather than merely plausible.

The Formal Definition

partitions, norms, and the limit that has to exist before any of this means anything

Partitioning a rectangle

Let \(R = [a,b]\times[c,d]\) be a closed rectangle. A partition \(\mathcal{P}\) of \(R\) is built from a partition \(a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_m=b\) of \([a,b]\) together with a partition \(c=y_0<y_1<\dots<y_n=d\) of \([c,d]\). Together these slice \(R\) into \(mn\) sub-rectangles \(R_{ij}=[x_{i-1},x_i]\times[y_{j-1},y_j]\), each with area \(\Delta A_{ij} = \Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j\).

Pick any sample point \((x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\in R_{ij}\) and form the double Riemann sum:

Define the norm of the partition, \(\|\mathcal{P}\|\), as the length of the longest diagonal among all the sub-rectangles — not the number of pieces, but the size of the coarsest piece. This is the quantity that has to go to zero.

Definition — the double integral

\(f\) is (Riemann) integrable on \(R\) with integral \(L\) if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(\delta>0\) such that every partition \(\mathcal{P}\) with \(\|\mathcal{P}\|<\delta\), with any choice of sample points, satisfies \(|S(\mathcal{P}) - L| < \varepsilon\). When this \(L\) exists, it is unique, and we write

Read the quantifiers carefully — they're doing all the work. It's not enough that some sequence of grids converges nicely; every sufficiently fine partition, sampled however you like, has to land within \(\varepsilon\) of the same number \(L\). That's a strong requirement, and it's exactly what makes the resulting value \(L\) a property of \(f\) and \(R\) alone — not an artifact of how you happened to chop things up.

Insight

This is the same shape of guarantee that made the single-variable derivative well-defined: there, uniqueness came from forcing the approximation to work for every direction of approach, not just one. Here, uniqueness comes from forcing the sum to converge for every partition and sample choice, not just a convenient sequence of grids. Both are the same mathematical instinct — refuse to let the answer depend on an arbitrary choice — applied to two different limiting processes.

An equivalent formulation: Darboux sums

Quantifying over "every choice of sample point" is correct but awkward to reason with. The Darboux formulation sidesteps sample points entirely by using the best- and worst-case heights available on each sub-rectangle. For bounded \(f\) and sub-rectangle \(R_{ij}\), set

Every genuine Riemann sum \(S(\mathcal P)\), for any sample choice, satisfies \(L(f,\mathcal P)\le S(\mathcal P)\le U(f,\mathcal P)\) — it is trapped between the worst case and the best case on every cell. Refining a partition can only push \(L\) up and \(U\) down, never the reverse, so as partitions refine, the two sums close in on each other from opposite sides.

Darboux's criterion

\(f\) is integrable on \(R\) if and only if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists a partition \(\mathcal P\) with \(U(f,\mathcal P)-L(f,\mathcal P)<\varepsilon\). When this holds, the squeezed common value equals the double integral, and this criterion is provably equivalent to the Riemann definition above. Its advantage is structural: Darboux sums involve no arbitrary sample-point choice, which is exactly what makes them the workhorse behind the integrability proofs that follow — in particular the continuity proof below and the proof of Fubini's theorem in Panel III.

What guarantees the limit exists?

Not every bounded function is integrable. The clean, practically-universal sufficient condition is continuity:

Note

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed, bounded rectangle \(R\), then \(f\) is integrable on \(R\). More generally (this is the fact that actually licenses the "general regions" of Panel III), a bounded function is Riemann integrable on \(R\) as long as its set of discontinuities has measure zero — informally, a set thin enough that it can be covered by rectangles of arbitrarily small total area. A finite union of smooth curves inside \(R\) always qualifies, which is precisely the situation when you integrate a continuous \(f\) over a region \(D\subset R\) by declaring it \(0\) outside \(D\): the discontinuity you introduce sits exactly on \(\partial D\), a curve.

Definition — measure zero

A set \(Z\subset\mathbb R^2\) has measure zero if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a countable collection of rectangles covering \(Z\) whose total area is less than \(\varepsilon\). Points, finite sets, and — the case that matters here — any finite union of smooth curves all qualify: a curve can be covered by a chain of thin rectangles hugging it, and shrinking their width shrinks their total area to nothing, no matter how long the curve is. A measure-zero set can still be uncountably infinite; "measure zero" is a statement about total area, not about how many points the set contains.

Why continuity is enough: a proof sketch

The Darboux criterion turns "continuous implies integrable" into a short, genuinely illuminating argument rather than a fact to take on faith. Because \(R\) is closed and bounded and \(f\) is continuous, \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \(R\) (Heine–Cantor theorem): given \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a single \(\delta>0\) that works everywhere on \(R\) at once, so that any two points within distance \(\delta\) of each other have \(f\)-values within \(\varepsilon\) of each other.

Take any partition \(\mathcal P\) with norm \(\|\mathcal P\|<\delta\). On each sub-rectangle \(R_{ij}\), every pair of points is within \(\delta\) of each other, so the oscillation \(M_{ij}-m_{ij}\) is at most \(\varepsilon\). Then

Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary and \(\text{Area}(R)\) is a fixed number, \(U-L\) can be forced below any target — exactly the Darboux criterion. The whole proof is one inequality, and it never had to name a specific favorable sequence of partitions; uniform continuity handed us a single \(\delta\) that works for every sufficiently fine partition simultaneously — the same "no dependence on an arbitrary choice" theme running through this entire essay.

Warning

Boundedness alone is not enough. Let \(f(x,y)=1\) if both \(x\) and \(y\) are rational, and \(0\) otherwise, on \(R=[0,1]^2\). Every sub-rectangle of every partition, no matter how fine, contains both rational and irrational coordinate pairs, so it contains points where \(f=1\) and points where \(f=0\). The Riemann sum can therefore be forced to equal \(1\) (sample only rational points) or \(0\) (sample only irrational points) for the same partition — indeed \(M_{ij}=1\) and \(m_{ij}=0\) on every cell of every partition, so \(U(f,\mathcal P)-L(f,\mathcal P)=\text{Area}(R)\) never shrinks. The limit in the definition above does not exist — this function is simply not Riemann integrable, however fine you make the grid.

That's the exception, not the rule — repeat the computation for \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) instead (Panel V's second interactive lab does exactly this, numerically) and a coarse grid only approximates the true value, improving steadily as the partition refines. Comparing the two cases is the fastest way to feel why the definition insists on a limit as the norm shrinks to zero, rather than accepting any single partition's answer.

Fubini & General Regions

Fubini's theorem: the bridge back to single-variable calculus

The partition-limit definition is correct but nearly useless for actually computing anything — nobody evaluates a double limit of double sums by hand. Fubini's theorem is the bridge: for \(f\) continuous on \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\),

Two claims are bundled into that statement, and it's worth separating them: (1) the double integral equals an iterated pair of ordinary single-variable integrals, and (2) it does so regardless of which variable you integrate first.

Why this is true: Cavalieri's principle made algebraic

Freeze \(x\) at some fixed value. The function \(y\mapsto f(x,y)\) is now an ordinary single-variable function, and \(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\) is the area of the cross-section of the solid sliced at that \(x\) — call this cross-sectional area function \(A(x)\). Fubini's theorem says the volume is obtained by accumulating these cross-sectional areas along \(x\):

Insight

This is exactly Cavalieri's principle from solid geometry — a solid's volume is determined by the areas of its parallel cross-sections — restated as an algebraic identity rather than an appeal to intuition. Slicing vertically first and integrating the resulting areas horizontally (or vice versa) are two different bookkeeping strategies for summing the same infinitesimal volume elements \(f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\), which is why the two iterated integrals are forced to agree.

VERTICAL SLICING (dy first) HORIZONTAL SLICING (dx first) for each fixed x: for each fixed y: A(x) = ∫ f(x,y) dy (area) A(y) = ∫ f(x,y) dx (area) then V = ∫ A(x) dx then V = ∫ A(y) dy same volume V either way — Fubini's theorem

A genuine proof, via Darboux sums

The Cavalieri picture is convincing, but the Darboux machinery from Panel II upgrades it into an actual proof for continuous \(f\) on \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\). Fix a grid of sub-rectangles \(R_{ij}\) as before. For each fixed \(x\) in the \(i\)-th column, the cross-sectional area function is itself an ordinary single integral, so it's trapped between its own lower and upper Darboux sums along that column:

Integrating this chain of inequalities over \(x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\) and summing over every column \(i\) produces exactly \(L(f,\mathcal P) \le \int_a^b A(x)\,dx \le U(f,\mathcal P)\). But \(\iint_R f\,dA\) is also trapped between \(L(f,\mathcal P)\) and \(U(f,\mathcal P)\), by definition of the double integral. Since \(f\) is continuous, Panel II's proof sketch shows \(U(f,\mathcal P)-L(f,\mathcal P)\) can be forced below any \(\varepsilon\) by refining the partition. Two numbers squeezed into the same shrinking interval must coincide — hence \(\int_a^b A(x)\,dx=\iint_R f\,dA\), with no appeal to physical intuition required anywhere in the argument.

Warning — Fubini needs integrability, not merely that the iterated integrals exist

It's possible for both iterated integrals \(\int\int f\,dy\,dx\) and \(\int\int f\,dx\,dy\) to exist as ordinary single-variable computations and yet disagree, when \(f\) is not integrable over \(R\) as a genuine double integral. The standard counterexample is \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\) on \((0,1]\times(0,1]\): direct computation gives \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 f\,dy\,dx = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) but \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 f\,dx\,dy = -\tfrac{\pi}{4}\). The culprit is that \(f\) is unbounded as \((x,y)\to(0,0)\), so it fails the hypotheses of Fubini's theorem outright — continuity (or at least boundedness plus integrability) is not fine print here, it is load-bearing, exactly as the proof sketch above required it at the step where the Darboux gap was forced to zero.

General regions: letting the bounds curve

Real domains are rarely rectangles. The extension is to let the inner limits of integration be functions rather than constants, while the outer limits stay fixed numbers:

Warning

This asymmetry is the single most common source of setup errors: the outer bounds of an iterated integral must be absolute constants. If a variable survives into the outer limits, the "answer" you get back is a function, not a volume — a sure sign the bounds were read off incorrectly.

The most reliable way to set these up is geometric, not algebraic: sketch \(D\), then imagine a scanning arrow — vertical for Type I, horizontal for Type II — sweeping across the region. The curve the arrow enters through is the lower inner bound; the curve it exits through is the upper inner bound; the range the arrow's base sweeps over is the pair of outer constants.

Note

A region might not be classifiable as a single Type I or Type II piece — the "entering" or "exiting" curve can switch partway across. When that happens, additivity of regions (Panel II) is the escape hatch: split \(D\) at the point where the bounding curve changes, treat each piece as its own Type I or Type II region, and add the results. Often, switching the slicing direction entirely (Type I ↔ Type II) avoids the split altogether — worth checking before committing to extra algebra.

Changing order to rescue an unsolvable integral

Order of integration is not just a bookkeeping choice — sometimes only one order is computable at all. Consider \(I=\int_0^1\!\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy\): the inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, full stop. Reading the bounds geometrically, this is the triangle with vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\), bounded by \(x=y\), \(x=1\), \(y=0\), \(y=1\). The Mean Value Theorem for double integrals

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed, connected, bounded region \(D\), there exists at least one point \((x_0,y_0)\in D\) where \(f\) exactly equals the average value of \(f\) over \(D\):

The proof is another squeeze, and a short one: continuity on a closed, bounded set guarantees \(f\) attains a minimum \(m\) and maximum \(M\) somewhere on \(D\), so monotonicity (Panel II) gives \(m\cdot\text{Area}(D) \le \iint_D f\,dA \le M\cdot\text{Area}(D)\), which places \(\bar f\) between \(m\) and \(M\). Continuity then guarantees (via the Intermediate Value Theorem, applied along a path connecting the two extremum points) that \(f\) actually attains that intermediate value somewhere in \(D\) — it isn't merely sandwiched between two values \(f\) achieves elsewhere, it hits \(\bar f\) exactly, at some genuine point of the region.

Insight

This theorem is what makes "average value," "centroid," and — read through a probability lens — "expected value" all meaningful concepts rather than just formulas: each is defined as exactly this kind of area-normalized double integral, and the Mean Value Theorem is the guarantee that the resulting number is not an abstraction floating outside the region, but a value \(f\) genuinely takes on somewhere inside \(D\). Panel VI leans on this fact directly.

Warping the Grid

changing coordinates, and the factor you must pay to do it honestly

Why change coordinates at all

Cartesian coordinates are native to straight lines. A disk, forced into Cartesian bounds, becomes a nest of square roots: \(\int_{-1}^1\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{\sqrt{1-x^2}} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\). Under a substitution \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), that same disk becomes the plain rectangle \(r\in[0,1],\ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\). The region got simpler. But a substitution changes more than the labels of the axes — it changes how much area a fixed-size coordinate cell represents, and any honest change of variables has to account for that.

Deriving the price geometrically: polar coordinates

A small polar "rectangle" — inner radius \(r\), outer radius \(r+\Delta r\), angular width \(\Delta\theta\) — is really an annular sector, not a square. Its area is the outer sector minus the inner sector, \(\tfrac12(r+\Delta r)^2\Delta\theta - \tfrac12 r^2\Delta\theta\), which simplifies to \(\bar r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) where \(\bar r\) is the average radius. As \(\Delta r\to0\), \(\bar r\to r\), giving the differential area element:

That lone factor of \(r\) is not decorative — it is the exact reason polar coordinate cells shrink to nothing near the origin and swell far from it, even though \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta\theta\) never change.

The general price: the Jacobian determinant

Polar coordinates are one instance of a completely general phenomenon. Let \(T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))\) be a smooth, invertible map sending a region \(D'\) in the \(uv\)-plane onto \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane. Near any point, \(T\) is well-approximated by its Jacobian matrix — a linear map that sends a small square to a small parallelogram, scaling area locally by the factor \(|\det J_T|\). Summing these locally-rescaled pieces gives the change of variables formula:

Insight

Every "extra factor" you've ever multiplied in after a substitution — the lone r in polar coordinates, the extra constants in a linear rescaling — is the same object: \(|\det J_T|\), the local area-scaling factor of the coordinate change. Polar coordinates aren't a special trick with its own rule to memorize; they're the \(T(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\) case of one universal formula, and computing \(\det J_T = r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta = r\) recovers \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\) by direct calculation rather than the geometric annulus argument above — the two derivations agree because they're describing the same fact from different angles.

Warning

Unlike the signed determinant that tracks orientation elsewhere in linear algebra, the change-of-variables formula uses \(|\det J_T|\) — absolute value. Area is never negative, so a coordinate change that happens to reverse orientation (like a reflection) still contributes a positive area-scaling factor. Dropping the absolute value is a common and consequential setup error.

A famous payoff: the Gaussian integral

The function \(e^{-x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative, so \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx\) resists single-variable methods entirely. Squaring it converts the problem into a double integral over the whole plane, \(I^2=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy\), and the combination \(x^2+y^2\) is exactly \(r^2\) — a signal to switch to polar coordinates:

The extra factor of \(r\) — the very quantity derived above as the price of polar coordinates — is precisely what turns an intractable exponential into something a plain \(u\)-substitution finishes off. Without paying that price honestly, the computation gives the wrong answer despite looking algebraically plausible.

A general linear substitution

Polar coordinates are the most famous change of variables, but linear substitutions are the ones reached for most often in practice — typically to tame a combination like \(x+y\) or \(x-y\) that recurs throughout an integrand or its region's boundary. Let \(u=x+y\), \(v=x-y\), so \(x=\tfrac{u+v}{2}\), \(y=\tfrac{u-v}{2}\). Computing the Jacobian of this inverse map directly:

Note — the reciprocal relationship

Here \(|\det J_{T^{-1}}|=\tfrac12\); computing the forward map's Jacobian directly from \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\) gives \(\det J_T=-2\), so \(|\det J_T|\cdot|\det J_{T^{-1}}|=1\). This is not a coincidence specific to this example. For any invertible smooth map, \(J_{T^{-1}}(T(p)) = \big[J_T(p)\big]^{-1}\) — the Inverse Function Theorem, from the companion Jacobian essay — and \(\det(A^{-1})=1/\det(A)\) for any invertible matrix \(A\). Whichever direction is more convenient to differentiate, the other Jacobian determinant is just its reciprocal, with no separate computation needed.

Composing changes of variables

Nothing prevents chaining two substitutions — shift to \((u,v)\), then again to \((p,q)\) — and the bookkeeping for the combined area-scaling factor is exactly the chain rule for Jacobians: if \(T=T_2\circ T_1\), then \(J_T = J_{T_2}\cdot J_{T_1}\) (each evaluated at the appropriate point), so \(\det J_T = \det J_{T_2}\cdot\det J_{T_1}\), since the determinant of a matrix product is the product of the determinants. Composing coordinate changes composes their local area-scaling factors by plain multiplication — there is never a need to re-derive a combined formula from scratch; the two factors already in hand simply multiply.

Elliptical coordinates: stretching polar unevenly

Polar coordinates are the natural substitution for circular symmetry. For a region shaped like an ellipse instead, stretch each polar axis independently: \(x=ar\cos\theta,\ y=br\sin\theta\). The Jacobian determinant picks up both stretch factors:

Setting \(f\equiv1\) and integrating over \(r\in[0,1],\ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\) gives \(\iint 1\cdot ab\,r\,dr\,d\theta = ab\pi\) — the area of an ellipse with semi-axes \(a,b\), recovered in two lines from a substitution built by inspection, rather than memorized as an isolated formula.

Warning — the map must be injective, or the overlap must be accounted for

The change-of-variables formula assumes \(T\) is a one-to-one correspondence between \(D'\) and \(D\), except possibly on a measure-zero set. Polar coordinates already illustrate the subtlety: \(T(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\) collapses the entire segment \(r=0\) to the single point \((0,0)\), and the rays \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi\) describe the same points — both are measure-zero failures of injectivity, harmless to the formula, exactly like the curve-shaped discontinuity sets from Panel II's integrability discussion. But if a substitution folds a positive-area piece of \(D'\) onto itself more than once, applying the formula naively double-counts that region; the fix is to restrict \(D'\) to a piece on which \(T\) is genuinely injective before integrating, then handle the remaining pieces (if any) separately.

That gap, and its shrinking as \(\Delta\to0\), is the entire content of why \(|\det J_T|\) is the correct — and only the local — area-scaling factor to use inside an integral.

Try the polar map with P dragged close to \(u=0\): the predicted parallelogram collapses toward a sliver, matching the geometric fact from Panel IV that polar cells shrink to nothing near the origin. Try shear: the parallelogram tilts but its area never changes as you drag P around, since \(\det J\) is constant for a linear map — the grid distorts in shape but never in local area.

What It Actually Computes

beyond volume — mass, moments, probability, and where this generalizes to

From height to density: physical accumulation

Strip away the "volume under a surface" picture and the double integral is really just the general-purpose tool for continuously accumulating a quantity over a 2D region. Let a flat plate (a lamina) occupy region \(D\), with mass density \(\rho(x,y)\) (mass per unit area) varying from point to point. Summing infinitesimal masses \(dm=\rho\,dA\) over \(D\) gives the total mass, and weighting by position before summing gives the moments that locate the centroid:

Nothing here is new mathematics — it's the same "chop, weight, sum, limit" machinery from Panel I, with \(\rho\,dA\) standing in for \(f\,dA\).

Moment of inertia and radius of gyration

The moment of inertia about an axis, \(I=\iint_D r(x,y)^2\,\rho(x,y)\,dA\) with \(r\) the distance to that axis, measures resistance to rotational acceleration the way mass measures resistance to linear acceleration — it follows the exact same accumulation pattern as \(M\) and the centroid, just with a different weight attached to each infinitesimal piece of mass. Dividing by the total mass and taking a square root gives the radius of gyration,

— the single radius at which all of the lamina's mass could be concentrated in a thin ring and reproduce the identical moment of inertia. It is, in a precise sense, a mass-weighted "average distance from the axis," exactly parallel to how the centroid is a mass-weighted average position.

Surface area as a double integral

A double integral doesn't only measure the volume under a surface \(z=g(x,y)\) — a close cousin measures the surface's own area. Over a small cell of dimensions \(\Delta x\times\Delta y\), the surface is approximated by the tangent plane spanned by the vectors \((1,0,g_x)\) and \((0,1,g_y)\); the parallelogram those two vectors span has area \(\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\) — the same "a linear map distorts a small cell into a parallelogram, and the distortion factor is computable from the local derivative data" idea from Panel IV, now one dimension up, with the cross-product magnitude standing in for the Jacobian determinant. From density to probability

If \(f(x,y)\ge0\) is a joint probability density for two continuous random variables and \(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} f\,dA = 1\), then for any region \(E\), \(\iint_E f(x,y)\,dA\) is the probability that the pair \((X,Y)\) lands in \(E\). The Gaussian integral from Panel IV is not a curiosity here — it's the normalizing constant of the 2D normal distribution, and the same polar-coordinates trick that evaluated it is the standard route to deriving that constant in the first place.

Marginal densities: integrating out a variable

Given a joint density \(f(x,y)\), Fubini's theorem (Panel III) is exactly the tool that recovers the density of \(X\) alone: integrate \(y\) out entirely,

\(X\) and \(Y\) are called independent precisely when the joint density factors as \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\) — in which case the double integral of any product \(g(x)h(y)\) against \(f\) itself factors into a product of two ordinary single integrals, \(\iint g(x)h(y)f(x,y)\,dA = \Big(\int g\,f_X\,dx\Big)\Big(\int h\,f_Y\,dy\Big)\), collapsing a 2D computation back into two independent 1D ones. This is Fubini's theorem showing up as a modeling assumption about the world, not merely a computational trick for evaluating integrals.

Note

All of these readings — volume, mass, probability — are the same integral with a different interpretation attached to \(f\). This is worth sitting with: the double integral doesn't "know" whether it's computing a volume or a probability. The mathematics is agnostic; the meaning is supplied entirely by what you decide the integrand represents.

Where this generalizes to

Everything here extends to a third dimension with essentially no new ideas, only more bookkeeping: partitions become boxes, \(dA\) becomes \(dV\), Fubini's theorem becomes three nested integrals in any order, and the change-of-variables formula keeps the identical shape with a \(3\times3\) Jacobian determinant in place of a \(2\times2\) one. Push further, to \(n\) dimensions or to infinite-dimensional integration theory, and the same partition-and-limit skeleton is still recognizably present — though the Riemann framework itself eventually gives way to the more robust Lebesgue integral, which is built by partitioning the range of \(f\) rather than its domain, precisely to fix the kind of integrability failure seen in Panel II's rational-indicator example.

Note — a forward glance

Double integrals over a region \(D\) are also tied to line integrals around \(D\)'s boundary curve by Green's theorem: \(\iint_D\!\left(\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA = \oint_{\partial D} P\,dx+Q\,dy\). One immediate corollary converts plain area into a boundary integral, \(\text{Area}(D)=\tfrac12\oint_{\partial D}(x\,dy-y\,dx)\) — a reminder that the double integral is not an isolated topic but one node in the larger network connecting differentiation, integration, and geometry that the rest of multivariable calculus is built from.

Insight

One line to carry away: the double integral is not a new idea bolted onto single-variable calculus — it is the same partition-sample-sum-limit machinery, run over a two-dimensional domain, and every technique in this essay (Fubini, general regions, change of variables) exists purely to translate that 2D limit back into computations a single-variable toolkit already knows how to finish.
