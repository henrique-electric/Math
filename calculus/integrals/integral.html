THE INTEGRAL

Total Accumulation

how a total measured against a constant rate becomes a total measured against a rate that never stops changing

Two kinds of total

Arithmetic hands you an exact total the moment a rate holds steady. Drive at a constant 50 mph for 2 hours and you have gone exactly 100 miles — no subtlety, just a product: rate times elapsed time. Geometrically that product is an area, the area of a rectangle whose height is the rate and whose width is the duration, the same quantity that measures the area swept out under any horizontal line on a graph.

Definition — total at a constant rate

For a quantity accumulating at a constant rate \(r\) over an interval of length \(L\), the total accumulated is \(r\cdot L\). Geometrically it is the area of the rectangle of height \(r\) and width \(L\) sitting above that interval.

The paradox of a total with no constant rate

But a car's speedometer rarely sits still. Velocity climbs through a green light, eases for a curve, and the honest total distance travelled is the area under a curve that keeps bending — not a rectangle at all, but some irregular region with a curved top edge. "Rate times time" cannot be applied wholesale anymore, because there is no single rate to multiply by. The obvious fix is to chop the trip into pieces so short that velocity is essentially constant on each one — but "essentially constant" is not "constant," and any residual wobble, multiplied across infinitely many pieces, seems like it ought to matter.

Push the chopping to its limit and a genuine paradox appears, a mirror image of the one that opens the companion derivative essay. Shrink each piece down to a single instant, and the "area" contributed by that one instant is a rectangle of zero width — which has zero area, no matter how tall its height. Zero, added to itself infinitely many times, is still zero by every ordinary rule of arithmetic. If the whole region really is nothing but infinitely many zero-width instants stacked side by side, how can the total area be a positive number at all?

Insight — the illusion of "infinitely many slices"

The integral does not actually add up infinitely many zero-width slices, and it does not really contain a step where "\(0\) added to itself infinitely often" becomes positive — no such step exists, because \(0\times\infty\) is not an arithmetic operation with a defined answer. Instead the integral studies the trend of ordinary finite sums, built from slices of perfectly positive width, and asks what single number those sums are converging toward as the slices are made narrower and more numerous. "Total accumulated quantity" is the name for that destination, not for a summation that ever literally reaches infinitely many terms.

Rectangles closing in on the exact area

Geometrically, a finite sum of rectangle areas is only ever an approximation to the true area under a curve — some rectangles poke out above the curve, others fall short below it. As the rectangles are made narrower and more numerous, that overshoot-and-undershoot shrinks, and for any reasonably well-behaved curve the sum approaches a limiting value: the exact area under the curve. That limiting sum is precisely the total accumulated quantity.

Turning "make the rectangles narrower and more numerous" into a precise piece of algebra, one that pins down the exact area as a single number rather than a vague destination, is the entire content of Panel II.

Note — what this essay assumes, and what it redeems

This essay leans on the same limit machinery built up in the companion derivative essay, and it redeems a promise made at that essay's close: the total accumulated quantity is approached through exactly the strategy used there for the tangent slope — an ordinary, finite quantity, made exact by watching what it approaches as a shrinking parameter goes to zero. Where the derivative slid a second point toward a first, the integral narrows a rectangle's width toward nothing while multiplying their number without bound.

The Limit Definition

turning "narrower, more numerous rectangles" into an algebraic formula that pins down the integral exactly

Partitions, subintervals, sample points

Fix an interval \([a,b]\) and chop it into \(n\) pieces using a partition \(P\): a chain of points \(a=x_0\lt x_1\lt x_2\lt\cdots\lt x_n=b\). Each piece \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) has width \(\Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}\), and on each piece a single sample point \(x_i^{*}\) is chosen, anywhere in \([x_{i-1},x_i]\), to stand in for the function's height across that whole piece.

f(x_i*) ●┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┐ │ │ height = f(x_i*) │ │ 0 ───┴────────┴─── └─ Δx_i ─┘ one rectangle's signed area = f(x_i*) · Δx_i

The signed area of that one rectangle is \(f(x_i^{*})\cdot\Delta x_i\) — positive where the curve sits above the axis, negative where it dips below, exactly the bookkeeping needed so that regions below the axis subtract from the total rather than being counted as unsigned area. Summing every rectangle's contribution gives the Riemann sum:

This is exactly the constant-rate formula from Panel I, applied piecewise and added up. As the partition is refined — every \(\Delta x_i\) shrinking, \(n\) growing without bound — the Riemann sum traces out the sequence of stairstep approximations from the ascii diagram in Panel I, and the integral is defined to be wherever that sequence is headed.

Definition of the definite integral

Definition — the definite integral

The definite integral of \(f\) over \([a,b]\), written \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), is the limit of the Riemann sum as the partition's mesh — the width of its widest subinterval, written \(\|P\|\) — shrinks to zero:

— provided this limit exists and comes out the same number no matter which partitions or which sample points \(x_i^{*}\) are used along the way. If it does, \(f\) is said to be Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\), and the integral is the exact signed area between the curve and the axis. Leibniz's notation is not decorative: \(\int\) is a stretched-out \(S\), for "sum," and \(dx\) is what \(\Delta x_i\) becomes once it is pushed all the way to the limit — the entire symbol is a fossil of exactly the Riemann sum it replaces.

Left, right, and midpoint sums must agree

Because this is a genuine limit over every admissible choice of sample point, three natural special cases — always sampling at the left endpoint, always at the right endpoint, always at the midpoint of each subinterval — must all converge to the same number for the integral to exist at all. For a reasonably tame function they do, and the gap between the left-sum and right-sum approximations is exactly the kind of overshoot-and-undershoot visible in Panel I's ascii diagram, closing as the mesh shrinks. Panel V exhibits a function so wild that even this much agreement fails.

Two languages: Riemann and Darboux

As with the derivative, this idea was formalized in two dominant ways, and each earns its keep in different settings.

Riemann's formulation, given above, is the more intuitive one: pick any partition, pick any sample points, sum the rectangles, and ask what the sums approach. It matches the picture in Panel I almost exactly.

Darboux's formulation replaces "pick a sample point" with the two extreme choices available on each subinterval, removing the sample-point freedom entirely and replacing it with the subinterval's own infimum and supremum:

\(L(P)\) is the lower sum, built from the shortest possible rectangles that still touch the curve from below; \(U(P)\) is the upper sum, built from the tallest. Every Riemann sum for that same partition, no matter which sample points were used, is trapped between them: \(L(P)\le S(P)\le U(P)\). Darboux integrability asks for the largest lower sum over all partitions to meet the smallest upper sum over all partitions — the upper and lower integrals agreeing exactly — and this turns out to be fully equivalent to Riemann's definition. It is not the more intuitive picture, but, like Leibniz notation for the derivative, it is the version later theorems lean on, because "does this one number sandwich every rectangle from above and below" is easier to reason about than "does this limit agree for every conceivable sample-point choice."

Continuity is sufficient — but, this time, not necessary

The companion derivative essay proved that continuity is necessary for differentiability but not sufficient for it. Integrability inverts that relationship, and the inversion is worth sitting with.

Continuity turns out to be sufficient. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), it is automatically uniformly continuous there (a standard consequence of \([a,b]\) being closed and bounded, usually proved separately as the Heine–Cantor theorem): for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a \(\delta>0\) such that any two points within \(\delta\) of each other have outputs within \(\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}\) of each other, regardless of where in \([a,b]\) those points sit. Choose a partition with mesh smaller than that \(\delta\). On every subinterval, any two points are within \(\delta\) of each other by construction, so \(M_i - m_i \le \frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}\) on every piece, and summing:

Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, the upper and lower integrals can be squeezed arbitrarily close together, forcing them to be equal: continuous functions on closed, bounded intervals are always Riemann integrable.

But continuity is not necessary. A function can fail to be continuous at a handful of points — even a step function with an outright jump — and still be perfectly integrable, because a finite number of misbehaving points cannot stop the upper and lower sums from converging together; each bad point only ever spoils one shrinking subinterval's worth of area, and that contribution vanishes in the limit right along with the mesh.

Insight — the arrow flips

For the derivative: differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous, always; continuous \(\not\Rightarrow\) differentiable, as \(|x|\) showed. For the integral, the implication runs the other direction: continuous \(\Rightarrow\) integrable, always; but integrable \(\not\Rightarrow\) continuous, as a plain step function shows. Panel V pushes this gap to its breaking point — a bounded function so pathologically discontinuous that no amount of refining the partition ever forces the upper and lower sums together, the direct counterpart to the derivative essay's nowhere-differentiable Weierstrass function.

The Fundamental Theorem

why accumulating a rate and then differentiating it hands the rate straight back

The accumulation function

Fix a starting point \(a\) and let the far endpoint slide. Define the accumulation function:

\(A(x)\) is the running total swept out from \(a\) up to \(x\) — a brand-new function, built out of \(f\) by the integral, in exactly the way the derivative built \(f'\) out of \(f\). The question this panel answers is the deepest one calculus asks: if \(f\) is a rate, and \(A\) is the total that rate accumulates, what is the rate of change of the total itself? The answer is not "some new function requiring new machinery" — it is \(f\) again, exactly.

FTC Part 1: accumulation, differentiated, is the original rate

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \(A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is differentiable on \([a,b]\), and \(A'(x) = f(x)\).

Before chasing the algebra, look at what is actually being claimed. \(A(x+h)-A(x)\) is the sliver of new area swept out as the accumulation window grows from \(x\) to \(x+h\) — and for \(h\) small, that sliver looks almost exactly like a thin rectangle: width \(h\), height roughly \(f(x)\), the height the curve happens to have right at the sliver's left edge. "Almost exactly" is the entire content of the theorem: the sliver's average height, \(\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}\), converges to the curve's height at the single point \(x\) as the sliver's width shrinks to nothing — which is precisely what "\(A'(x)=f(x)\)" says.

FTC Part 2: evaluating a total without ever summing a rectangle

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

If \(F\) is any function with \(F'=f\) on \([a,b]\) — any antiderivative of \(f\) — then \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)\).

Part 1 already produced one antiderivative of \(f\): the function \(A\) itself. If \(F\) is any other antiderivative, then \((F-A)'(x) = F'(x)-A'(x) = f(x)-f(x) = 0\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\). A function whose derivative is zero everywhere on an interval is constant there — a standard consequence of the Mean Value Theorem, established in most treatments of the derivative and not reproduced here. So \(F(x) = A(x) + C\) for some fixed constant \(C\), and that constant cancels the instant the function is evaluated at two points and subtracted:

Insight — where the rigour was actually hiding

This version makes visible exactly which theorem does the load-bearing work. The Mean Value Theorem is what converts "rate times tiny width" from an approximation into an exact local statement — it is the reason the sliver picture from Part 1's proof can be made to hold with equality rather than merely in the limit. Panel II's integrability guarantee is what then licenses trusting a sum built from MVT's oddly-placed, not-freely-chosen sample points to converge to the very same limit as every other Riemann sum. Nothing here required guessing that \(F(b)-F(a)\) and \(\int_a^b f\) might coincide and then checking it after the fact; the equality falls straight out of telescoping algebra plus one theorem about derivatives, applied one small piece at a time.

Insight — the two-way bridge

This is the promise the derivative essay closed on, redeemed in full: differentiation and integration are inverse operations. Part 1 says integrating and then differentiating gets you back where you started, \(\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)\). Part 2 turns that fact into a computational shortcut — it means nobody has to take the limit of a Riemann sum by hand ever again. Find any one antiderivative, however it is found, and the entire infinite refining-rectangles process from Panels I and II collapses into a single subtraction.

The derivative essay proved, from the limit definition, that \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[x^n\big] = nx^{n-1}\). When It Doesn't Exist

boundedness is necessary but not sufficient — a taxonomy of the ways accumulation can fail to converge

Unbounded functions break the sum outright

If \(f\) is unbounded on \([a,b]\), no partition can save it: some subinterval will always contain points where \(f\) grows arbitrarily large, so the sample point \(x_i^{*}\) on that piece can be chosen to make \(f(x_i^{*})\Delta x_i\) — and hence the whole Riemann sum — as large as desired, no matter how the rest of the partition is arranged. The limit in Panel II's definition cannot settle on a single number when the sums themselves refuse to settle at all. Boundedness on \([a,b]\) is therefore necessary for Riemann integrability. It is not, on its own, sufficient.

The Dirichlet function: a taxonomy-breaking monster

Define \(D(x)=1\) when \(x\) is rational and \(D(x)=0\) when \(x\) is irrational, on \([0,1]\). This function is perfectly bounded — it only ever takes the values \(0\) and \(1\) — yet it fails to be integrable in the most total way possible. Between any two distinct real numbers, no matter how close together, there sits both a rational number and an irrational one (both sets are dense in \(\mathbb{R}\)). So on every subinterval of every partition, no matter how fine, \(D\) still takes both values \(0\) and \(1\):

EVERY PARTITION, NO MATTER HOW FINE: U(P) = 1 ───────────────────────── (sup = 1 on every subinterval, forever) the gap never closes L(P) = 0 ───────────────────────── (inf = 0 on every subinterval, forever) upper integral = 1, lower integral = 0, 1 ≠ 0 — not integrable, at any scale

Every lower sum is exactly \(0\) and every upper sum is exactly \(1\), for every partition without exception — refining the mesh doesn't even narrow the gap, let alone close it. The upper and lower integrals are stuck at \(1\) and \(0\) respectively, and \(D\) is not Riemann integrable on any subinterval of \([0,1]\) either.

Warning — the monster of integration

Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet introduced this function in 1829, decades before Weierstrass's nowhere-differentiable curve. It is, in its own way, a more total failure than Weierstrass's: the derivative essay's monster fails to have a tangent line at every point, but its upper and lower difference quotients can still be finite and merely disagree. Dirichlet's function's upper and lower sums disagree by the maximum possible amount, \(1\), at every scale, from the very first partition onward — there is no refining process to even begin.

Note — the exception, not the rule, resolved

Riemann's definition partitions the domain — the \(x\)-axis — into pieces. Henri Lebesgue's 1902 integral partitions the range instead, weighting each output value by the "measure" (a generalized notion of length) of the set of inputs producing it. The rationals are countable, and every countable subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) has Lebesgue measure zero, so the Lebesgue integral of \(D\) is simply \(1\times(\text{measure }0) + 0\times(\text{measure }1) = 0\) — a single, unambiguous value where the Riemann sums saw nothing but permanent disagreement. Lebesgue integration is the direct measure-theoretic descendant of everything built in this essay, and it is the standard on which modern probability theory is built.

Improper integrals: relaxing the boundary, not the machinery

A function can stay perfectly tame everywhere except at the very edge of the interval — a vertical asymptote at an endpoint, or an interval that never ends at all — and the integral is rescued the same way the derivative essay rescued corner points: not by changing the definition, but by wrapping an ordinary limit around it.

The region under \(1/\sqrt{x}\) near \(0\) is infinitely tall but converges to a finite area anyway — the asymptote is "thin enough" for its area to add up to something finite. The same rescue handles an infinite domain:

Yet the outwardly similar integral \(\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty}\big[\ln x\big]_1^b = \lim_{b\to\infty}\ln b = \infty\) diverges, even though \(1/x\to0\) just as \(1/x^2\) does. The integrand shrinking to zero is not enough on its own — how fast it shrinks decides whether infinitely much of it still adds up to a finite total. That razor's edge between convergence and divergence is exactly where the taxonomy of infinite series, met in the companion Taylor-series essay, picks up the same question in a different guise.

Where This Leads

area becomes volume, rates become differential equations, and the loop back to the derivative closes

Multiple integrals: accumulating over area and volume

Nothing in Panel II's construction was secretly one-dimensional — a partition, a sample point, a sum, a shrinking limit. Replace the interval \([a,b]\) with a two-dimensional region and the subintervals with small patches of area, and the exact same machinery accumulates a quantity spread across a plane instead of a line: the double integral. Replace the patches with small blocks of volume and it accumulates density into mass across three-dimensional space: the triple integral. The companion double-integral essay develops what accumulation means in two dimensions from exactly this starting point, and the triple-integral essay carries it into physical mass and density. When the region itself is easier to describe in curved coordinates than in a rectangular grid, the factor that keeps area or volume honest under that change of coordinates is the Jacobian determinant — the same object the derivative essay's closing panel introduced as the multivariable derivative, now doing double duty as an area-scaling correction inside an integral, worked out in full in the companion integration-techniques essay.

Differential equations: running FTC in the most direct way possible

FTC says integration undoes differentiation. The most immediate use of that fact solves the simplest possible differential equation, \(\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)\): integrate both sides, and \(y\) is recovered up to the one constant of integration that antidifferentiation can never pin down on its own. The companion differential-equations essay takes on the far richer cases where the rate depends on \(y\) itself, not just on \(x\) — where the accumulation and the accumulated quantity feed back into each other.

Taylor series: the integral as the exact leftover term

The derivative essay's Taylor approximation, \(f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h+\frac{h^2}{2}f''(a)\), is only ever approximate — but FTC turns the error in that approximation into an exact integral rather than a vague "small leftover." Applying FTC Part 2 repeatedly (integrating \(f''\), then \(f'''\), and so on) expresses the gap between a function and its polynomial approximation as \(R_n(x) = \int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)\,\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}\,dt\) — the integral form of the Taylor remainder. The companion Taylor-series essay develops what happens as more and more terms are added, and this is precisely where that expansion's error term comes from.

Insight — one line to carry away

Every idea in this essay is one continuous thread: a total with no constant rate to multiply by, rescued by watching what a perfectly ordinary sum of finitely many rectangles approaches as those rectangles narrow to nothing and multiply without bound. The Riemann sum, the Fundamental Theorem, every integration rule, and every failure mode in Panel V are all restatements of that single rescue — the very same rescue, run in reverse, that built the derivative in the first place.
