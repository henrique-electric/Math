POLAR DOUBLE INTEGRALS

Polar Regions

why a rotating ray finds bounds that a sliding line never could

The scanning-line trick, and where it breaks down

The companion Double Integral essay's answer to "how do I find the bounds of an iterated integral" was geometric: sketch the region \(D\), slide a scanning line across it, and read the entrance and exit curves straight off the picture. That trick is excellent for regions built out of lines and parabolas. It gets ugly fast for regions built out of circles. Integrate over the unit disk in Cartesian Type I form and the bounds are already unpleasant —

— and that's the easy circular region, one centered right at the origin. A circle sitting off-center, a cardioid, a rose — for these, forcing a Type I or Type II description isn't just inconvenient, it can require solving for \(y\) as multiple disconnected branches of a square root, or fail to produce a closed form at all.

A different kind of line: the ray from the pole

The fix is not a new piece of calculus — it's a different sweeping motion. Instead of sliding a straight line across \(D\), anchor one endpoint of a ray at the origin (the pole) and let it rotate through every angle \(D\) occupies. For circular, cardioid-shaped, or rose-shaped boundaries, this works cleanly for a structural reason: those curves are almost always defined by an equation of the form \(r=f(\theta)\) in the first place. A ray at angle \(\theta\) doesn't need to solve anything to find where it crosses such a curve — the curve's own equation hands back the crossing radius directly.

Insight

The reason the ray is so much cleaner than the scanning line isn't mysterious once stated plainly: a circle, a cardioid, a rose petal — every shape this essay cares about — is a curve whose natural equation already gives \(r\) as an explicit function of \(\theta\). A Cartesian scanning line has to invert that relationship, algebraically, into \(y\) as a function of \(x\), which is exactly the step that produces square roots and case splits. A polar ray never inverts anything; it reads the curve's own defining formula and stops.

Note — when to reach for this

The signal is usually one of two things: the integrand contains a bare combination like \(x^2+y^2\) or \(\arctan(y/x)\) that simplifies the moment \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) is substituted in — or the region itself is a disk, a sector, an annulus, or is bounded by a curve most naturally written as \(r=f(\theta)\). Either signal alone is often enough reason to switch; both together make the choice automatic.

Why the pole never causes trouble

Because \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\) carries an explicit factor of \(r\), a cell sitting right at \(r=0\) contributes essentially zero area no matter how wide an angular slice \(\Delta\theta\) it spans — infinitely many rays converge at the pole, but they converge onto a single point of zero area. This is exactly why every region in Panel IV that touches the origin is allowed to set \(g_1(\theta)=0\) with no special handling: the pole isn't a singularity to work around, it's just the ordinary case \(r=0\) inside a formula that already vanishes there.

Warning — the single most common setup error

Dropping the factor of \(r\) is the polar equivalent of forgetting \(dx\,dy\) altogether — it silently computes the wrong quantity while still returning a plausible-looking number. Integrate \(1\) over the unit disk both ways:

The version without \(r\) returns exactly double the true area of the unit disk — not a subtle error, but one that's easy to miss because \(2\pi\) still looks like a reasonable answer for a problem involving a circle.

The Radial Sweep Method

a five-question recipe for α, β, g₁(θ), g₂(θ) — and the reason it's guaranteed to work

Reading the bounds off the picture

The most reliable way to set up a polar-type region is geometric, exactly as it was for Cartesian Type I and Type II regions: sketch \(D\), then instead of sliding a straight scanning line across it, anchor one end of a ray at the pole and let it sweep through every angle the region touches. For a fixed angle \(\theta\), that ray is a straight line through the origin; walking outward along it, the first boundary curve it crosses is the inner limit \(g_1(\theta)\) — or the pole itself, \(r=0\), if the ray already starts inside \(D\) — and the last boundary curve it crosses before finally leaving \(D\) is the outer limit \(g_2(\theta)\). Once \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) are pinned down as functions of \(\theta\), ask a second, entirely separate question: over what range of angles does the ray intersect \(D\) at all? That range is two plain numbers, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) — and exactly as the companion Double Integral essay warned for Cartesian bounds, if a \(\theta\) survives into what should be a constant outer limit, the bounds were read off backwards.

Insight — this recipe is Type I bounds in disguise

Nothing here is a new piece of mathematics bolted onto Fubini's theorem — it's Fubini's theorem, run inside the \((\theta,r)\)-plane instead of the \(xy\)-plane, then carried back through the coordinate change \(T(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)\). In the \((\theta,r)\)-plane,

is an entirely ordinary Cartesian Type I region — \(\theta\) plays the role of \(x\), \(r\) plays the role of \(y\), and the same vertical-scanning-line logic from Panel III of the companion essay applies without a single modification. What makes the result look unfamiliar is only the map \(T\) that warps that plain, rectangle-ish \(D'\) into the curved \(D\) sitting in the \(xy\)-plane — and the lone factor of \(r\) riding along in \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\) is exactly \(|\det J_T|\), the local area price of that warp.

Warning — the region must be radially simple

The recipe assumes every ray in \([\alpha,\beta]\) crosses \(\partial D\) exactly twice — once going in, once coming out — so that a single pair \(g_1(\theta),g_2(\theta)\) can describe the whole crossing. A region that isn't radially simple in this sense — one where some ray clips the boundary four times, say — can't be written this way as a single piece. The fix is identical to Panel III of the companion essay: split \([\alpha,\beta]\) at the angle where the crossing count changes, treat each piece as its own polar-type region, and add the results.

Note — the pole is a free lunch, until it isn't

When \(D\) contains the pole for a given \(\theta\), the ray starts its journey already inside the region, so \(g_1(\theta)=0\) with no extra work — the disk, the cardioid, and every rose petal in Panel IV lean on exactly this. The annulus is the exception that proves the rule: it never contains the pole, so \(g_1(\theta)\) there is a genuine positive constant for every \(\theta\), not zero.

Converting a Cartesian boundary curve into polar

When a region is given in Cartesian form but the region itself is circular or rotationally natural, the boundary curves usually convert with almost no algebra, since \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ x^2+y^2=r^2\):

The first line is the entire reason polar coordinates exist for circular regions: a circle centered at the origin, which needs a square root to express as \(y=f(x)\), needs nothing at all to express as \(r=f(\theta)\) — it's simply the constant function \(r=a\).

Warning — extending θ past where r stays non-negative retraces the curve

Table row four restricts \(\theta\) to \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) for \(r=2a\cos\theta\), and that restriction is load-bearing, not cosmetic. Push \(\theta\) past \(\pi/2\) and \(\cos\theta\) turns negative — under the standard convention that \(r\ge0\), those angles simply aren't part of the curve at all. Under the alternate convention that allows negative \(r\) (plotting the point in the opposite direction), \(\theta=\pi\) gives \(r=-2a\), which is the point \((2a,0)\) — a point already reached at \(\theta=0\). Either way, angles outside \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) add nothing new; feeding them into the bounds anyway integrates the same physical region a second time and silently doubles the answer.

Example B — a region between two curves

Find the area inside the cardioid \(r=1+\cos\theta\) but outside the circle \(r=1\). First, find where the ray-sweep description is even valid: the cardioid sits outside the circle exactly when \(1+\cos\theta\ge1\), i.e. \(\cos\theta\ge0\), i.e. \(\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) — outside that range the cardioid dips inside the circle, and there's no lens there at all. Within that range, \(g_1(\theta)=1\) (the circle, inner) and \(g_2(\theta)=1+\cos\theta\) (the cardioid, outer):

This exact region — the lens between a circle and a cardioid — is one of the presets in Panel VI's lab. Dragging the probe there and watching \(g_1(\theta)\) and \(g_2(\theta)\) converge to the same radius right at \(\theta=\pm\pi/2\) is the geometric picture behind the boundary condition \(\cos\theta\ge0\) used to find \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) above.

Example C — the pole trap

Solving \(g_1(\theta)=g_2(\theta)\) for a shared \(\theta\) finds every point where two curves have identical polar coordinates — but polar coordinates aren't unique at the origin, where \(r=0\) regardless of what \(\theta\) is. Two curves can pass through the exact same point \((0,0)\) while reaching it at completely different angles, and a shared point like that is invisible to solving \(g_1(\theta)=g_2(\theta)\) as ordinary algebra.

Take \(r=\sin\theta\) and \(r=\cos\theta\) — two circles of radius \(\tfrac12\), centered at \((0,\tfrac12)\) and \((\tfrac12,0)\) respectively. Solving algebraically:

— one intersection point. But check each curve against the pole separately:

Different angles, but both give the identical Cartesian point \((0,0)\). Sketching the two circles confirms it: they cross at two points, not one — the algebraic crossing at \(\theta=\pi/4\), and the pole, reached by each curve on its own schedule. Solving \(g_1(\theta)=g_2(\theta)\) found exactly one of the two.

Warning — always check the pole separately

Before trusting a list of intersection points found by solving \(g_1(\theta)=g_2(\theta)\), ask a second question that no amount of algebra on that equation will answer: does \(g_1\) reach \(0\) somewhere, and does \(g_2\) reach \(0\) somewhere — possibly at a completely different \(\theta\)? If both do, the pole is a shared point of the two curves and belongs on the list, whether or not the equation \(g_1(\theta)=g_2(\theta)\) ever produced it.

Pitfalls & Where This Leads

symmetry shortcuts, sign conventions, and the jump to three dimensions

Symmetry cuts the work, not the correctness

Whenever both \(D\) and the integrand are symmetric under \(\theta\to-\theta\) (reflection across the x-axis), it's legitimate to integrate over half the angular range and double the result — and the same idea stacks for quarter- or eighth-symmetric regions. The disk from Panel IV, for instance, can be computed as four identical quarter-wedges instead of one full turn:

This is bookkeeping, not new mathematics — additivity of regions, the same fact the companion essay uses to justify splitting a domain into pieces, run in reverse to merge identical pieces back into one shortcut.

Two reminders worth keeping close

Splitting still applies here exactly as it did in Panel III: if no single pair \(g_1(\theta),g_2(\theta)\) covers the whole angular range because the boundary curve switches partway through, split \([\alpha,\beta]\) at the switch point and add the pieces — the polar mirror of splitting a Cartesian region at a change of boundary curve. And the sign convention matters: by the standard convention used throughout this essay, \(r\) never goes negative in a bounds computation; if a candidate formula for \(r\) would go negative over part of a proposed \(\theta\)-range, that part of the range is simply excluded, exactly as Panel IV's warning did for \(r=2a\cos\theta\). Mixing that convention with the alternate one — where negative \(r\) is allowed and plotted in the opposite direction — inside a single bounds computation is one of the more common sources of a silently doubled or halved answer.

The occasional reversed order

Just as a Cartesian region is occasionally more natural as Type II (fix \(y\), let \(x\) range between two curves) than Type I, a polar region is occasionally more natural with the roles reversed: fix a band of radii and let \(\theta\) range between two curves \(h_1(r),h_2(r)\), integrating \(\theta\) first. This is rare — most curves worth naming are given as \(r=f(\theta)\), not \(\theta=h(r)\) — but a "keyhole" or slotted region can make it the cleaner choice, and the bounds are read off with the identical ray-sweep logic, just holding \(r\) fixed instead of \(\theta\).

Insight — one line to carry away

Finding polar bounds was never a separate skill from finding Cartesian bounds — it's the same ray-sweep-and-read-the-boundary technique from Panel III of the companion essay, run in the \((\theta,r)\)-plane instead of the \(xy\)-plane, with a single factor of \(r\) left behind as the toll for the coordinate change that carries the answer back into the plane the region actually lives in.
