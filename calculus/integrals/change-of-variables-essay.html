CHANGE OF VARIABLES

Change of Variables

what a substitution really costs, once there's more than one variable to substitute

u-substitution, reread as an exchange rate

Every calculus student learns \(u\)-substitution as an algebra trick: spot a composite function, name the inner piece \(u\), replace \(du\) with whatever \(u'(x)\,dx\) turns out to be, and hope the rest of the integrand rewrites cleanly in terms of \(u\). That mechanical description is correct but hides the one idea that actually generalizes. Substitution is really a statement about exchange rates between lengths:

Read the factor \(g'(u)\) literally: it says a small interval \(du\) sitting in \(u\)-space corresponds to an interval of length \(\approx|g'(u)|\,du\) sitting in \(x\)-space. Substituting a variable doesn't just relabel the axis — it stretches or compresses it, unevenly, by an amount that changes from point to point. \(g'(u)\) is the local exchange rate, and the integral has to be paid in that new currency for the two sides to agree.

u-space x-space (x = g(u)) ├──du──┤ ├────g'(u)·du────┤ u u+du g g(u) g(u+du) ─────────────── ────────▶ ───────────────────────────────── a short interval near u maps to a LONGER (or shorter) interval near g(u), stretched by the local factor g'(u) — that factor is exactly what dx becomes
Note — why no absolute value shows up here

You'll sometimes see \(u\)-substitution written with \(g'(u)\) bare, no absolute value, even though \(g\) might be decreasing. That's not an oversight — it's because a 1D integral already carries a sign convention. If \(g\) is decreasing, \(g^{-1}(a) > g^{-1}(b)\), so the new limits of integration flip order, and that flip exactly cancels the sign that a negative \(g'(u)\) would otherwise introduce. Direction and magnitude are bundled together in a single number, \(g'(u)\), and the bookkeeping works itself out automatically.

The question this essay chases

Now ask the same question with two variables. Let \(T(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))\) send a region \(D'\) in the \(uv\)-plane onto a region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane, and try to rewrite \(\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\) as an integral over \(D'\). The obvious guess — just replace \(dx\,dy\) with \(du\,dv\) and call it a day — is wrong, and it's wrong for a reason the 1D case never had to confront: an area, unlike a length, has no built-in sign convention to lean on, and a coordinate change can distort a 2D patch in ways a single number like \(g'(u)\) can't begin to describe. A patch can stretch along one direction while compressing along another, get sheared into a parallelogram, or rotated — all at once, all depending on where in \(D'\) it sits. Whatever plays the role of \(g'(u)\) here has to be able to describe all of that. That object is the Jacobian, and finding the right correction factor built from it is exactly what the next two panels do.

Insight

Nothing conceptually new is about to happen — this essay is, start to finish, one generalization of \(dx=g'(u)\,du\). The single number \(g'(u)\) becomes a matrix of partial derivatives (the Jacobian), and "stretch a length by \(|g'(u)|\)" becomes "stretch an area or volume by \(|\det J|\)." Keep that one substitution in mind as the anchor; every formula in Panels II–IV is that same idea, one dimension further up.

Distorting a Small Patch

Definition — the Jacobian matrix

\(J_T(u,v)\) is the matrix of partial derivatives

the exact multivariable analogue of \(g'(u)\): the best linear stand-in for \(T\) at that point. (The full derivation of why this matrix is forced to exist and be unique — differentiability, partial derivatives, local linearity in general — lives in the companion Jacobian essay; this essay only needs the finished object.)

A rectangle becomes a parallelogram

Apply that linear approximation to a small rectangle \([u,u+\Delta u]\times[v,v+\Delta v]\) sitting in \(D'\). Its four corners get carried, to first order, to \(T(u,v)\) plus a linear combination of the two columns of \(J_T\) — which means the image of the rectangle is, to the same first order, the parallelogram spanned by \(J_T\)'s columns scaled by \(\Delta u\) and \(\Delta v\):

uv-plane xy-plane (image under T) ┌──────────┐ ╱▔▔▔▔▔╱ │ Δv │ T ╱ ╱ │ Δu │ ────────▶ ╱ ╱ └──────────┘ ╱______╱ rectangle, area = Δu·Δv parallelogram spanned by (∂x/∂u,∂y/∂u)Δu and (∂x/∂v,∂y/∂v)Δv

The area of a parallelogram spanned by two vectors is the absolute value of the \(2\times2\) determinant they form — a plain fact from linear algebra, independent of anything about \(T\). Plugging in the two scaled columns above gives the local area-scaling law:

This is precisely the object standing in for \(g'(u)\). It's used so often that it earns its own notation, the Jacobian determinant:

Sign versus magnitude

The determinant carries two pieces of information, and only one of them matters for area. Its sign records orientation: a positive sign means \(T\) preserves the counterclockwise sense of a small loop, a negative sign means it reverses it (like a reflection). Its magnitude records how much area the patch actually gains or loses. Because area is never negative, the local scaling law above needs the absolute value — |det J_T|, not det J_T — and that single absolute-value sign is the one place a change-of-variables computation most often goes wrong.

Warning — where a zero determinant leads

If \(\det J_T(u,v)=0\) at some point, the parallelogram above degenerates to a line segment or a point — the map is crushing a whole direction flat right there, and it can't be locally invertible at that point. The change-of-variables theorem in the next panel needs \(\det J_T\ne0\) almost everywhere in \(D'\) for exactly this reason: a few isolated zero-area collapses (a single point, a single curve) don't disturb an integral, but a coordinate change that's singular on a whole region of positive area breaks the correspondence the theorem depends on.

Why this is the right generalization of \(g'(u)\)

Set \(v\) aside entirely and this collapses back to the 1D picture exactly: a "rectangle" \([u,u+\Delta u]\) of "area" (length) \(\Delta u\) maps to an interval of length \(|g'(u)|\,\Delta u\). The Jacobian determinant is not an analogy to \(g'(u)\) — it's the literal same construction, run through a determinant instead of a bare product, because a determinant is what "how much do these \(n\) spanning vectors' volume scale by" means once there's more than one dimension to track at once.

Why the Riemann sums have to agree

Panel II's local law is the whole proof, once it's summed rather than applied at a single point. Partition \(D'\) into a fine grid of small rectangles, each of area \(\Delta u\,\Delta v\). Panel II says each rectangle's image under \(T\) is, to excellent approximation, a parallelogram of area \(|\det J_T|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\), evaluated at any sample point in that rectangle. So a Riemann sum built over \(D'\) can be read two ways at once:

The left side, as the grid refines, is by definition a Riemann sum converging to \(\iint_{D'}(f\circ T)|\det J_T|\,du\,dv\). The right side is a Riemann sum too — not over neat rectangles, but over the (approximately parallelogram-shaped) tiles that the rectangles get carried to, which tile \(D\) itself as \(T\) ranges over all of \(D'\). Riemann sums converge to the same integral no matter how the domain is tiled, as long as the tiles shrink to zero — so the right side converges to \(\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\). Both sides were, all along, limits of the same quantity approached from two different tilings; the theorem is the statement that those two limits agree.

Two practical corollaries

You can differentiate whichever direction is easier

Sometimes the inverse map \(T^{-1}:(x,y)\mapsto(u,v)\) is the one handed to you directly (Panel IV's linear example is exactly this case), and computing \(\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)\) would mean first solving for \(x,y\) in terms of \(u,v\). That extra step is never necessary, because the two Jacobian determinants are reciprocals of each other:

which follows immediately from the chain rule for Jacobians, \(J_{T^{-1}}(T(p))\cdot J_T(p) = I\), and the determinant-of-a-product rule. Compute whichever Jacobian is more convenient, then invert (reciprocate) that one number if you happen to need the other.

Injectivity failures on a thin set are harmless

The theorem's hypotheses only ask for injectivity except on a set of area zero — a deliberately weak requirement, because the standard substitutions all violate strict injectivity somewhere. Polar coordinates collapse the entire segment \(r=0\) to the single point \((0,0)\), and the rays \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=2\pi\) trace the same points twice. Both failures sit on a set with no area, so they cost the integral nothing. What the theorem does forbid is a substitution that folds a piece of \(D'\) with genuine positive area onto itself more than once — that would double-count a real region of \(D\), and no correction factor fixes double-counting; the fix is to restrict \(D'\) to a piece where \(T\) is honestly injective before integrating.

Worked Examples

six substitutions, each earning its Jacobian determinant from scratch

The mechanics, as a five-step recipe

Every substitution in this panel — and really, every change of variables anyone ever writes down — follows the same five moves. It's worth stating them once, plainly, before working through six examples that all secretly do the same thing underneath different formulas:

1. CHOOSE the substitution. Pick new variables (u,v,…) tailored to the integrand or the region's boundary — usually whichever one is awkward in x,y. 2. GET the forward map T. Solve for x,y (or x,y,z,…) in terms of the new variables, if you weren't handed T directly. 3. TRANSFORM the region. Substitute the forward map into each boundary curve of D, one at a time, to get the boundary curves of D′ — then read off bounds for the new variables the same way you would in Cartesian. 4. TRANSFORM the integrand. Rewrite f(x,y) in terms of u,v by substituting the forward map into f directly. 5. COMPUTE |det J_T| and combine. Differentiate the forward map to get J_T, take |det J_T|, and assemble the new integral: ∬_D f dA = ∬_D′ (f∘T)·|det J_T| du dv

Steps 1 and 2 are choices — there's no algorithm for picking a good substitution, only experience with which combinations tend to simplify a given integrand or region. Steps 3 through 5 are mechanical, and the six examples below vary almost entirely in how much work step 3 — transforming the region — actually takes: sometimes it's nearly free, sometimes it needs real work of its own.

1 · A linear substitution

Let \(R\) be the square tilted \(45°\) — bounded by \(x+y=0\), \(x+y=2\), \(x-y=0\), \(x-y=2\) — and evaluate \(\iint_R e^{x+y}\,dA\). Cartesian bounds here are awkward (every edge is a diagonal line), so choose (step 1) \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\), and solve for \(x,y\) to get the forward map (step 2):

Step 3, transforming the region, is unusually cheap here because \(u\) and \(v\) were chosen to be the exact combinations that appear in \(R\)'s own boundary equations. Substituting is immediate — each boundary line already reads as a constant-\(u\) or constant-\(v\) line, no algebra required:

boundary of R (in x,y) is already, verbatim boundary of R′ (in u,v) x + y = 0 ────────────────────────────▶ u = 0 x + y = 2 ────────────────────────────▶ u = 2 x − y = 0 ────────────────────────────▶ v = 0 x − y = 2 ────────────────────────────▶ v = 2

Four diagonal lines in the \(xy\)-plane become four axis-aligned lines in the \(uv\)-plane, and the tilted square \(R\) becomes the plain square \(R':\ 0\le u\le2,\ 0\le v\le2\). Step 4, the integrand, is equally direct — \(e^{x+y}=e^u\), since \(u\) was defined to be exactly \(x+y\). Step 5: \(|\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)|=\tfrac12\), a constant, since the substitution is linear and linear maps distort every cell by the same fixed factor everywhere. 2 · The same substitution, on a region that isn't a box

Example 1 got lucky: \(R\)'s boundary happened to align exactly with lines of constant \(u\) and constant \(v\), so step 3 cost nothing. That won't always happen, and it's worth seeing what step 3 looks like when it doesn't. Keep the identical substitution — \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\), same forward map, same \(|\det J_T|=\tfrac12\) — but change the region to the triangle \(T\) with vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\), bounded by \(x=0\), \(y=0\), \(x+y=1\), and evaluate \(\iint_T xy\,dA\).

This time each boundary curve needs an actual substitution step, not just a relabeling — plug the forward map's \(x=(u+v)/2,\ y=(u-v)/2\) into each equation in turn and simplify:

boundary of T (in x,y) substitute x=(u+v)/2, y=(u−v)/2 boundary of T′ (in u,v) x = 0 ──▶ (u+v)/2 = 0 ──▶ v = −u y = 0 ──▶ (u−v)/2 = 0 ──▶ v = u x + y = 1 ──▶ u = 1 (immediate: u is x+y)──▶ u = 1

Three straight lines went in, three straight lines came out — a substitution always sends a boundary curve to another single, unbroken curve, never to a scattered mess of points, precisely because \(T\) is continuous and one-to-one. Plotting \(v=u\), \(v=-u\), and \(u=1\) gives a triangle \(T'\) with vertices \((0,0),(1,1),(1,-1)\) — the same three corners as the original triangle, just relocated. Reading bounds off that picture works exactly like reading bounds for a Type-I Cartesian region: sweep \(u\) across its range, and at each \(u\) let \(v\) run between the two slanted edges:

Insight — the general procedure this demonstrates

Finding the new region is always the same routine, however complicated the boundary: take each piece of the old boundary, substitute the forward map's formulas into that curve's equation, and simplify. Whatever curve comes out is the corresponding piece of the new boundary. The only thing that changes from problem to problem is how much algebra that simplification takes — trivial when, as in Example 1, the substitution was chosen to match the boundary exactly; real but routine otherwise, as here.

Pitfalls & Quick Reference

the mistakes this formula invites, and a table to skip re-deriving the standard cases

Three ways this goes wrong in practice

Warning — dropping the absolute value

\(\det J_T\) can be negative — it happens exactly when \(T\) reverses orientation — but a negative area is meaningless, so the theorem always uses \(|\det J_T|\), never the signed determinant on its own. Plugging in a formula that came out negative, without taking its magnitude, silently flips the sign of the whole integral. Panel II's linear substitution example above had \(\det J_T=-\tfrac12\) for exactly this reason; the working integral used \(\tfrac12\), not \(-\tfrac12\).

Warning — differentiating the wrong direction

Given \(u=x+y,\ v=x-y\) (the inverse map, \(T^{-1}\)), it's tempting to differentiate exactly that and plug the result straight into \(dx\,dy = |\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)|\,du\,dv\). That's differentiating \(T^{-1}\), which gives \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)\) — the reciprocal of what the formula needs. Either solve for \(x,y\) first and differentiate the forward map, or differentiate whichever direction is convenient and take the reciprocal, per Panel III's corollary — but don't skip that reciprocal step.

Warning — double-counting from a non-injective substitution

A substitution that's many-to-one on a set of positive area — not just a single point or curve — will make the formula overcount \(D\). This doesn't happen with polar, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates used over their standard ranges (\(\theta\in[0,2\pi)\), \(\varphi\in[0,\pi]\)); it happens when a range gets extended sloppily, e.g. letting \(\theta\) run over \([0,4\pi)\) "to be safe," which sweeps every point of \(D\) twice and doubles the answer.

One line to carry away

Every substitution formula on this page is the same sentence, resized: replacing one set of coordinates with another changes how much space a fixed-size coordinate cell represents, and the Jacobian determinant is exactly the exchange rate that keeps the integral honest through that change — nothing more mysterious than \(dx=g'(u)\,du\), taught to track a parallelogram's or a parallelepiped's volume instead of a single interval's length.
