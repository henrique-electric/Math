JACOBIAN

The Jacobian

how a single matrix captures the way a map bends, stretches, and folds space

A function is a rule for relocating points

Stop reading \(f(x,y)\) as "a formula that spits out a number," just for a moment, and read it instead as an instruction: take the point \((x,y)\), and move it somewhere else. A map \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) does this to every point of its domain at once — and when you picture the domain as a solid sheet rather than a scattering of individual points, applying \(F\) to all of it simultaneously is not a metaphor. It's an honest, physical deformation: straight grid lines get pulled into curves, right angles get sheared into oblique ones, a patch that started out as a neat square can come out as a stretched sliver, a ballooned wedge, or a mirror-flipped copy of itself.

This is the picture the whole essay is built around. Take \(F(x,y) = (x+0.4y,\ y)\) — an unremarkable-looking pair of formulas. Applied to a square grid, it does something visibly geometric, not because the formulas are complicated (they're linear), but because any map, viewed this way, does something to the shape of space:

Definition

A map (or transformation) \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) assigns to every point of its domain exactly one point of its codomain. To say \(F\) "transforms space" is to shift attention from a single input–output pair to the whole picture at once: what happens to a region, a grid, a shape, when every one of its points is relocated by \(F\) simultaneously.

The question this whole essay chases

The shear above is deliberately tame — a linear map, so it does the exact same thing to every square in the grid, no matter where that square sits. Most maps worth studying aren't so obliging. Let \(F(x,y)=(x^2-y^2,\ 2xy)\): far from the origin the grid doesn't just tilt, it curls, and a square near \((2,0)\) gets stretched completely differently from a square near \((0,2)\). The transformation's effect genuinely depends on where you are.

And yet — this is the discovery underneath everything that follows — if you stop looking at the whole grid and zoom in on one single point, that dependence disappears. However curled the transformation is globally, a small enough patch around any one point gets carried, to breathtaking approximation, into a plain parallelogram: the image of a square under some linear map. Different points get different parallelograms, but at each individual point, the local behavior is exactly as simple as the shear above.

Insight

Every genuinely nonlinear transformation of space is a linear transformation of space in disguise — one linear map per point, changing smoothly as the point changes. That single sentence is the entire content of differentiability, restated geometrically instead of algebraically. The Jacobian is nothing more than the name for that point-by-point linear map. The rest of this essay pins the claim down with a proof (Panel II), catalogues everything a linear map is capable of doing to a shape (Panel III), and asks why you keep running into transformations like these in the first place (Panel IV).

Why "linear" is the right kind of simple

It's worth pausing on why reducing the local behavior to a linear map is a substantial simplification, not just a technicality. A linear map \(L:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is completely determined by where it sends just \(n\) vectors — the standard basis \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) — because every other point is a combination, \(L(c_1e_1+\dots+c_ne_n) = c_1L(e_1)+\dots+c_nL(e_n)\). Know \(n\) vectors' worth of output, and you know the map's action on the entire infinite space, exactly, with no approximation involved. A nonlinear map has no such shortcut: describing it exactly means describing what happens at every one of its infinitely many points, independently.

What makes calculus necessary at all is exactly this contrast. A nonlinear transformation can't be captured by one matrix valid everywhere — but it can be captured by infinitely many matrices, one attached to each point, each valid only in an infinitesimal neighborhood of that point. Assembling that one matrix, at one point, out of the raw material of partial derivatives is the entire business of Panel II.

Note

The cleanest mental picture — grid lines warping into curves — lives in the case \(n=m\), where input and output share a space and you can genuinely picture one deforming into the other. Everything here also covers \(n\ne m\): a curve's parametrization is a map \(\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^3\); a scalar field like temperature is a map \(\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\). The grid-warping picture just specializes beautifully when the dimensions match, which is why most of the illustrations here use it.

Warning

You cannot divide by a vector. The instant the input becomes \(h\in\mathbb{R}^n\) for \(n>1\), the expression \(\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) stops meaning anything — there is no operation "divide by \(h\)" for a vector \(h\). The approximation framing above never divides by \(h\); it only ever divides the scalar error by the scalar \(|h|\). That's the one feature of the definition that has to survive the jump to several variables, and it's exactly why this is the version worth memorizing.

Several inputs: partial derivatives are single-variable derivatives in disguise

Let \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\), written coordinate by coordinate as \(F=(f_1,\dots,f_m)\). Freeze every input except \(x_j\), and differentiate \(f_i\) as an ordinary function of that one variable:

Every partial derivative is, individually, exactly the 1D construction above, run along one coordinate axis with the rest of space held motionless. But a collection of axis-aligned slopes doesn't yet say what happens when you nudge several coordinates together — which is exactly what "moving a point" requires, since a general displacement \(h\in\mathbb{R}^n\) has nonzero components along every axis at once.

The right generalization: a linear map, not a list of numbers

\(F\) is differentiable at \(a\) if there exists a linear map \(J:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) such that

Symbol for symbol, this is the same sentence as the 1D definition — a number times a number has become a matrix times a vector, and \(|h|\) has become \(\|h\|\), the one change needed to keep "divide by a scalar" legal. A linear map \(\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\), once coordinates are fixed, is an \(m\times n\) matrix. That matrix is the Jacobian, written \(J_F(a)\).

Insight

A linear map \(\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) has exactly \(mn\) degrees of freedom — and those degrees of freedom turn out to be exactly the \(mn\) partial derivatives \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\), no more and no fewer. This isn't a coincidence: a linear map is fully pinned down by the images of the \(n\) basis vectors \(e_j\), and \(J\,e_j\) is precisely the \(m\)-vector of partial derivatives \(\partial F/\partial x_j\). Nothing about this construction is extra machinery bolted onto partial derivatives — it's just the unique way to arrange them so the result is a bona fide linear map.

Existence of partials is not enough — the gap has teeth

It's tempting to think "all the partial derivatives exist at \(a\)" and "\(F\) is differentiable at \(a\)" say the same thing. They don't, and the gap between them is the entire difference between axis-aligned information and true, all-directions local linearity.

Warning

Both partials \(\partial f/\partial x(0,0)\) and \(\partial f/\partial y(0,0)\) exist and equal \(0\) — along either axis, \(f\) is identically zero. But approach the origin along the diagonal \(y=x\) instead, and \(f\to\tfrac12\) — so \(f\) isn't even continuous at \((0,0)\), let alone differentiable there (differentiability forces continuity: as \(h\to0\), \(F(a+h)-F(a)=Jh+o(\|h\|)\to0\) directly from the definition). Two perfectly well-behaved axis-slopes coexisted with a function doing something completely different along a direction neither axis happens to point in — exactly why the correct definition demands one linear map working for every direction \(h\) at once, rather than one slope per axis.

Definition — the \(C^1\) criterion

The gap closes under one clean, almost-always-true hypothesis: if every partial \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\) exists near \(a\) and is continuous at \(a\), then \(F\) is genuinely differentiable at \(a\), with derivative exactly the matrix of those partials. Such an \(F\) is called \(C^1\). Panel IV proves this rather than just asserting it — it's the most satisfying "why" in the whole essay.

Assembling the Jacobian matrix

Deriving the matrix's entries from scratch

The row/column layout below is usually just handed over as notation to memorize. It doesn't have to be — the placement of every entry, row \(i\), column \(j\), value \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\), is forced, with no free choice left anywhere, by nothing more than the definition of matrix-vector multiplication and the definition of \(J\) already pinned down above.

Start from the defining approximation, \(F(a+h)-F(a)=Jh+o(\|h\|)\), and read it one output coordinate at a time. By the ordinary mechanics of matrix-vector multiplication, the \(i\)-th entry of \(Jh\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(J\) with \(h\) — write that row's entries as \((J_{i1},\dots,J_{in})\), unknown for now. To isolate one unknown coefficient \(J_{ij}\), move in only the \(j\)-th coordinate direction: set \(h=t\,e_j\) for a small scalar \(t\), so every \(h_k\) with \(k\ne j\) is exactly \(0\) and \(\|h\|=|t|\). Divide by \(t\) and let \(t\to0\). The left side becomes, by definition, exactly the partial derivative of \(f_i\) with respect to \(x_j\) — freeze every input except \(x_j\) and take a plain single-variable limit, the same construction from earlier in this panel — and the error term vanishes because it's \(o(t)/t\to0\):

Nothing above was a convention or a choice. Row came from reading the matrix-vector product one output coordinate at a time — one row per component of \(F\), because there's one equation \(f_i(a+h)-f_i(a)=\dots\) per output. Column came from restricting to a single input direction \(e_j\) to isolate one coefficient — one column per input variable, because there's one coordinate direction to move in per input. The entry sitting at their intersection is pinned down by nothing more exotic than a limit already defined earlier in this very panel.

Insight

There's a second, equally valid route to the identical matrix, worth seeing because it explains "column \(j\) is \(\partial F/\partial x_j\)" as one object rather than \(m\) separate limits stitched together afterward. Instead of reading the defining equation component-by-component, read all \(m\) components together and set \(h=t\,e_j\) directly: \(F(a+t\,e_j)-F(a)=t\,(Je_j)+o(t)\), so dividing by \(t\) and taking the limit gives \(Je_j=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{F(a+te_j)-F(a)}{t}\) — the entire column vector of partials \(\partial F/\partial x_j\) extracted in one shot. And \(Je_j\) is, by the ordinary mechanics of matrix-vector multiplication, always exactly the \(j\)-th column of \(J\) — multiplying a matrix by a standard basis vector reads off that column, no more and no less. Row-first or column-first, restricting to one output or one input direction, both derivations are forced to land on the same \(m\times n\) array, because both are just the single defining equation, read in two different orders.

Collecting every entry \(J_{ij}=\partial f_i/\partial x_j(a)\) into one array gives exactly the matrix this logic was building toward:

Definition

Row \(i\) is \(\nabla f_i(a)^{\mathsf T}\) — how output coordinate \(i\) alone responds to a nudge in each input. Column \(j\) is \(\partial F/\partial x_j\), the direction — in the output space — that the whole vector \(F\) moves when only \(x_j\) is nudged. Since column \(j\) is exactly \(J_F(a)\,e_j\), the columns of the Jacobian are literally where the standard basis vectors of the domain land under the local linear map.

Worked example: watching the local map change from point to point

Let \(F(x,y)=(x^2-y^2,\ 2xy)\) — complex squaring, \(z\mapsto z^2\), written in real coordinates. Then

At \((1,0)\) this is \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\) — pure scaling by \(2\), no rotation. At \((0,1)\) it's \(\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}\) — scaling by \(2\) and a \(90°\) rotation. Same formula for \(F\), two completely different local transformations, purely because the point changed — precisely the point-dependence flagged in Panel I, now numerically concrete. (At a general \((x,y)\), \(J_F\) is always \(2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) times a rotation — matching the complex-analysis fact \(\frac{d}{dz}z^2=2z\): multiplying by the complex number \(2z\) is exactly "rotate by \(\arg(2z)\) and scale by \(|2z|\)".)

Uniqueness: why there's only one honest candidate

Suppose two linear maps \(J_1,J_2\) both satisfy the defining approximation at \(a\). Subtracting the two expansions gives \((J_1-J_2)h = o(\|h\|)\); divide by \(\|h\|\ne0\) and let \(h\to0\) along any fixed direction \(u\) (that is, \(h=tu\), \(t\to0^+\)) to get \((J_1-J_2)u=0\) for every unit vector \(u\) — which forces \(J_1=J_2\). The Jacobian isn't one of several equally-good linear stand-ins picked by convention; it's forced to be unique by the same one-line argument that pins down the ordinary 1D derivative.

Anatomy of a Warp

everything a single linear map can do to a shape — and nothing it can't

Every linear map is rotate, stretch, rotate

Panel II pinned down \(J_F(a)\) as a matrix; this panel asks what a matrix, geometrically, is capable of doing. The answer is total: every linear map \(L:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) factors as

the singular value decomposition. \(V^{\mathsf T}\) is a rotation (or reflection) that lines up a chosen set of perpendicular directions with the coordinate axes; \(\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n)\), the singular values, stretches independently along each of those axes; \(U\) rotates the result into its final orientation. Feed in a circle: rotate it (no visible change — a circle is rotation-symmetric), stretch it along perpendicular axes into an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n\), then rotate the ellipse into place.

Insight

This is the complete vocabulary of what a linear map — and therefore, locally, any differentiable map — can do to space: rotate, stretch along some set of perpendicular directions (possibly by different amounts, possibly by zero), rotate again. Shape distortion always reduces to this three-step recipe; there is nothing else a linear map is capable of doing.

The determinant is the local volume-scaling factor

Restrict to \(n=2\) to derive this cleanly, then state the general pattern. A linear map with matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\) sends the unit square (spanned by \(e_1,e_2\)) to the parallelogram spanned by its two columns, \(v_1=(a,c)\) and \(v_2=(b,d)\). The area of that parallelogram is base times height: \(\|v_1\|\) times the component of \(v_2\) perpendicular to \(v_1\), which trigonometry reduces to \(\|v_1\|\|v_2\|\sin\theta = |ad-bc|\) — exactly \(|\det L|\).

Because linear maps scale uniformly — a rectangle of dimensions \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\) is just \(\Delta u\) copies of a unit-height strip times \(\Delta v\) copies of a unit-width strip — this same factor \(|\det L|\) rescales the area of any region, not just the unit square. Now bring Panel II back in: near a point \(a\), \(F(a+h)\approx F(a)+J_F(a)h\), so a small square of side \(\Delta\) around \(a\) is carried to a parallelogram of area \(\approx|\det J_F(a)|\,\Delta^2\), to an accuracy that improves as \(\Delta\to0\). That local scaling factor, \(|\det J_F(a)|\), is exactly the quantity that appears in the change-of-variables formula for integrals (Panel VI) — every infinitesimal patch of area has to be paid for, or refunded, by this factor when you switch coordinates.

Note

The same argument in \(n\) dimensions replaces "parallelogram area" with "parallelepiped volume," and the base-times-height trigonometric argument generalizes to the standard fact that the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the columns of an \(n\times n\) matrix is \(|\det L|\). The Jacobian determinant is always, in every dimension, the local volume-scaling factor — never anything looser than that.

Sign, and the moment a map stops being invertible

The determinant's sign tracks orientation: \(\det L>0\) means a counterclockwise loop stays counterclockwise (a proper rotation-and-stretch, no mirroring); \(\det L<0\) means the map includes a reflection, flipping handedness. And \(\det L=0\) is the boundary case where the ellipse from the SVD picture above degenerates — at least one singular value \(\sigma_i\) is exactly \(0\), so the map crushes an entire direction to a point, and the image of a full-dimensional region collapses onto something of strictly lower dimension.

Warning

A point where \(\det J_F(a)=0\) is called a critical or singular point of \(F\). Nothing computed there is wrong, but the local picture changes qualitatively: the map cannot be, even locally, a bijection near \(a\) — it has just crushed a whole direction of the domain flat. This is exactly the condition that will make the Inverse Function Theorem fail in Panel VI.

How & Why Maps Arise

a proof that the linear stand-in is forced to exist, and a tour of where these transformations come from

Why smooth maps are forced to look linear up close

Panel II defined differentiability and stated, without proof, that continuous partials are enough to guarantee it. Here is the proof — worth seeing once, because it explains mechanically why zooming in produces a flat parallelogram, rather than merely asserting that it does.

Suppose every partial of \(F\) is continuous near \(a\). For a component \(f_i\) and a fixed displacement \(h\), define \(g(t)=f_i(a+th)\) for \(t\in[0,1]\) — a single-variable function riding along the straight segment from \(a\) to \(a+h\). The single-variable chain rule gives \(g'(t)=\nabla f_i(a+th)\cdot h\), so the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus yields

Stacking this over every component \(i\) gives the vector identity \(F(a+h)-F(a) = \int_0^1 J_F(a+th)\,h\;dt\) — an exact equation, no approximation yet. Now subtract off the candidate linear term \(J_F(a)h\):

Bound the right side using the operator norm: its length is at most \(\|h\|\cdot\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|J_F(a+th)-J_F(a)\|\). As \(h\to0\), the points \(a+th\) all converge to \(a\) uniformly in \(t\), and continuity of \(J_F\) at \(a\) forces that supremum to \(0\). So the error is \(\|h\|\) times something going to zero — exactly \(o(\|h\|)\), which is the definition of differentiability with derivative \(J_F(a)\).

Insight

This is the honest "why": a smooth map looks linear up close because its exact change, \(F(a+h)-F(a)\), is an average of the Jacobian sampled along the segment from \(a\) to \(a+h\) — that's what the integral says — and continuity guarantees every sample in that average sits arbitrarily close to \(J_F(a)\) once \(h\) is small. Zooming in doesn't approximately flatten the map by luck; it flattens it because the only Jacobians involved in computing the true change are all clustered arbitrarily close to the one at \(a\).

Composing transformations: why the chain rule is matrix multiplication

Space is rarely transformed in a single step. A robot arm's tip position is a function of joint angles, which are themselves a function of motor commands; a 3D point's location on your screen is the composition of a world-to-camera transformation and a camera-to-image projection. Whenever transformations chain together, so do their linear stand-ins — and because composing two linear maps is exactly matrix multiplication, so must composing two Jacobians be:

Warning

Order matters, and so do shapes: for \(\mathbb{R}^n\xrightarrow{H}\mathbb{R}^k\xrightarrow{F}\mathbb{R}^m\), \(J_F(H(a))\) is \(m\times k\) and \(J_H(a)\) is \(k\times n\) — they're only compatible for multiplication in the order written, matching function composition read right-to-left.

The proof is a two-line echo of the argument above: substitute the linear expansion of \(H\) at \(a\) into the linear expansion of \(F\) at \(H(a)\), and the \(o(\|h\|)\) terms absorb into each other while the term linear in \(h\) is forced, by uniqueness (Panel II), to be exactly the product \(J_F(H(a))J_H(a)\). This one fact — composing warps of space means multiplying their local matrices — is the entire mechanism behind training a neural network (each layer is a transformation; backpropagation is this chain rule, run right-to-left through dozens of layers) as much as it's behind the single-variable rule \((f\circ g)'=f'(g(x))g'(x)\), which is just this statement for \(1\times1\) matrices.

Where these mappings actually come from

It's worth naming the recurring reasons a transformation of space shows up in the first place, since "how and why" is really a question about origin, not just mechanism.

Choosing better coordinates

Polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates are maps \(T:(r,\theta)\mapsto(x,y)\) constructed on purpose, purely to make some region or integral easier to describe. The transformation exists because Cartesian coordinates are the wrong native language for anything with rotational symmetry — a disk is an ugly nest of square roots in \(x,y\) and a plain rectangle in \(r,\theta\). The Jacobian of the coordinate change is exactly what tracks the area price of having made that swap — a thread picked back up in Panel VI.

Describing an actual physical deformation

In continuum mechanics, if a solid body gets stretched, sheared, or twisted under load, the map from an original material point to its displaced position is a genuine transformation of physical space, and its Jacobian — called there the deformation gradient — literally measures how much a small piece of material has stretched and rotated at that point. This is not an analogy; it's the same mathematical object, applied to a rubber sheet instead of a coordinate grid.

Parametrizing a curve or surface

A map \(\Phi:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\) that traces out a surface — say, \(\Phi(\phi,\theta)=(\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \cos\phi)\), a sphere — transforms a flat rectangle of parameters into a curved surface sitting in 3D. Its Jacobian's columns are tangent vectors to that surface, and the area-scaling idea from Panel III, now measuring how a flat parameter patch stretches into a curved surface patch, is exactly the machinery behind computing surface area.

Tracking motion or evolution

If \(\dot x = V(x)\) describes a flow — a fluid, a population, a dynamical system — the map \(\Phi_t\) that advances every point \(t\) units forward in time is itself a transformation of the whole space, one for every value of \(t\). Its Jacobian tells you whether nearby trajectories are converging (mixing, stable) or diverging (chaotic, unstable) — a question that only makes sense because \(\Phi_t\) is a genuine space-warping map.

Note

All four of these are the identical mathematical object wearing different clothes: a smooth map between spaces, locally approximable by a matrix. What differs is only the story behind why the map was written down — convenience, physics, geometry, or time. Panel VI returns to two of these threads, coordinates and flows, with their standard theorems attached.

Consequences

what falls out once "locally linear" is taken seriously

Undoing a transformation: the Inverse Function Theorem

Theorem — Inverse Function Theorem

If \(F\) is \(C^1\) near \(a\) and \(J_F(a)\) is invertible, then \(F\) is locally invertible near \(a\): there are neighborhoods \(U\ni a\), \(V\ni F(a)\) such that \(F:U\to V\) is a bijection with \(C^1\) inverse, and \(J_{F^{-1}}(F(a)) = J_F(a)^{-1}\).

The mechanism is worth sketching, not just citing. Rescale so that \(J_F(a)=I\) (multiply throughout by \(J_F(a)^{-1}\), legal precisely because it's invertible). Then \(F(x) = x + R(x)\) where \(R\) has \(J_R(a)=0\), so — by continuity of the Jacobian — \(R\) is a contraction (its Lipschitz constant is arbitrarily small) on a small enough ball around \(a\). Solving \(F(x)=y\) for \(x\) is the same as solving the fixed-point equation \(x = y-R(x)\), and the Contraction Mapping (Banach fixed-point) Theorem solves that uniquely for every \(y\) close enough to \(F(a)\) — producing the local inverse directly. Differentiating the identity \(F(F^{-1}(y))=y\) with Panel IV's chain rule then forces \(J_{F^{-1}}(y) = \big[J_F(F^{-1}(y))\big]^{-1}\).

Insight

This is why Panel III flagged \(\det J_F(a)=0\) as the boundary of invertibility: the theorem's hypothesis is exactly "the local linear stand-in is invertible," and a singular Jacobian is precisely a linear map with no inverse. The theorem's content is that this purely linear-algebra condition, checked at a single point, is enough to guarantee the true nonlinear map is genuinely, locally undoable — not just its flat approximation.

Stability of a flow: eigenvalues of the Jacobian

Panel IV's fourth thread — a map \(\Phi_t\) that advances a dynamical system forward in time — has its stability governed entirely by the Jacobian at an equilibrium. If \(\dot x=V(x)\) and \(x^*\) is an equilibrium (\(V(x^*)=0\)), then for \(h=x-x^*\) small, \(\dot h \approx J_V(x^*)\,h\) — a linear system, whose solutions are fully determined by the eigenvalues of \(J_V(x^*)\).

Note

If every eigenvalue of \(J_V(x^*)\) has negative real part, nearby trajectories are pulled in and \(x^*\) is locally stable; if any eigenvalue has positive real part, nearby trajectories are pushed away and \(x^*\) is unstable. This is the single most consequential use of the Jacobian outside of pure calculus — the entire local qualitative behavior of a nonlinear system, near an equilibrium, read off from one linear-algebra computation.

Beyond finite dimensions: the Fréchet derivative

Nothing about "the derivative is whatever linear object best approximates \(F\) at a point" requires \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and \(\mathbb{R}^m\) specifically. Let \(X,Y\) be Banach spaces and \(F:X\to Y\); then \(F\) is Fréchet differentiable at \(a\) if there's a bounded linear operator \(DF(a):X\to Y\) with \(\|F(a+h)-F(a)-DF(a)h\|_Y=o(\|h\|_X)\) — symbol for symbol, Panel II's definition, with a Banach-space norm in place of the Euclidean one. When \(X=\mathbb{R}^n,\ Y=\mathbb{R}^m\), every bounded linear operator is a matrix, and \(DF(a)\) reduces exactly to the Jacobian. The Jacobian is the shadow the Fréchet derivative casts onto finite-dimensional coordinates — the same idea, one level more general, used wherever the domain is a space of functions rather than a space of vectors.

One line to carry away

Every idea in this essay is one sentence, restated at increasing levels of generality: a transformation of space, however wildly it curves globally, looks exactly like a matrix once you zoom in far enough — that matrix is the Jacobian, its determinant is the local price of area or volume, its invertibility decides whether the transformation can be undone, and its chain rule is just what happens when you stack several such transformations end to end.

Rank, Implicit Surfaces, and Constraints

what a Jacobian's rank says about the shape of what it produces

Rank: how many dimensions actually survive the map

Every linear map \(L:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) has a rank — the dimension of its image, \(L(\mathbb{R}^n)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m\) — and Panel III's "\(\det=0\) means a direction gets crushed" observation is really a statement about rank in disguise: a square matrix with \(\det=0\) has rank strictly less than \(n\), meaning its image is a lower-dimensional subspace rather than all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Rank is the more fundamental of the two ideas; the determinant is just the special \(n=m\) test for whether the rank is as large as it possibly could be — "full rank."

For \(J_F(a)\), rank keeps an equally concrete meaning even when \(F\) isn't square: it's the number of genuinely independent directions the map actually moves things in, right at \(a\), out of the \(n\) directions available in the input. A rank-deficient Jacobian means some combination of input directions gets locally flattened to nothing — precisely Panel III's "critical point" idea, restated for maps that aren't square at all.

Level sets: the shape a scalar-valued map leaves behind

Return to a scalar field \(f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\) (rank at most \(1\), since the output space is one-dimensional). Its level set \(\{x:f(x)=c\}\) is whatever gets flattened onto the single value \(c\) — a curve in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), a surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The gradient's geometric meaning falls directly out of the local-linearity picture from Panel I:

Insight

Moving along a level set, by definition, doesn't change \(f\)'s value — so to first order, \(\nabla f(a)\cdot h\approx0\) for any displacement \(h\) tangent to the level set through \(a\). That's exactly the statement that \(\nabla f(a)\) is perpendicular to the level set at \(a\). The gradient doesn't just point uphill in some vague sense — it points in the one direction guaranteed not tangent to the flattened set the map leaves behind, which is precisely why it's the steepest-ascent direction: every tangent direction is, to first order, flat.

The Implicit Function Theorem: when a level set is honestly a graph

A level set can be a well-behaved curve, or it can cross itself, pinch to a point, or do worse — and the question of which is, once again, a Jacobian-rank question. Consider \(G(x,y)=0\) for \(G:\mathbb{R}^n\times\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^m\), and ask whether the last \(m\) coordinates can be solved for in terms of the first \(n\), at least near a known solution \((a,b)\).

This is not independent machinery — it's Panel VI's Inverse Function Theorem, applied to the augmented map \(\widetilde G(x,y)=(x,\,G(x,y))\), whose Jacobian is block-triangular with \(D_yG\) sitting in exactly the corner that has to be invertible. And the formula for \(D\phi\) is nothing but implicit differentiation, dressed in Jacobian notation: differentiate \(G(x,\phi(x))=0\) with Panel IV's chain rule to get \(D_xG+D_yG\cdot D\phi=0\), then solve the resulting linear equation for \(D\phi\).

Worked example: the rank test in action

Let \(G(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1\) — the unit circle. \(D_yG=\partial G/\partial y=2y\), invertible (nonzero) everywhere except \(y=0\), i.e. everywhere except the two points \((\pm1,0)\). Away from those two points, the theorem guarantees the circle really is locally a graph \(y=\phi(x)\) — which matches the picture exactly: near \((\pm1,0)\) the circle's tangent line is vertical, so no function \(y=\phi(x)\) can trace it there (it would need two \(y\)-values for one \(x\)), while everywhere else it's a perfectly good graph, either \(y=\sqrt{1-x^2}\) or \(y=-\sqrt{1-x^2}\). The theorem's hypothesis failing at exactly those two points isn't a technical gap — it correctly flags the only two points where the geometric claim actually breaks down.

The Lagrange multiplier condition is often presented as a trick — build the Lagrangian \(f-\lambda g\) and set its gradient to zero — but the derivation above shows it's a direct corollary of two ideas already fully established: gradients are perpendicular to level sets, and the Implicit Function Theorem is what guarantees the constraint set \(g=0\) is an honest surface with a well-defined perpendicular direction in the first place. Non-Square Jacobians

what to do when there's no exact solution to trust the linear stand-in for

When rows and columns disagree

Every worked problem in Panel VII assumed \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) — as many equations as unknowns, so \(J_F\) is square and Newton's method solves a well-posed linear system at every step. Most real fitting and estimation problems aren't so tidy. Panel III's transformation-of-space picture never actually required a square matrix — it's only the determinant and its "local volume factor" reading that needed \(m=n\) to make sense. Whenever \(m\ne n\), \(J_F\) still transforms space perfectly well; it's just transforming between spaces of different dimension, and it has no determinant and no ordinary inverse to fall back on.

Overdetermined systems: more equations than unknowns

If \(m>n\) — more conditions to satisfy than free parameters — \(F(x)=0\) generally has no exact solution at all: the image of \(F\) is an \(n\)-dimensional (or smaller) sliver sitting inside \(\mathbb{R}^m\), by Panel IX's rank argument, and the origin usually isn't on it. The sensible substitute is to minimize the residual instead of solving for it exactly:

Underdetermined systems: more unknowns than equations

The opposite imbalance, \(m<n\), is the flip side: too few conditions to pin down a unique answer, so \(F(x)=0\) typically has an entire \((n-m)\)-dimensional family of solutions. A redundant robot arm — more joints than the \(2\) or \(3\) numbers needed to place its endpoint — faces exactly this problem at every instant: infinitely many joint configurations reach the same target point, the mirror image of Panel IV's two-link arm example, now with a third joint added and nothing left to pin it down.

Note — the pseudoinverse

Among all solutions to an underdetermined \(J\Delta=v\), the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse \(J^+=J^{\mathsf T}(JJ^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\) picks out the one with smallest norm — the "laziest" adjustment consistent with the constraint. Both pseudoinverse formulas, \(J^+=(J^{\mathsf T}J)^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}\) for the overdetermined case above and \(J^+=J^{\mathsf T}(JJ^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\) for the underdetermined one, agree with the ordinary matrix inverse whenever \(J\) happens to be square and invertible — one object that specializes correctly in every case Panels VI through IX have already covered.

Insight

Zoom out across Panels VIII, IX, and X and a pattern emerges: every one of them takes the single square-and-invertible case this essay started with (Panel II) and asks what survives when that convenient assumption fails — a second derivative when the first vanishes, a rank below full, a shape that's rectangular rather than square. In every case, nothing about the transformation-of-space picture from Panel I broke; the machinery just had to be generalized to keep describing it honestly.

The Metric Tensor

what happens to lengths, not just areas, when coordinates change

Jacobian columns as a new, moving set of basis vectors

Every worked coordinate change in this essay — polar, spherical — has quietly been doing something worth naming outright: at each point, the Jacobian's columns, \(\partial T/\partial r\) and \(\partial T/\partial \theta\) for instance, form a genuine basis for the tangent directions at that point, exactly the way \(e_1,e_2\) form a basis for the plane. The difference is that these curvilinear basis vectors aren't fixed — they rotate, stretch, and shrink from point to point, precisely because \(J_T\) itself is a function of position rather than a single constant matrix. Panel I's "one linear map per point" idea is, from this angle, the statement that every point of a curved coordinate system carries its own private set of basis vectors.

Insight

The metric tensor and Panel III's area-scaling factor are not two separate facts to remember — one is derived from the other. For a square \(J\), \(\det g = \det(J^{\mathsf T}J) = \det(J^{\mathsf T})\det(J)=(\det J)^2\), so \(\sqrt{\det g}=|\det J|\) exactly. Every local-volume-scaling claim in this essay was already latent in the metric tensor; the determinant is just the one number you get by asking "how much does \(g\) inflate volume," while \(g\) itself answers the strictly more detailed question of how it inflates length, in every direction, individually.
