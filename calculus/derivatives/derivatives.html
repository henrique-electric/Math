THE DERIVATIVE

Instantaneous Change

how a rate of change measured over an interval becomes a rate of change measured at a single instant

Two kinds of rate

Arithmetic hands you a rate of change the moment you have two distinct readings to compare. Drive 100 miles in 2 hours and your average speed is 50 mph — nothing subtle, just a ratio: change in distance divided by change in time. Geometrically that ratio is a slope, \(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\), the same quantity that measures the steepness of a straight line drawn between two points on any graph.

Definition — average rate of change

For a function \(f\) between the input \(x\) and the input \(x+h\), the average rate of change is \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) — the change in output divided by the change in input. Geometrically it is the slope of the straight line joining \((x,f(x))\) and \((x+h,f(x+h))\).

The paradox of a rate with no interval

But there is a second kind of reading, one you take without ever comparing two separate moments. Glance at a speedometer at exactly 2:00 PM and it reads 65 mph. That number describes something happening at an instant, not between two instants — and an instant, by definition, has zero duration. "65 miles per hour, right now" cannot be arithmetic in the ordinary sense: there is no pair of distinct readings to subtract, no \(\Delta x\) to divide by. This is close to Zeno's paradox of the arrow: frozen at any single instant, an arrow occupies one fixed position and is, in that frozen picture, perfectly motionless. If time is really just a sequence of such frozen instants, how does motion happen at all?

Insight — the illusion of "instant"

The derivative does not actually divide by zero, and it does not really measure a rate over an interval of zero length — no such thing exists. It studies the trend of ordinary average rates, computed over intervals that are perfectly legitimate but shrinking, and asks what single number those trends are converging toward. "Instantaneous rate" is the name for that destination, not for a computation that ever literally happens at zero width.

Secant sliding toward tangent

Geometrically, an average rate of change is the slope of a secant line — a line crossing a curve through two distinct points. As the second point slides along the curve toward the first, the secant's slope changes continuously, and for any reasonably well-behaved curve it approaches a limiting value: the slope of the tangent line, the line that grazes the curve at a single point and matches its direction there exactly. That limiting slope is precisely the instantaneous rate of change.

Nothing here divides by zero at any finite stage of the slide. Every secant slope, for every distinct pair of points, is an ordinary fraction. Only in the limit — and only in the informal English description of what is being approached — does "zero" enter the picture at all. Turning "the second point slides toward the first" into a precise piece of algebra, one that pins down the tangent slope as an exact number rather than a vague destination, is the entire content of Panel II.

Note — a preview

The tool that performs this slide rigorously is the limit. Nothing about limits is specific to derivatives — the same machinery answers "what value does this quantity approach" in dozens of unrelated settings — but the derivative is arguably the single most consequential place a limit is ever taken, because it converts every curve in the plane into a source of exact, pointwise slope information.

The Limit Definition

turning the secant-to-tangent slide into an algebraic formula that pins down f′(x) exactly

The difference quotient

Fix a base point \(x\) and a second point \(x+h\), where \(h\) is the (signed) distance between them. The rise and run between the two points on the graph of \(f\) have standard names, and their ratio is called the difference quotient:

f(x+h) ●───────────┐ │ │ Δy = f(x+h) − f(x) │ │ f(x) ●────────────┘ └── Δx = h ──┘ secant slope = Δy / Δx = [ f(x+h) − f(x) ] / h

This is exactly the average-rate-of-change formula from Panel I, just relabeled to emphasize that \(h\) is the thing about to shrink. As \(h\) ranges over smaller and smaller nonzero values, the difference quotient traces out the sequence of secant slopes from the ascii diagram in Panel I — and the derivative is defined to be wherever that sequence is headed.

Definition of the derivative

Definition — the derivative

The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\), written \(f'(x)\), is the limit of the difference quotient as \(h\) approaches zero:

— provided this limit exists. If it does, \(f\) is said to be differentiable at \(x\), and \(f'(x)\) is the exact slope of the tangent line there. Nothing in this formula ever sets \(h\) equal to \(0\); it asks what the quotient approaches as \(h\) gets arbitrarily close to \(0\) without reaching it, which is precisely why the \(\frac{0}{0}\) obstruction from Panel I never actually arises.

One-sided derivatives

Because this is an honest two-sided limit, \(h\) is allowed to approach \(0\) from positive values (the right) or negative values (the left), and for \(f'(x)\) to exist both approaches must agree exactly:

When the two one-sided limits disagree, the curve has a sharp corner, and no single tangent slope makes sense there. The standard example is \(f(x)=|x|\) at \(x=0\): for \(h>0\), \(f(h)=h\), so the quotient is \(h/h=1\); for \(h<0\), \(f(h)=-h\), so the quotient is \(-h/h=-1\). The right-hand limit is \(1\), the left-hand limit is \(-1\), and since \(1\ne-1\) the derivative simply does not exist at that point — even though \(f\) is perfectly continuous there. Panel V returns to this example as the first entry in a full catalogue of ways differentiability can fail.

Differentiability forces continuity

Differentiability turns out to be a strictly stronger requirement than continuity — every differentiable function is automatically continuous, though (as the corner above already hints) the reverse implication fails. The proof is short enough to write out in full, and it is worth doing once because Panel IV's product rule leans on exactly this fact. So \(f(x+h)\to f(x)\) as \(h\to0\), which is exactly the definition of continuity at \(x\).

Insight — the arrow only points one way

This proof is a one-way street, and it is worth remembering which way. Differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous, always. Continuous \(\Rightarrow\) differentiable is false in general — \(f(x)=|x|\) is continuous everywhere and fails to be differentiable at exactly one point. Panel V pushes this gap much further: there exist functions continuous absolutely everywhere and differentiable absolutely nowhere.

Two languages: Newton and Leibniz

Calculus was co-invented independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and the field never fully settled on one notation — because the two dominant systems emphasize genuinely different aspects of the same object, and each earns its keep in different settings.

Newton–Lagrange notation, \(f'(x)\): read aloud as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)," this treats the derivative as a brand-new function, manufactured from \(f\), that can itself be evaluated, composed, or differentiated again. It is compact and reads naturally in equations built from function composition.

Leibniz notation, \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\): read as "\(d\,y\), \(d\,x\)," this keeps the ratio structure visible — a vanishingly small change in \(y\) divided by a vanishingly small change in \(x\), the difference quotient's numerator and denominator carried all the way to the limit. It is not literally a fraction of two numbers, but it obeys fraction-like manipulation rules closely enough that Panel IV's chain rule reads, almost embarrassingly simply, as \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}\) — units of \(u\) cancelling exactly as they would in ordinary arithmetic.

Local Linearity

why the tangent line is the best straight stand-in for a curve near one point

Zooming in until the curve looks straight

Here is a second, purely geometric route to the same idea, and it is often the more intuitive one. Viewed from space, the earth looks curved; stand in an open field and it looks flat. The closer you zoom in on any smooth curve, the more it comes to resemble a straight line — not approximately, in some loose visual sense, but in a way that can be made numerically precise. The derivative is exactly the slope of that straight line you converge toward under infinite magnification.

The tangent line as best linear approximation

Definition — linearization

The linearization of \(f\) at \(a\) is the tangent-line function \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\). For \(x\) near \(a\), \(L(x)\) is the best possible straight-line approximation to \(f(x)\) — better than any other line through \((a,f(a))\), in the precise sense developed below.

"Best possible" can be extracted directly from the limit definition itself. Rearranging the definition of \(f'(a)\), define an error term \(\varepsilon(h) = \dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} - f'(a)\) for \(h\ne0\). By the very definition of the limit, \(\varepsilon(h)\to0\) as \(h\to0\). Solving for \(f(a+h)\) turns this into a statement about the function itself rather than about a limit:

Read this as: the true value \(f(a+h)\) equals the linear prediction \(f(a)+f'(a)h\), plus an error term \(\varepsilon(h)h\) that vanishes faster than \(h\) itself does — not just to zero, but to zero relative to the size of the step. No other line through \((a,f(a))\) can make that same claim; a line with any other slope produces an error proportional to \(h\) itself, not to something smaller than \(h\).

Insight — a second, equivalent definition

This \(\varepsilon(h)h\) formulation is fully equivalent to the original limit definition, and it is the version that generalizes cleanly. It says a function is differentiable at a point exactly when it admits a linear stand-in whose error shrinks faster than the step size — a condition usually abbreviated \(o(h)\). That is precisely the definition used, one dimension up, by the companion Jacobian essay: a map \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is differentiable at a point when it has a best linear stand-in there too, with the same \(o(h)\) error condition, except the single number \(f'(a)\) is replaced by a whole matrix of partial derivatives.

What f′ means outside geometry

Once "slope of the tangent line" is understood as "instantaneous rate of change," the same idea translates directly into every field that models something changing.

That "excellent approximation" is local linearity, applied to \(A(r)=\pi r^2\) itself — which is exactly why \(A'(r)\) comes out to be the circumference on the nose, not merely something proportional to it.

Where local linearity breaks

Local linearity is a genuine claim about a curve's behavior, not a guarantee that holds automatically. Some curves refuse to flatten out under magnification no matter how far you zoom — a sharp corner stays a sharp corner at every scale. At exactly those points, no tangent line exists, and \(f'(x)\) is undefined. Panel V catalogues precisely how that failure happens, and — at the extreme end — a function pathological enough to fail everywhere at once.

Two derivatives that lean on a pair of standard limits

The exponential and trigonometric derivatives follow the same difference-quotient template, but they bottom out in two limits that are usually established separately, by a geometric squeeze-theorem argument (bounding \(\sin h\) between the areas of two circular sectors): \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h}=1\) and \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\) (with the companion fact \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h}=0\) following from it). When It Doesn't Exist

continuity is necessary but not sufficient — a taxonomy of the ways smoothness can fail

Continuity is necessary, not sufficient

Panel II proved one direction cleanly: differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous. The converse is false, and \(f(x)=|x|\) at \(x=0\) is the standard witness — continuous with no gap or jump, yet its two one-sided difference quotients disagree outright:

Since \(1\ne-1\), the two-sided limit does not exist, and neither does \(f'(0)\). Geometrically, the graph has a sharp corner: the "tangent line" would have to be simultaneously a line of slope \(1\) and a line of slope \(-1\), which is not a single line at all.

A taxonomy of failure

The corner in \(|x|\) is only the mildest of several distinct ways a curve can refuse to be locally linear at a point.

Corner (\(f(x)=|x|\) at \(x=0\)): both one-sided derivatives exist and are finite, but they disagree — two straight tangent directions meet at an angle, exactly as computed above.

Cusp (\(f(x)=x^{2/3}\) at \(x=0\)): the difference quotient is \(\dfrac{h^{2/3}}{h}=h^{-1/3}\). As \(h\to0^+\), this diverges to \(+\infty\); as \(h\to0^-\), it diverges to \(-\infty\). Both one-sided "slopes" blow up, but with opposite sign — the graph comes to a sharp point that would need a vertical tangent pointing in two opposite directions at once.

Vertical tangent (\(f(x)=x^{1/3}\) at \(x=0\)): the difference quotient is \(\dfrac{h^{1/3}}{h}=h^{-2/3}\), which diverges to \(+\infty\) from both sides, since the even-looking exponent \(-2/3\) is positive regardless of the sign of \(h\). The curve is perfectly smooth and monotonic through the origin — there is no corner or cusp to see — but the tangent line is vertical, and a vertical line has no finite slope for \(f'(0)\) to equal.

Jump discontinuity (a step function, \(f(x)=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(f(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\)): this one fails before a single difference quotient is even computed. Since \(f\) is not continuous at \(0\), Panel II's theorem — read in its contrapositive form, not continuous \(\Rightarrow\) not differentiable — rules out a derivative there immediately.

Warning — the monster of calculus

In 1872, Karl Weierstrass shocked the mathematical world with a function continuous at every real number and differentiable at none of them: \(W(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n\cos(b^n\pi x)\), for a constant \(0\lt a\lt1\) and an odd integer \(b\) satisfying \(ab\gt1+\frac{3\pi}{2}\). Zoom in anywhere on its graph and, instead of flattening into a line the way Panel III promises for well-behaved functions, it keeps producing fresh, sharper wiggles at every scale, forever — a corner hiding inside every corner.

Note — the exception, not the rule

It gets stranger still: Weierstrass's function is not a rare pathology cooked up to break the theory. Stefan Banach and Stefan Mazurkiewicz showed in 1931 that, in a precise topological sense (Baire category), a "generic" continuous function is nowhere differentiable — smooth, tangent-line-friendly curves like the ones filling this essay are the thin exception among continuous functions, not the typical case.

Where This Leads

higher derivatives, Taylor's approximation, and the two directions calculus grows from here

Higher-order derivatives and concavity

Nothing stops \(f'\) itself from being differentiated again. The second derivative \(f''(x)\) measures the rate of change of the rate of change — in the physics row of Panel III's table, if \(s(t)\) is position and \(s'(t)\) velocity, then \(s''(t)\) is acceleration, the feeling of being pushed back into your seat. Geometrically, \(f''(x)>0\) means the curve bends upward like a cup (concave up), \(f''(x)<0\) means it bends downward like a cap (concave down), and points where concavity flips sign are inflection points.

Taylor's theorem: local linearity, refined

Panel III's linear approximation \(f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h\) is only the first term of a longer, increasingly accurate expansion. Adding the next term brings the second derivative into play:

Subtract \(f(a)\) from both sides and divide by \(h\), and the difference quotient itself falls out with an explicit error term attached: \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \approx f'(a) + \dfrac{h}{2}f''(a)\). That is exactly the first-order convergence behavior Panel VI's Lab 2 displayed numerically — the error is proportional to \(h\), scaled by how sharply the curve is bending, i.e. by \(f''(a)\). Local linearity was never the end of the story, only its opening move; each additional derivative buys one more order of accuracy.

Two directions calculus grows from here

This essay has stayed almost entirely in one dimension — one input, one output, one slope. Two natural questions push past that boundary, and both already have answers elsewhere.

The first: what if the input or output has more than one component? A map \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is locally linear in exactly the sense of Panel III, except the single number \(f'(a)\) is replaced by a matrix that records every partial rate of change at once. That matrix, and everything it makes possible — area scaling, invertibility, stability — is the entire subject of the companion Jacobian essay.

The second: what if, instead of a rate of change, you want to recover the total accumulated quantity from a known rate — running the process in reverse? Given \(f'\), can you reconstruct \(f\)? That question is the seed of the integral, approached through Riemann sums in exactly the same spirit this essay approached the derivative through secant lines: an average quantity, made exact by a shrinking limit.

A smaller, immediate payoff belongs here too, closing the loop back to Panel I's opening obstruction. Certain limits — \(\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) where both \(f(a)=g(a)=0\) — hit the exact same \(\frac{0}{0}\) wall that motivated this entire essay. L'Hôpital's rule says that, under mild conditions, \(\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\): the derivative, built specifically to tame one \(\frac{0}{0}\) indeterminate form, turns out to tame every other one it meets along the way.

Insight — one line to carry away

Every idea in this essay is one continuous thread: a rate with no interval to measure it over, rescued by watching what a perfectly ordinary secant slope approaches as its interval shrinks to nothing. The limit definition, local linearity, every differentiation rule, and every failure mode in Panel V are all restatements of that single rescue, examined from different angles.
