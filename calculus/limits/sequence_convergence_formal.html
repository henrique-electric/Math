SEQUENCE CONVERGENCE

Sequences and the ε–N Definition

why "gets closer and closer" needed a rule at all, and what that rule turns out to say

A sequence is just a function on ℕ

Strip away the dots-and-arrow notation \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\) and a sequence is nothing more exotic than a function \(a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}\), with \(a(n)\) conventionally written \(a_n\). That reframing matters: "does \(a_n\) converge" is not a new species of question requiring new machinery — it is the single most important special case of "what does a function do as its input grows," restricted to a function whose domain happens to be the natural numbers instead of an interval of reals.

Definition — sequence

A sequence of real numbers is a function \(a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}\). Its values are written \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\), and the whole sequence is denoted \((a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}\), or just \((a_n)\).

Why "gets closer and closer" isn't good enough on its own

What actually needs defining is the word "converges." Informally: \(a_n\to L\) means the terms get closer and closer to \(L\) as \(n\) grows. That sentence is evocative and completely useless for proving anything — "closer and closer" has no numerical content, and a sequence can wiggle, overshoot, and correct itself indefinitely while still deserving to be called convergent. (The historical struggle to get this exactly right — from Newton's and Leibniz's originally shaky infinitesimals through Cauchy and Weierstrass's 19th-century fix — is its own story, told in full in the philosophy of calculus essay elsewhere on this site; this panel only needs the destination.)

The ε–N definition

Definition — convergence of a sequence (Weierstrass-style)

The sequence \((a_n)\) converges to \(L\) if: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists a natural number \(N_0\) such that \(n\ge N_0\) implies \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\). We write \(a_n\to L\), or \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = L\). If no such \(L\) exists, the sequence diverges.

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N_0\in\mathbb{N} : n\ge N_0 \implies |a_n-L|<\varepsilon \]

Parse the quantifiers in order. \(\forall\varepsilon>0\) — the statement must hold for every positive tolerance, without exception: for \(\varepsilon=1\), for \(\varepsilon=10^{-9}\), for every other positive real. \(\exists N_0\) — a single index answers the challenge; \(N_0\) is allowed, and expected, to depend on \(\varepsilon\), typically growing without bound as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks toward 0. The implication must hold for every later index, not just one; a single term escaping the band, however far out, breaks it for that \(N_0\). This is precisely the ε–δ definition of a function limit — worked in full in the companion Formal Limit essay — specialized to a function whose domain is \(\mathbb{N}\) and whose "input approaches \(\infty\)" replaces "input approaches \(c\)."

Worked example — proving a limit rigorously

Claim: \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+1} = 1\).

  1. Let ε > 0 be given.challenger moves first
  2. Choose N₀ = ⌈1/ε⌉.defender's response
  3. Suppose n ≥ N₀.assume the challenger's window is respected
  4. |n/(n+1) − 1| = |(n−(n+1))/(n+1)| = 1/(n+1)algebra
  5. n ≥ N₀ = ⌈1/ε⌉ ⟹ n+1 > 1/ε ⟹ 1/(n+1) < εsince n ≥ 1/ε, by assumption
  6. ∴ |n/(n+1) − 1| < εchained together

Because a valid \(N_0\) was produced for an arbitrary \(\varepsilon\), the defender wins the game no matter what the challenger picks, and the limit is proven rigorously — no picture, no appeal to "gets closer and closer," just two finite numbers and an inequality. Panel VI unpacks exactly this challenge–response picture in far more depth, and the companion Formal Convergence of Series essay is this entire definition applied to one specific kind of sequence — the partial-sum sequence \(S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n\).

Uniqueness, Boundedness, and the Algebra of Limits

the three cheapest theorems in the subject, and why each one is nearly free once the definition is in hand

Uniqueness of the limit

The definition never asserted that \(L\) is the only number \(a_n\) could converge to — that has to be proved, and the proof is short enough to state in full, because it is the cleanest illustration of the ε/2-splitting technique that recurs throughout this subject.

  1. Suppose aₙ → L and aₙ → L′ with L ≠ L′.for contradiction
  2. Let ε = |L−L′|/2 > 0.a fixed positive gap, halved
  3. ∃N₁ : n≥N₁ ⟹ |aₙ−L| < ε, and ∃N₂ : n≥N₂ ⟹ |aₙ−L′| < ε.definition, applied twice
  4. Let N = max(N₁,N₂). For n≥N: |L−L′| ≤ |L−aₙ|+|aₙ−L′| < ε+ε = |L−L′|.triangle inequality
  5. |L−L′| < |L−L′| is a contradiction.a number is never less than itself
  6. ∴ L = L′. Limits, when they exist, are unique.contradiction resolved

Convergent implies bounded

Theorem

If \((a_n)\) converges, then \((a_n)\) is bounded: there exists \(M\) such that \(|a_n|\le M\) for every \(n\).

Take \(\varepsilon=1\) in the definition: some \(N_0\) guarantees \(|a_n-L|<1\), hence \(|a_n|<|L|+1\), for every \(n\ge N_0\). That handles the entire infinite tail in one stroke. What remains — \(a_1,\dots,a_{N_0-1}\) — is a finite list, and any finite list of real numbers trivially has a largest absolute value. Take \(M\) to be the larger of that finite maximum and \(|L|+1\), and every term, early or late, satisfies \(|a_n|\le M\).

Warning — the converse is false

\((-1)^n\) is bounded and never converges. Boundedness is a consequence of convergence, never a substitute for it — Panel IV returns to exactly this example to show what boundedness alone still buys you.

The algebra of limits

If \(a_n\to A\) and \(b_n\to B\), each of the following holds, and each is provable directly from the ε–N definition:

Limit laws for sequences (assuming both individual limits exist)
LawStatement
Sumaₙ + bₙ → A + B
Productaₙ · bₙ → A · B
Constant multiplec · aₙ → c · A
Quotientaₙ / bₙ → A / B  (provided B ≠ 0)

The sum rule is the cleanest to see in full, since it is exactly the same ε/2-splitting move used for uniqueness above.

Setup — splitting the error budget

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), apply the definition to \((a_n)\) with tolerance \(\varepsilon/2\), getting \(N_1\), and to \((b_n)\) with tolerance \(\varepsilon/2\), getting \(N_2\). Let \(N_0=\max(N_1,N_2)\).

Payoff — the triangle inequality closes it

For \(n\ge N_0\): \(|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|\le|a_n-A|+|b_n-B|<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon\). Two half-sized errors combine into one full-sized, still-arbitrary \(\varepsilon\).

The squeeze (sandwich) theorem

If \(a_n\le b_n\le c_n\) for all \(n\) past some point, and \(a_n\to L\) and \(c_n\to L\), then \(b_n\to L\) as well. This is often the only practical route to a limit when \(b_n\) itself has no clean closed form — \(\sin(n)/n\), for instance, is trapped between \(-1/n\) and \(1/n\), both of which converge to 0.

A divergence disproof: (−1)ⁿ has no limit

Rather than test every candidate \(L\) individually, disprove convergence to any \(L\) at once: for the fixed choice \(\varepsilon=1\), no \(L\) and no \(N_0\) can possibly work.

  1. Fix any candidate L and take ε = 1.covers every candidate at once
  2. For any N₀, take n ≥ N₀ odd, so aₙ = −1 and aₙ₊₁ = 1.consecutive terms, past any proposed N₀
  3. 2 = |aₙ − aₙ₊₁| ≤ |aₙ − L| + |L − aₙ₊₁|triangle inequality
  4. If both terms on the right were < 1, their sum would be < 2 — contradicting the left side.arithmetic
  5. ∴ no N₀ forces both consecutive terms inside the band, for any L.no candidate survives

No real number \(L\) satisfies the ε–N definition for \((-1)^n\). The sequence diverges.

The Monotone Convergence Theorem

the theorem where completeness of ℝ first does visible, load-bearing work

Bounded and climbing

Restrict to an increasing sequence: \(a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le\cdots\). Picture it as a staircase that can only climb, never step back down. If that staircase never climbs past some fixed ceiling, intuition says it has to be "settling in" against the highest step it can actually reach.

a₁ a₂ a₃ a₄ a₅ a₆ a₇ ... L = sup{aₙ} |-----|------|--------|----------|---|--|------------------| climbing, never stepping back down ceiling, never crossed Choose ε. L−ε is not an upper bound (L is the LEAST one) → some a_N₀ > L−ε Past N₀, monotonicity keeps every later term ≥ a_N₀ > L−ε, and every term ≤ L. So every aₙ, n≥N₀, is squeezed into (L−ε, L] ⊂ (L−ε, L+ε).
Theorem — monotone convergence

Every bounded, monotonic sequence of real numbers converges — to its supremum, if increasing, or its infimum, if decreasing.

Proving it from completeness

Claim: if \((a_n)\) is increasing and bounded above, then \(a_n\to L\) where \(L=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\).

  1. L exists because {aₙ} is non-empty and bounded above.least-upper-bound property
  2. Let ε > 0. Since L is the LEAST upper bound, L−ε is not an upper bound.definition of supremum
  3. So some term a_N₀ satisfies a_N₀ > L−ε.L−ε failing to bound the set
  4. For n ≥ N₀: aₙ ≥ a_N₀ > L−ε (monotonicity), and aₙ ≤ L < L+ε (L is an upper bound).combine both bounds
  5. ∴ L−ε < aₙ ≤ L < L+ε, i.e. |aₙ−L| < ε, for all n ≥ N₀.definition of convergence, satisfied
Insight — where completeness earned its keep

The entire proof hinges on one line — "\(L\) exists because the set is bounded above." Nothing else is more than algebra and the definition of supremum. That single existence claim is the least-upper-bound property, and it is the only place in the whole argument where \(\mathbb{R}\)'s completeness, rather than \(\mathbb{Q}\)'s ordering, is actually used. See the companion Continuous Mathematics Foundations essay for where that property itself comes from.

An example where the closed form isn't the point

\(\displaystyle\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n}\to e\): the binomial expansion shows the sequence is increasing, and is term-by-term dominated by \(\sum 1/k!\), itself bounded above by 3. By the theorem, the sequence converges — with the limit defined to be \(e\). Existence is proved first; the value \(e\approx2.71828\) is identified only afterward.

Subsequences and the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem

what boundedness alone still guarantees, even when a sequence refuses to settle down

Subsequences

Definition — subsequence

Given \((a_n)\) and a strictly increasing sequence of indices \(n_1subsequence of \((a_n)\).

Every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit — deleting terms cannot introduce new behavior that was not already forced by the original tail condition. The converse question is more interesting: must a sequence that never settles down still contain a well-behaved piece inside it?

Theorem — Bolzano–Weierstrass

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

Lion hunting by bisection

The classical proof strategy has a nickname worth keeping: lion hunting. A bounded sequence lives entirely inside some interval \([-M,M]\). Split it in half. Since the sequence has infinitely many terms and only two halves to distribute them into, at least one half must still contain infinitely many terms — the infinite pigeonhole principle. Keep that half, split it again, and repeat forever.

Start: [ -M ................................... M ] infinitely many terms live here Split: [ -M ............ 0 ][ 0 ............... M ] ↑ keep whichever half still holds infinitely many terms ↑ Split again: [ .. | .. ] ↑ repeat forever, halving the width each time ↑ Nested intervals, width → 0, each still holding infinitely many terms of the sequence. Their intersection is a single point c — pick one term per interval → a subsequence → c.
Insight — where completeness reappears, quietly

This bisection produces a nested, shrinking family of closed intervals with widths halving toward 0. The nested interval property — that such a family always has exactly one point in its intersection — is itself a direct consequence of the least-upper-bound property. Boundedness makes escape to infinity impossible; completeness guarantees the piling-up point is an actual real number rather than a gap in the line.

Warning — bounded and divergent still isn't empty-handed

\((-1)^n\) is bounded, so Bolzano–Weierstrass guarantees a convergent subsequence — and indeed the even-indexed terms alone converge to 1, the odd-indexed terms alone to \(-1\). Divergence of the whole sequence and convergence of a piece of it are entirely compatible facts. Note precisely what the theorem does and does not claim: it never says the sequence itself converges.

Cauchy Sequences and Completeness

certifying a limit exists without ever naming it — and why that trick only works over ℝ

A criterion with no candidate required

The ε–N definition has a real practical limitation: to even write it down, a candidate value \(L\) must already be in hand. For sequences defined recursively, or arising as approximations with no closed form, no such candidate is obvious in advance. Cauchy's criterion removes the requirement entirely, by asking a purely internal question: do the terms eventually stop moving relative to each other?

Definition — Cauchy sequence

\((a_n)\) is a Cauchy sequence if: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(N_0\) such that for all \(m,n\ge N_0\), \(|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon\).

Theorem — Cauchy's criterion for sequences

A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence.

Convergent \(\implies\) Cauchy is an easy ε/2-split, identical in spirit to the algebra-of-limits proof in Panel II. Cauchy \(\implies\) convergent is the deep direction, and it is exactly equivalent to the statement that \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete.

Why the hard direction needs completeness

Warning — internal consistency alone isn't enough, over ℚ

The decimal truncations of \(\sqrt2\) — \(1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\ 1.4142,\dots\) — form a sequence whose terms bunch closer and closer together: exactly the Cauchy condition. Yet considered strictly as a sequence of rational numbers required to converge to a rational limit, it converges to nothing: \(\sqrt2\) is irrational, so no rational target exists for it to land on. Being internally consistent (Cauchy) and actually converging come apart the instant the number system has gaps in it — \(\mathbb{R}\) is built (Dedekind cuts, or classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals, detailed in the companion Continuous Mathematics Foundations essay) specifically so this failure cannot happen.

The proof chain, unpacked

The proof that a Cauchy sequence of reals converges is not one idea but three, chained together — and no new axiom is needed at the last step, only the triangle inequality doing the same error-budget-splitting trick seen throughout this essay.

  1. Cauchy ⟹ bounded — past some N₀, later terms sit within 1 of a_N₀; the finitely many earlier terms are automatically bounded.link 1
  2. Bounded ⟹ a convergent subsequence exists, say aₙₖ → L, by Bolzano–Weierstrass.link 2 · Panel IV
  3. Cauchy + one convergent piece ⟹ the whole sequence converges to L: aₙ is close to some aₙₖ (Cauchy), which is close to L (subsequence).link 3 · triangle inequality

The reason this chain is worth feeling, not just reading, is that it shows completeness manifesting in two different guises — first as "bounded sets have suprema" (inside Bolzano–Weierstrass) and then as "Cauchy sequences have somewhere to land" (the conclusion itself) — and shows those two guises are, underneath, the same fact wearing different clothes.

Deep Intuition — Why, Not Just What

the picture underneath every ε and every N₀, and why completeness is the one fact doing all the work

The challenge–response game

The single clearest way to feel the ε–N definition is as a game between an adversarial Challenger and a Defender. The Challenger's move is \(\varepsilon\): a tolerance band, \((L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)\), around the claimed limit \(L\) — a demand that says, "make the sequence land inside this band." The Defender's move is \(N_0\): an index past which every single term, with no exceptions, is guaranteed to sit inside that band.

Insight — the definition as a game

\(a_n\to L\) means: the Defender always wins. No matter how small the Challenger makes \(\varepsilon\), the Defender can always name an \(N_0\) that works. If there is even one \(\varepsilon\) for which no \(N_0\) works — however small the escapes, however far out they occur — the sequence does not converge to \(L\).

Why the Challenger moves first

The order — \(\varepsilon\) first, \(N_0\) second — is not stylistic. Reversing it would demand a single \(N_0\) that works for every \(\varepsilon\) simultaneously, meaning the sequence would have to stop moving entirely past \(N_0\), not merely settle down arbitrarily close. That is a wildly stronger claim, true only of eventually-constant sequences. The actual definition asks only that the wiggle room shrink toward zero as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks, never that it vanish outright — and letting the Challenger fix \(\varepsilon\) first is exactly what encodes that weaker, correct demand.

The neighborhood picture

Reframe the same definition topologically: for every open interval around \(L\), however small, all but finitely many terms of the sequence lie inside it. The finitely-many-exceptions phrasing makes something vivid that the symbols can obscure: convergence places zero constraint on how the sequence behaves early on. The first million terms can do anything at all, and none of it matters, because convergence is entirely a statement about tails, never about beginnings.

Insight — a useful reframing

"\(a_n\to L\)" and "every neighborhood of \(L\) contains all but finitely many terms of the sequence" are logically identical statements. \(N_0\) is precisely the boundary past which the finite exception list is closed and the terms are permanently trapped.

Why uniqueness and boundedness are forced

Picture two candidate limits \(L\) and \(L'\) as the centers of two tolerance bands shrunk down to radius \(|L-L'|/2\) — small enough that the bands do not touch. A convergent sequence must eventually live entirely inside one band, and if it also converged to the other candidate, some term would have to sit in two disjoint intervals simultaneously — a plain impossibility for a single real number. Uniqueness is not an extra axiom bolted onto the definition; it falls straight out of the fact that a point cannot occupy two places that do not overlap.

Boundedness is forced the same way: a convergent tail is trapped inside a band of fixed width forever, so escaping to infinity is only ever available to the finitely many terms that come before the trap closes — and a finite collection of real numbers can never be unbounded, no matter how wild its individual values are.

The staircase picture, once more

Panel III proves monotone convergence formally; the intuition underneath is worth isolating on its own. "Settling in against the highest reachable step" is exactly the definition of a supremum — so the staircase picture only becomes a theorem once a bounded set of reals is guaranteed to have a supremum that is itself a real number.

Warning — why this can fail over ℚ, approximating √2

Define \(a_1=1\) and \(a_{n+1}=(a_n+2/a_n)/2\) — Newton's method chasing \(\sqrt2\). Truncated to rationals, this sequence is bounded above by 2, with every hallmark of the staircase picture present. If the "supremum" it settles against were required to be rational, there would be nothing there — \(\sqrt2\) is irrational, so no rational ceiling exists for the staircase to press up against. Over \(\mathbb{R}\) the hole is filled by construction, and the staircase always finds real ground to stand on.

Cauchy sequences — consistency without a destination

The ε–N definition asks "does the sequence approach a specific place." The Cauchy condition asks something quieter: "do the terms eventually stop moving relative to each other," with no candidate limit named or needed. The genuinely deep fact is that, over the real numbers, those two questions turn out to have the exact same answer — and that equivalence is not free, it is precisely what completeness buys, as the decimal-truncations-of-\(\sqrt2\) example in Panel V shows failing the instant the number system has gaps in it.

One line to carry away

Every idea across this essay is downstream of a single fact: \(\mathbb{R}\) has no missing points. The ε–N definition turns "approaches" into two finite numbers and a checkable relationship; uniqueness and boundedness fall out of that relationship for free; monotone convergence turns "bounded and climbing" into "convergent" because a ceiling always has real ground beneath it to settle on; Bolzano–Weierstrass turns "merely bounded" into "a convergent piece exists" because infinitely many points in finite space must pile up somewhere real; and Cauchy's criterion turns "internally consistent" into "actually convergent" for the identical reason. Sequence convergence, from top to bottom, is completeness of the real line doing quiet, load-bearing work.

Bounded, divergent, yet not empty-handed

sin(n) — bounded, no limit, but Bolzano–Weierstrass still applies

\(-1\le\sin(n)\le1\) trivially, so the sequence is bounded. Since \(\pi\) is irrational, \(\{n\bmod 2\pi\}\) is dense in \([0,2\pi)\), so \(\sin(n)\) returns arbitrarily close to every value in \([-1,1]\) infinitely often — ruling out any single limit. Yet boundedness alone still guarantees convergent subsequences by Bolzano–Weierstrass; density in fact guarantees convergent subsequences toward every point of \([-1,1]\). A sequence can be maximally far from convergent and still be full of convergent pieces.

The Short Version

a compact reference table, a glossary, and where to go for more depth

The one-paragraph summary

A sequence is just a function on \(\mathbb{N}\), and "converges" is made precise by the ε–N definition (Panel I): a challenge–response game the Defender must always be able to win. Uniqueness and boundedness fall out of that game for free (Panel II). Monotone convergence (Panel III) and Bolzano–Weierstrass (Panel IV) turn "bounded" into "convergent" — the first outright, the second at least for some subsequence — because \(\mathbb{R}\)'s completeness guarantees a bounded set always has real ground to settle on. Cauchy's criterion (Panel V) pushes this further, certifying convergence with no candidate limit named at all. Every one of these results, unpacked in Panel VI, is completeness of the real line doing the same quiet work in a different costume.
